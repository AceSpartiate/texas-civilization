## The one-line answer

**On Tuesday September 29 about a hundred Mexican dragoons under Lieutenant Francisco de Castañeda reached the west
bank of a swollen Guadalupe opposite Gonzales and found eighteen men under Captain Albert Martin behind a wooden
breastwork on the east bank, with the ferry flatboat and the canoes on their side (late accounts say the flatboat was
hidden in a slough above the town). The alcalde, Andrew Ponton, was said to be away. A man from Castañeda's side swam the
dispatches over; the town stalled; on the afternoon of September 30 the regidor Joseph D. Clements, with two others
and "a Mr. Smith", read Castañeda a letter from the far bank refusing to deliver the cannon. Couriers had been going
out since September 25; Coleman's thirty from Bastrop and Moore's forty or fifty from the Colorado came in on the
night of the 29th, and by October 1 there were "about 150". They elected John H. Moore colonel and J. W. E. Wallace
lieutenant colonel. The cannon, which by several late accounts had been buried in George W. Davis's peach orchard, was
dug up, mounted on a pair of cart or wagon wheels and given slugs of cut iron. Some families had already been moving
out, or hid in the timbered river bottoms; Mrs. Sarah DeWitt, whose house stood across the river, came into town. On
the evening of Thursday October 1 some 160 men crossed at the ferry with the cannon, gathered at Mrs. DeWitt's house,
heard an address by the Rev. W. P. Smith, and marched upriver in fog. They were back in Gonzales about two in the
afternoon of October 2.** No contemporary document of 1835 that was read mentions a flag, and none has anyone say
"come and take it". **The flag is documented only from memoirs of 1874–1900, two of which date it after the fight;
who made it is unknown, and the wedding dress is a tradition the Handbook itself calls apocryphal.**

## 2. Summary

- **Before (Sept 20s).** A corporal (Casimiro de León) and a few soldiers bring Ugartechea's demand; the townspeople
  meet and refuse ("but three names in favour of giving up the cannon", Bailey); the soldiers are held; Ponton writes
  on Sept 26 that the cannon was "given in perpetuity to this Town"; Davis writes for help on Sept 25; families talk of
  moving and some load wagons (Bailey, Sept 26). By several late accounts the cannon is buried in Davis's peach orchard.
- **Sept 29 (Tue).** Castañeda arrives about noon; eighteen men hold the east bank behind a wooden breastwork with the
  boats on their side; Juan de los Santos Coy swims the letters over at 4 p.m.; a regidor answers that the alcalde is
  away. Castañeda camps on the mound at DeWitt's, about 300 yards from the ford. Coleman (30) and Moore (40–50) ride
  in that night.
- **Sept 30 (Wed).** Talk across the river in the morning; at 4 p.m. Clements, two others and "a Mr. Smith" read the
  refusal from the east bank. The appeal to San Felipe and the Lavaca: "Yesterday we were but 18 strong, to day 150".
  Coleman: "we have as yet no head". Feints at the ford and the ferry (Macomb).
- **Oct 1 (Thu).** Castañeda leaves about 10 a.m. for the Williams place, seven miles up. Moore writes that he has
  "about 150 men". Moore and Wallace elected (the day is disputed, §10). The cannon is mounted and loaded. About 7–8
  p.m. the force crosses at the ferry with the cannon and fifty horses, gathers at Mrs. DeWitt's house, hears Smith's
  address about 11 p.m. to 1 a.m., and marches.
- **Oct 2 (Fri).** The fight (another file). The force is back in Gonzales **about 2 p.m.** with the Mexican baggage.

| Days before | The soldiers who brought the first demand are held. | When | What the town did | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Morning | Castañeda comes to the pass with an English-speaking soldier; the guard says the regidor will come; the regidor says "he had sent for the Alcalde because he couldn't decide upon the cannon", and agrees to talk at 4 p.m. if the alcalde has not come, "only on the condition that his personal safety would be guaranteed". | Castañeda Sept 30 and Oct 4 |

| During the day | Coleman to the alcalde of San Felipe: "we have as yet no head there will be one chosen to day"; "I am for attacking those that are now heare we are divided on the subject"; "we are all captains and have our views". | Coleman Sept 30 |

| Evening | Castañeda writes at 7 p.m. that he cannot carry out his orders "because I was hindered at the river", and that a Coushatta who had been "many days with them" put the Texian force at 140 with 300 more expected. | Castañeda Sept 30 |

**Who was "Mr. Smith"?** Not identified in any source read. It is not Launcelot Smither, who reached Castañeda from
Béxar only on October 1. It may have been an interpreter. Do not name him.

**Ugartechea's version, to Austin, October 4**, from Castañeda's reports: the alcalde "caused the inhabitants residing

withdrew to the other side the ferry in canoes"; "the next Regidor... had to consult with the Political Chief of the
Brazos and without his sanction he could not, would not deliver up the cannon."

"one hundred and sixty-eight men". Castañeda heard from the Coushatta that "two hundred men" left to attack him.

## 6. October 2 (Friday), the town's side only

- **Who stayed in town** is **not recorded** in any source read. Taylor's horse guard "in town" is the only mention of
  anyone left behind, and he is late and edited.
- **The return.** Macomb: the force "collected whatever baggage, &c. was left behind by the Mexicans, and marched in
  good order and high spirits, without losing a man, to Gonzales, where they arrived about two o'clock P.M.
  yesterday." Rusk: "the same day they marched to Gonales". W. T. Austin: "our troops were counter-marched to the town
  of Gonzales, where they were encamped". Dewees: "We then re-crossed the river to Gonzales." **The return on the
  afternoon of October 2 is STRONGLY SUPPORTED**; Macomb's "yesterday" cannot fix it alone because the printed letter
  is undated (SODC heads it October 5, which would make "yesterday" October 4 — against every other account).
- **What came back.** Baggage and "some few horses" (Macomb); Valentine Bennet's share of the camp spoils was "an
  excellent Spanish blanket" (Bennet p. 316). Castañeda lost the baggage in the cornfield at the Williams place.
- **Word sent out.** Fisher, chairman of the committee, writes to Austin on October 3: "The rubicon is crossed". Austin
  at San Felipe has the news by October 5, garbled ("forty killed"). A family at Gonzales would know the result by
  mid-afternoon on the 2nd.
- **After.** From October 4 volunteers keep arriving for a week (Taylor); the grist mill is repaired and meal ground
  (Macomb); Austin takes command on October 11 (TSHA Gonzales, TX). That is the muster, not this file.

### 7.2 What can and cannot be said

- **The design** — a white field, a black cannon, "Come and take it" — is **STRONGLY SUPPORTED** (Smithwick and Taylor,
  independently, both late memoirs; the Handbook follows them). **The lone star is DISPUTED**: Smithwick and Taylor have
  it; Mason as the Handbook quotes him does not; the Flags entry says "with or without a lone star". **Whether the
  words were above, below or both is DISPUTED.** Every drawing of the flag today is a reconstruction; Wikipedia calls the
  replicas "conjectural".
- **The material.** "White cotton cloth" (Smithwick) is the only material a man who saw it gives. **Silk from a wedding
  dress is TRADITION** and contradicts him.
- **The makers are unknown.** Smithwick, who was there when it was made, says so. The Handbook's own entries name four
  different makers across 1995–2025 (Burns and Evaline DeWitt; Zumwalt and Eveline DeWitt; Sarah and Eveline DeWitt; a
  committee of five officers). **Who first recorded the DeWitt women and the wedding dress, and when, was NOT FOUND.**
  Cox's *Romantic Flags of Texas* (1936) is a candidate that was not read.
- **Naomi DeWitt** married William Matthews in August 1835 (TSHA Sarah DeWitt). That the marriage happened is the only
  part of the dress story with a source behind it.
- **Before or after the fight: DISPUTED.** Taylor dates the flag October 10, Smithwick makes it for Austin's army; the
  Handbook cannon entry and Hardin put it on the field on October 2. No 1835 document settles it.
- **The Rumor Mill is the right home for the dress story**, told as what people later said.

## 8. The Old Eighteen and the boats

**The eighteen are DOCUMENTED as a number**: "eighteen men (the whole force then in this place,)" (Fisher, Oct 3);
"only eighteen men, commanded by captain Albert Martin" (Macomb); "Yesterday we were but 18 strong" (Martin, Coleman
and Moore, Sept 30). Castañeda counted "about 20 men" guarding the boats.

**The names are STRONGLY SUPPORTED**, resting on Bennet (1899), who had them from survivors, and followed by the
Handbook. Bennet's order: Capt. Albert Martin, Jacob C. Darst, Winslow Turner, W. W. Arrington, Graves Fulchear,
George W. Davis, John Sowell, James Hinds, Thomas Miller, Valentine Bennet, Ezekiel Williams, Simeon Bateman,
J. D. Clements, Almerion [Almeron] Dickinson, Benjamin Fuqua, Thomas Jackson, Charles Mason, Almon Cottle. The Handbook
(Hardin) lists the same eighteen.

- **Who else was about the river those days**: Jesse McCoy, Joseph Kent, Graves Fulchear and W. W. Arrington "kept watch
  at the river" (Bennet); Mason names McCoy, Fulcher and Littleton Tomlinson as spies. McCoy and Kent are **not** in
  the eighteen.
- **Officers**: Mason says the company elected Albert C. Martin captain and Arrington, Mason and Jesse McCoy lieutenants,
  "with about one hundred non-commissioned officers and privates, from sixty down to fifteen years of age" — that
  hundred is **before** the troops arrived and contradicts the eighteen; treat it as Mason's company of the colony, not
  the men in town on September 29.
- **Five of the eighteen are on Bennet's own list of Gonzales men killed at the Alamo** (Martin, Darst, Dickinson,
  Jackson, Miller; computed by comparing his two lists). His "Galby Fuqua" is not the eighteen's Benjamin Fuqua. Not
  needed for this file, but a family may know them.
- **The boats**: "a flatboat and canoes" on the east bank under guard (Castañeda, **DOCUMENTED**); the ferry-boat
  hidden in a slough or bayou above the town (Mason, Rusk, Bennet; **STRONGLY SUPPORTED**); "withdrew to the other side
  the ferry in canoes" (Ugartechea, DOCUMENTED as the Mexican understanding); **the flatboat back at its landing and the
  infantry and cannon crossing on it on October 1 (Macomb, DOCUMENTED)**. In November Smither was ordered to repair "the
  flat" at Gonzales and "attend to the boat, day and night for about Thirty Five days" (Smither, Nov 23). **This
  corrects `HIST-TEX-141`**, whose note says the ferry "is not drawn, the record having it gone before the story
  opens": the ferry was hidden, not gone, and it carried the force across on the night of October 1.
- **The breastwork**: "a temporary breastwork... just below the ferry" (Mason), "their parapet made up of wood"
  (Castañeda). **DOCUMENTED** that there was one; its size and shape are not recorded.

## 11. Families, women and children

**What is recorded:**

- **Sept 26, contemporary**: "Several families were talking of moveing. and some have actually prepared thier waggons
  to as so. They wish to move thier families this side of the Colorado and to return themselves to the defence of their
  frontier." Also that the people feared "foraging parties" on their provisions and stock (Bailey, who left Gonzales
  "Wednesday last", i.e. about Sept 23). **DOCUMENTED.**
- **Sept 29, hearsay at Moore's**: "The inhabitants of Gonzales are moving their families from the Town." (Saul).
  **DOCUMENTED as a report**, not as a count.
- **Mexican side**: the alcalde "caused the inhabitants residing on this side of the river [the west bank] to leave
  their houses, alarmed the Citizens" (Ugartechea to Austin, Oct 4). DOCUMENTED as the Mexican understanding.
- **Hiding in the bottoms**: "Some of the families secreted themselves in the timbered bottoms." (Bennet, 1899).
  Rusk: "the few Citizins who remained in Town (The rest were a moving their Families to tile swamp & other safe
  places)". **STRONGLY SUPPORTED.**
- **Mrs. Sarah DeWitt**, the empresario's widow, lived across the river from the town ("the residence of Mrs. Sarah
  DeWitt [on the Sara Seeley tract across from inner Gonzales town]", SODC's bracketed gloss on Mason; Rusk puts
  Castañeda's mound "in the Center of Mrs DeWitts League").
  "Mrs. DeWitt, who with her family had removed to Gonzales at the request of the returning spies" (Mason, 1874); her
  empty house was the rendezvous on the night of October 1 (Mason; Rusk). **STRONGLY SUPPORTED.** The soldiers who first
  came for the cannon had camped "near the residence of Mrs. Sarah DeWitt" (Mason).
- **Provisions**: the town offered them in writing on Sept 25 ("we have plenty at your service"). Taylor adds that
  "Corn was plentiful, the gardens were full of fresh vegetables" and the citizens "freely offered" them — during the
  "nearly two weeks" stay, i.e. mostly after October 2.
- **Women bringing iron**: "Women brought in their flatirons, pots, and skillets. One lady removed the spindle of her
  spinning wheel" (Taylor) — **after the fight**, at the muster, and a single late source: **TRADITION** for October.
- **The castañeda camp at the Williams place**: Rusk says Castañeda was "killing the hogs & cattle of Williams";
  Bennet that they "robbed" the place of "many sacks of watermelons". The Williams household's whereabouts: **NOT
  FOUND**.

**NOT FOUND:** any named woman or child doing anything in the town between September 29 and October 2 other than
Sarah DeWitt's move; any woman at the river, at the reading of the letter, at the crossing or at the address; Susanna
Dickinson in these days (TSHA, as read for `grass-fight.md`, has her living in Gonzales in 1835, but no source read
here places her in an event of Sept 29 – Oct 2); any
count of the families who left, or where exactly they went; any civilian hurt.

## 13. Enslaved people and Tejano residents

- **Enslaved people at Gonzales, Sept 29 – Oct 2, 1835: NOT FOUND** in any source read. (Sarah DeWitt held five
  enslaved people in 1850, per the Handbook; nothing places anyone enslaved in these events.)
- **Tejano residents of Gonzales: NOT FOUND.** The Tejano named in these days, **Juan de los Santos Coy**, came with

  river; he was on the Mexican side.
- **Indians**: a Coushatta came into Castañeda's camp from the Texians (Castañeda, Sept 30 and Oct 4); Mason has "an
  Indian (Shawnee or Cherokee)" who swam the river at night as a spy for the town and vanished when help came; Bennet
  "a half friendly Indian who had been loitering about the town". These may be one man. **STRONGLY SUPPORTED that an
  Indian man went between the camps; his nation and allegiance are DISPUTED.**
- **Spanish speakers in town**: "one of the Gentlemen who had quite conversent with the Spanish Language" read the
  dispatch (Rusk); Clements's letter survives in Spanish as well as English. Castañeda had "a soldier who spoke
  English".

## 17. Questions for the owner

Each is multiple choice. The research's leaning, where it has one, is marked, but the choice is the owner's.

1. **When does the flag appear?**
   - (a) **Only from the October muster** (about Oct 10), as Smithwick and Taylor, both there, remember it. *(Closest to the eyewitnesses.)*
   - (b) **On October 2 over the cannon**, as the current Handbook and Hardin say, marked disputed.
   - (c) **Never as an object**; only named in text.

2. **The DeWitt women and the wedding dress.**
   - (a) **Only in the Rumor Mill**, as a story told later, with the Handbook's "apocryphal". *(Leaning.)*
   - (b) Not at all.
   - (c) As a scene, labelled tradition.

3. **"Come and take it" spoken.**
   - (a) **Not spoken on screen**; the documented refusal (Clements) is read across the river. *(Leaning.)*
   - (b) Spoken once, captioned "as Charles Mason remembered in 1874".
   - (c) Spoken freely.

4. **The ferry (`HIST-TEX-141`).**
   - (a) **Draw it hidden** in a slough above the town from Sept 29, and have the force cross on it on Oct 1. *(Documented.)*
   - (b) Keep it undrawn and say so in text.

   - (a) **Stay, load the wagon toward the Colorado, or hide in the river bottom** — the three things recorded. *(Leaning.)*

   - (c) Also help at the forge or the breastwork (men only, since no woman is recorded there).
