## 1. The plain answer to the owner's premise: it was the grass, not the snow

**The snow — NOT FOUND, and the record leans against it** (`HIST-TEX-612`). No Texian source read says anyone in Texas knew
of the Mexican army's snowstorm of 13–14 February, or gave winter weather as a reason Santa Anna would wait:

- Gray's diary, searched through: every "snow" in it is Virginia, or the "Snow River" (the Neches). Nothing of the Mexican
  army's weather.
- Sutherland's narrative: no "snow", no "grass".
- Travis's letters of 23 February – 3 March, and the excerpts of his letters of the 12th and 13th: no weather reasoning.
- Houston's writings, December 1835 – March 1836: no snow.

And the snow never reached Texas as the Texians knew it. The Alamo's *Myths and Legends* calls "one of the coldest winters in
Texas history" a myth: the storm "began on the evening of February 13" and "did not extend into Texas", and until the 25th it
had been "'shirt sleeve' weather". Gray, on 25 February: "so warm that I rode in my shirt sleeves. It was summer heat."
([snow-march-1836.md](snow-march-1836.md) §5 puts the snow in Coahuila, and lightly on the vanguard at La Espantosa — Coahuila in
1836.) The one modern text found that resembles the premise (forcesnews.com, uncited) says the Texians reasoned the enemy
"surely wouldn't attack in that" — and rests on the claim the Alamo calls a myth.

**The grass — DOCUMENTED as the Texians' own reckoning, STRONGLY SUPPORTED as Travis's belief** (`HIST-TEX-610`). Houston,
writing of his own army's campaign: "Say to our friends that, by the rise of grass, we will be on the march" (to James Powers,
28 December 1835); "By the first of March the campaign will open" (27 December); "By the first of March next, we must meet the
enemy" (12 December). An army of that day moved when its horses, mules and oxen could graze. The Handbook (Hardin): "Though
Travis did not believe that Santa Anna could reach Bexar until March 15, his arrival on February 23 convinced him otherwise."
Wikipedia (citing Hardin p. 121 and Chariton p. 159): Travis was "convinced that the Mexican army would not arrive in Bexar
until at least mid-March"; the Texians thought it "would not march until spring, when the grass had begun to grow again. They
overlooked the fact that mesquite grass sprouted earlier." **No letter of Travis's giving a date was read.**

Against it, one late memoir (Menchaca, dictated decades after) has a courier from Plácido Benavides reach a ball at Béxar on the
night of 10–11 February and Travis reckon the army thirteen or fourteen days from the Rio Grande — about the 20th or 21st, not
mid-March. Hardin disputes Lord's reading of the scene. **DISPUTED.**

**How Santa Anna beat the grass** (DOCUMENTED, his own *Manifesto*, Castañeda pp. 10–11): "The people of Nuevo León and
Coahuila ... donated food supplies to the army"; "all transportation is done on mule back"; money-lenders paid for the march.
Caro: the soldiers marched "in the middle of the winter", on half rations of hard-tack.

**So the owner's instinct is right about the shape and wrong about the cause.** The Texians were caught because they reckoned
no army could move before the grass; the snow had nothing to do with it, and nobody in Texas is recorded as having heard of it
that winter. "News appropriate to that" is therefore **the grass**, and that is what the game tells.

## 2. What Béxar was the week before (`HIST-TEX-611`)

Small, unpaid, short of clothes, horses and food (`HIST-TEX-049`); Neill gone on furlough about 11 February; the volunteers
electing Bowie and the regulars following Travis, the two sharing command (`HIST-TEX-051`). On the 24th Travis wrote: "When the
enemy appeared in sight we had not three bushels of corn" — then "80 or 90 bushels" and "20 or 30 head of Beeves" gathered.

## 4. February 23 (`HIST-TEX-613`)

- **Almonte** (DOCUMENTED): the army marched at 7:30 a.m.; "At half a league from Bexar the division halted on the hills of
  Alazan at 12 1/2 o'clock"; "At 2 PM the army took up their march"; "At 3 PM the enemy filed off to the fort of Alamo." Sesma
  had held back because a captured "spy of the enemy" said a Texian advance was coming.
- **The sentry and the bell** (Sutherland): a man posted "on the roof of the old church" with orders to ring the bell; he cried
  "The enemy are in view"; others shouted "false alarm", and he swore "he had seen them".
- **The scouts**: Sutherland and John W. Smith rode out on the Laredo road; the road muddy "in consequence of a heavy rain
  through the previous night"; Sutherland's horse fell, rolling "directly across my knees". **How near they came: DISPUTED** —
  "within one hundred and fifty yards of fifteen hundred men" (Sutherland) or a mile and a half (Wikipedia, citing Nofi).
- **The hour of the bell: DISPUTED** — about 2:30 p.m. (Lindley; `HIST-TEX-054`) or late morning (Sutherland's telling).
- **Into the Alamo**: beeves driven down Alamo Street, corn found in the empty jacales (Sutherland); the Dickinsons, Juana Navarro
  Alsbury and Gertrudis Navarro that afternoon, the Esparzas that night through a chapel window (Wikipedia, citing Lord pp. 95, 105).

## 5. How the word went out (`HIST-TEX-616`)

- **To Gonzales**: Travis to Judge Andrew Ponton, "Commandancy of Bexar, 3 o'clock p.m.": "The enemy in large force is in sight.
  We want men and provisions. Send them to us. We have 150 men and are determined to defend the Alamo to the last. Give us
  assistance." The transcriptions differ in small words ("are in sight"; "defend the garrison"); one adds "P.S. Send an express
  to San Felipe with news night and day". **Carrier DISPUTED**: Launcelot Smither ("I left Bexar on the 23rd at 4 P.M.") and/or
  Sutherland and Smith (Sutherland: left "Between three and four o'clock", reached Gonzales "about four o'clock, P. M." on
  "Wednesday, the twenty-fourth"). **Both put the word in Gonzales on the afternoon of the 24th.**
- **To Goliad**: Travis and Bowie to Fannin, "We have removed all the men to the Alamo ... one hundred and forty six men", by "a
  young man by the name of Johnson"; at Goliad the next day.
- **San Felipe**: the note of the 23rd on the night of the 25th (Gray, 26 February: "The people now begin to think the wolf has
  actually come at last"). **Washington**: "considerable excitement ... owing to the news from Bexar" by the evening of the 27th.
- **Travis's own words on the surprise**: NOT FOUND in the letters read — no "unexpectedly". The nearest: "not three bushels of
  corn" (24 February); the enemy "entered the city of Bexar, which could not be prevented, as I had not sufficient force to occupy
  both positions" (25 February); "on the arrival of the enemy in Bexar ten days ago" (3 March).

## 6. What was built, and the choices made (2026-09-26)

**What families hear before the 23rd.**

- **Every family, at dawn on 17 February** (`spring-grass`, where the old `santa-anna-rumour` stood): *"Word from Béxar: Colonel
  Travis does not look for the Mexican army before the middle of March, when the grass is up and its horses and oxen can feed on
  the prairie. The men holding Béxar are few and short of everything - money, clothes, horses, food - and they have two
  commanders, Travis for the regulars and Bowie for the volunteers, who do not always agree."* (`HIST-TEX-610`, `-611`.)
- **Removed**: the rumour that Santa Anna "himself has crossed the Rio Grande with a great army, through snow, and is marching on
  Béxar", and `warnGarrison`'s "It is said Santa Anna is marching on Béxar".
- **Only a family with somebody in or within three miles of Béxar, at nine on the evening of 20 February** (`herrera`), as a
  `rumor` that is never the public's (so no tavern repeats it): *"At dark a Tejano rider, Blas Herrera, came in to Béxar saying
  there are Mexican soldiers on the Rio Grande. The officers talked it over in Colonel Travis's room until late. Most of them do
  not believe it - no army can come before the grass, they say - and they broke up without deciding anything."* It says soldiers
  are on the river, not that they are coming; Herrera's "forced march ... by surprise" is left out on purpose. With it, a family
  with somebody in the garrison is told the way out (`FIC-GONZ-383`): he "can still be sent for. If a Mexican army ever did
  reach the town, the garrison would be shut in, and nobody could be sent for then."
- **Not told** (`FIC-GONZ-620`, the owner's choice): the express of 18 February at San Felipe that a thousand men had passed the
  Rio Grande (`HIST-TEX-615`). The record has it; the owner asked that nobody hear the army is marching. **The reveal tells the
  class that the word came.**

**February 23, the shock.**

- **Drawn** (the engine's `arrival` phase, now 80 minutes from 14:30, and the red flag 160, so every later phase keeps its
  minute): the sentry on San Fernando crying "The enemy are in view!" and the street's "False alarm!" (both documented, Sutherland);
  Sutherland (added to the roster) and J. W. Smith riding out along the west road and back at a gallop; the townspeople who were
  left hurrying out; the garrison waiting in the town until the scouts are back, then running across the river into the Alamo
  ("They're coming! Into the Alamo!", "Bring the corn! Drive the cattle in!"); the army from out of sight up the west road,
  showing on the heights, then marching in. The caption tells it, with "we had not three bushels of corn" (`FIC-GONZ-621`).
- **Through a person, on the day**: every family with somebody in or within three miles of Béxar (`FIC-GONZ-622`) gets the card
  at that person's side — *"At Tomás's side: the bell of San Fernando is ringing. The sentry in the tower has seen Mexican
  cavalry on the road from the west - the army nobody here looked for before March. The garrison is running for the Alamo, and
  Tomás with them."* — with Watch; and learns it (`bexar-arrival`), in the family's journal in the words of §4.
- **The Host**: the spotlight on the compound — *"Béxar, February 23, about half past two: the bell of San Fernando rings - Mexican
  cavalry in sight, weeks before anybody looked for them. The garrison runs for the Alamo and Santa Anna's army marches into the
  town. The families in the colonies will not hear of it for days."*
- **Everybody else, when a rider comes**: Gonzales at four on the afternoon of the 24th, with Travis's note to Ponton in his
  words; the other settlements at noon on the 26th (the existing `travis-colonies`, between San Felipe's night of the 25th and
  Washington's 27th), with the note's first words; the public (the taverns) with them. Travis's letter of the 24th follows as
  before (`alamo-siege`, the 25th at Gonzales, the 26th elsewhere).
