## The one-line answer

**On the afternoon of February 23 Santa Anna's advance reached Béxar and the garrison - about 156 effective men and 14 sick -
went into the Alamo with the families of a few of them. A red flag of no quarter went up. For twelve days the Mexican guns
closed in while couriers rode out through the lines: Travis's letter of the 24th reached Gonzales on the 25th and San Felipe
by the 26th. Fannin set out from Goliad with 320 men on the 26th and turned back within a mile. On the 27th about 25 men left
Gonzales under George Kimbell and Albert Martin; 32 got through the Mexican lines into the Alamo in the dark of March 1.
Bonham rode back in on March 3 with word that Fannin was not coming. At about 5 or 5:30 a.m. on Sunday, March 6, some 1,400–1,800
Mexican soldiers in four columns stormed the walls, and within an hour to ninety minutes every defender was dead - 182 to 257
of them, 189 on the official list - a handful of them executed after surrendering. The women, the children and Travis's
slave Joe were spared.** On March 11 two Tejano riders brought the news to Gonzales, where Houston had just arrived; he did
not believe them at first. Susanna Dickinson and Joe confirmed it about the 13th, and that night Houston burned Gonzales and
retreated: the Runaway Scrape began. Meanwhile, south, Urrea surprised Johnson at San Patricio at 3 a.m. on February 27 and
destroyed Grant's party at Agua Dulce Creek on March 2.

**The death rate among the Alamo's defenders is 1: nobody who fought survived. Noncombatants survived. Couriers who were sent
out before the end survived.**

## 3. Who survived, and the rates

- **Defenders:** all killed. 182–257 (Wikipedia); 189 on the official list, research may raise it to 257 (TSHA). Five to seven
  who surrendered were executed on Santa Anna's order. **Death rate among those who fought: 1.0.**
- **Noncombatants:** Susanna Dickinson and her infant daughter Angelina, Travis's slave Joe, Juana Navarro Alsbury and her
  son, and other Tejano women and children, spared.
- **Couriers:** the men Travis sent out - Martin, Seguín and Cruz, Bonham (who came back), Smith, Allen and others - survived
  the siege, except Bonham.
- **The Gonzales relief:** all 32 died.
- **Fannin's relief:** never came within a mile of Goliad's walls; nobody hurt.
- **Mexican losses:** about 600 killed and wounded (TSHA); 400–600 (most historians, Wikipedia).
- **San Patricio (Feb 27):** 34 men; 8 killed, 13 captured, 6 escaped (TSHA; the figures do not sum to 34). **About 24 in 100
  killed, 38 captured, 18 escaped.** Prisoners to Matamoros.
- **Agua Dulce (Mar 2):** 26 men; 14 killed, 6 captured, 6 escaped (five of whom later died at Goliad). **About 54 in 100
  killed, 23 captured, 23 escaped.** Prisoners to Matamoros.

## 4. What families heard, and when

- Travis's letter of February 24 reached Gonzales on the 25th (Martin) and the acting governor by the 26th; Governor Smith's
  appeal followed: "The call is upon ALL who are able to bear arms."
- The Convention received Travis's letter of the 25th on the evening of March 2 and "took no further action", believing
  Fannin on the way.
- **The fall:** on March 11 Andrés Bárcena and Anselmo Bergara reached Gonzales, where Houston had just arrived, and were
  arrested as spies; Susanna Dickinson and Joe confirmed it about March 12–13. Gonzales and its ferry were burned the night of
  March 13 and Houston retreated toward the Colorado with about 400 men, ordering the inhabitants to go with him.

## 5. What the repository has wrong or too simple

- **The second period ends February 23** (`winter-end`), the day the siege begins.
- **Service has no fights** (`sim/winter.mjs`): garrison members stand at Béxar and the Matamoros men at Refugio, with nobody
  at San Patricio or Agua Dulce, which are not on the map.
- **`rollFates`** (`sim/army.mjs`) rolls killed and wounded; a battle that takes **prisoners** (San Patricio, Agua Dulce, and
  later Coleto and Goliad) needs a captured fate, and `captured` exists as a condition but nothing decides what becomes of a
  prisoner.
- **News in the winter reaches every family at once**; the fall of the Alamo is the most famous case of news that travelled,
  was disbelieved, and was confirmed.
