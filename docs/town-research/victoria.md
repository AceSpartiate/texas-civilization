# Guadalupe Victoria in 1835 — what is known about the town plan

Research only. Nothing here is built, and no claim below has a `HIST-` or `FIC-` ID yet;
IDs are assigned when something is drawn. Read alongside `docs/town-research/san-felipe.md`,
which this follows in shape, and `public/bexar-layout.js` and `public/gonzales-art.js`, the
two towns already drawn building by building, which set the vocabulary the sketch in §7 uses.

Researched 2026-09-15. Every URL in §1 was opened and read on that date. The measurements in
§2.6 and §3.3 were computed here on that date from the sources named.

**The one-line answer.** More of Victoria's plan is recoverable than San Felipe's, and by a
different route. **The 1824/1830s plat itself was not found** — no archive read holds it. But
three things survive that together fix the grid on the ground: the **thirteen original street
names with their modern equivalents**, published by the City of Victoria from Dr. Robert
Shook's research; a **c. 1850 manuscript plat in the Texas State Archives** that states in its
own hand *"Side of Blocks = 100 varas each (square blocks)"* with streets of 21 43/100 varas;
and the **surviving street grid itself**, which still stands at that module and can be
measured. The thirteen named streets turn out to form a single unbroken rectangle **five
streets by eight** — a core of **4 × 7 = 28 blocks**, about 1,350 × 2,360 ft, bearing
**N 20.7° E**, on a terrace about **55 ft above the Guadalupe**, with the river about **half a
mile** away to the south-west. Four squares inside it can be placed by block.

What is missing is the built town. **No source read counts the buildings of 1835, describes
the town's appearance, or places any house except by street corner.** The materials are
documented only as a class — jacales first, hewn log after — and the count of houses is
nowhere. That is the gap that decides how much of a drawn Victoria would be invention.

### 1b. Sources looked for and not found

- **The original plat of Guadalupe Victoria does not appear in any archive searched.** A title
  search of the whole Texas State Archives Map Collection for "Victoria" returns 24 records;
  the earliest town document is S5, the **c. 1850** plat. There is no 1824 and no 1830s plat
  of the town there. **If De León's or Carbajal's plat survives, this research did not find
  where.** Its most likely homes are the Victoria County Clerk's plat records and the Victoria
  Regional History Center; neither was searched.
- **The Portal to Texas History could not be reached.** Every attempt — direct fetch and
  browser, repeatedly over the session — returned its "Validating your request… I'm not a
  robot" interstitial and never resolved. The Portal is the single most likely place for a
  digitised Victoria town map and **it is entirely unsearched here**. That is the largest hole
  in this research and the first thing to redo.
- **The Texas General Land Office map collection** was searched through its public interface
  and its keyword filter would not apply; no Victoria town plan was retrieved. The GLO's
  De León Colony Records (S10) are described as titles and correspondence, with **no map
  mentioned** in the finding aid.
- **No published archaeological report** on the original Victoria townsite was found. Nothing
  read describes an excavation, a feature, a foundation or a house pattern in the town. This
  is a real absence, not a gap in searching effort: the searches that found San Felipe's lot
  566 dig found nothing comparable for Victoria.
- **John J. Linn, *Reminiscences of Fifty Years in Texas* (1883), was not read.** Linn lived in
  Victoria from 1829, was its last alcalde and first mayor, and is the obvious Victoria
  counterpart to Smithwick at San Felipe. The only Internet Archive copy is a lending-library
  scan of the 1935 reprint whose text is not retrievable. **This is the most valuable unread
  source for this town** and should be read before anything is drawn.
- **A. C. C. Crimm, *De León: A Tejano Family History* (University of Texas Press, 2003)**, the
  standard modern scholarship, was not read — only its publisher description. Note that the
  publisher's blurb gives Martín De León's death as **1834**, where every other source read
  gives **1833**; that disagreement is left standing.
- **The 1873 Brosius bird's-eye view of Victoria** (S19) was catalogued but the image was not
  examined. It is 38 years after the period, but it is the earliest townscape located.
- **Sanborn fire insurance maps of Victoria** exist at the Library of Congress (an 1891 edition
  was indexed in search results) but every LOC URL attempted returned HTTP 403 and **no sheet
  was read**. Sanborn sheets give street names and widths and would independently check §2.6.

## 2. Documented — the plan

### 2.1 Who laid it out, and when — the sources disagree

- Martín De León founded the town in **1824** on the lower Guadalupe, at a site earlier called
  **Cypress Grove**, and named it for Our Lady of Guadalupe and for Guadalupe Victoria, first
  president of Mexico (S1, S2). The full name appears variously as *Nuestra Señora de
  Guadalupe de Jesús Victoria* (S2, S8) and *Villa Nuestra Señora de Guadalupe de Jesús
  Victoria* (S6).
- **José María Jesús Carbajal** (the marker spells it **Carvajal** and notes some descendants
  use *Carbajal*) surveyed and platted the town. He was De León's son-in-law, a protégé of
  Stephen F. Austin, educated in Kentucky, and completed his surveying studies **by 1831**
  (S1, S2, S3-Carvajal).
- **The date of the survey is disputed.** The THC's De Leon Plaza marker says the plaza "was
  included as part of an early 1830s survey of Victoria by Jose M. Carbajal" and that
  Carbajal's survey "expanded on the founder's basic design" (S3-De Leon Plaza). The 1892
  Courthouse marker and the THC's National Register nomination both say De León himself
  **platted the townsite in 1824** and that the 1824 plat map already included the burial
  ground (S3-Courthouse, S9). Rose says the town was "not actually laid out, however, until
  about 1830" (S7). Carbajal's own marker says he was the surveyor of "Victoria's first plat"
  without a date.
  **Do not resolve this.** The defensible statement is: a basic design of 1824 by De León, an
  expanded survey by Carbajal in the early 1830s — which is also the only reading in which the
  1824 and 1830s claims are both true.
- Whatever its date, the survey was **finished before 1835**: the city's own street-name panel
  is titled "The Street of Ten Friends **1835**" and captions its map "Streets in Guadalupe
  Victoria • 1835" (S3-Diez Amigos).

### 2.2 The rule it was laid out under

The Carvajal marker states the governing rule plainly, and it is the most useful single
sentence found about this town's plan: Guadalupe Victoria was platted according to Spanish
Colonization Law, **which dictated the width of streets, a market square, a public square, a
square for government buildings and for the church, and blocks for schools and cemeteries**
(S3-Carvajal). The Diez Amigos marker attributes the grid and the placing of the landmarks to
the **Laws of the Indies** (S3-Diez Amigos). S12 gives the same scheme in local terms: a
public square, a government square on one side, a church square on the other, and a market
square nearby.

The townsite was laid out within **four square leagues** (S6, S15), which S15 reckons at 4,428
acres to the league. Each settler received **a town lot, plus a league of grazing land and a
labor of 177 acres of arable** (S13). Fernando de León as land commissioner issued **102 land
titles for the colony and 59 titles for town lots in Guadalupe Victoria** (S10) — the only
count of town lots found anywhere.

### 2.3 The squares

Four squares are named or located by sources read, and a fifth is named as planned and never
built.

1. **Plaza de Mercado — Market Square.** "The Plaza de Mercado, Market Square, was the center
   of the town for the first twenty-five years" (S3-Carvajal, quoted). S12 says it "formed
   where City Hall now stands" and that De Leon Plaza is "a few blocks north of the original".
   S6 records dancing "in the early market square" in De León's own lifetime. The Heaton House
   marker calls the same ground the **Plaza del Mercado** (S3-Heaton).
2. **Plaza de la Constitución — now De Leon Plaza.** "Originally called 'Plaza de la
   Constitución' by Martin de Leon" (S3-De Leon Plaza, quoted); renamed De Leon Plaza in 1941
   (the marker) or "in the 1940s" (S14). A brick-lined well was sunk in its centre in 1850.
3. **The government square — Courthouse Square.** De León "designated public squares, including
   one that would later be shared by city and county governments for many years"
   (S3-Courthouse, quoted). The county's first courthouse, a two-storey brick building, went
   up on Courthouse Square in 1849. S14 notes that the 1892 courthouse stands on a corner
   *across from* the plaza rather than in the middle of it — Spanish practice, not Anglo.
4. **The burial ground — Memorial Square.** "This square was laid out in 1824 when Martin de
   Leon founded the town" and "In early years the cemetery included the block to the east"
   (S3-Memorial Square, quoted) — i.e. **two blocks**. S9 says the same from the plat side:
   De León's 1824 plat map included a two-block burial ground, used for public interments
   until the 1860s. S3-Memorial Square (Trail) adds that Spanish colonisation requirements
   dictated land be set aside for public burial but that home burial persisted and the ground
   was **seldom used in De León's time**.
5. **The church square that was never built.** De León planned the town's growth around "a
   magnificent cathedral, rivaling the greatest cathedrals in Mexico", had sketches and
   estimates ready, and died in the 1833 cholera epidemic before bringing engineers from
   Mexico (S6). S12 says he never built the church, which was to stand near the site of a
   present-day downtown sandwich shop — a location this document declines to convert into a
   coordinate. **The grand church square is a plan, not a place.**

**One claim contradicts the rest and should be recorded, not adopted.** Wikipedia's article on
DeLeon Plaza says the plaza "began as a Market Square designed in 1839 by Edward Linn" (S13).
Every other source read makes Market Square a separate square four blocks south, and makes the
plaza De León's own Plaza de la Constitución from the 1820s–30s. Wikipedia's own infobox also
contradicts its text on the plaza's size ("1.77 acres" against "less than one acre"). Treat
S13 as weak here.

### 2.4 The streets

**Thirteen original street names are known, and all thirteen can be matched to a modern
street.** This is the single most valuable find in this research. The Diez Amigos marker states
the frame: "we know the names of the thirteen original streets. Calle Libertad, Calle
Empesario, and La Calle de los Diez Amigos, are three of those names. The remaining street
names are those of the 'Ten Friends'" (S3-Diez Amigos), and credits the graphic *Streets in
Guadalupe Victoria • 1835* to Dr. Robert Shook. The City of Victoria then published the full
correspondence when it put historic-name signs under the modern ones for the bicentennial
(S4); the city says the names were "determined through original research by historian Dr.
Robert Shook and verified by Victoria Preservation, Inc." (S4, quoted).

Of the ten friends, only **De León and Manchola actually lived in Victoria**; the rest were
military officers who may or may not have served with De León, and four of the ten —
Santa Anna, Guadalupe Victoria, Bustamante and Guerrero — became presidents of Mexico
(S11, quoting Blanche De León, a sixth-generation descendant).

**Calle de los Diez Amigos was the main street** (S1, S2, S6, S7). The town's later boundaries
were "officially outlined in 1877 by North, South, East, and West streets" (S1, quoted),
replacing earlier Spanish terminology; S9 calls the ground inside those four streets the
**historic limits of the city**.

### 2.5 Block and lot size, from the plat

S5, the c. 1850 manuscript plat in the Texas State Archives, carries a three-line note in the
surveyor's hand across its head. Read off the image at full resolution:

> **"Block 1, R. 2, E. above Town — Divided into Buildg. lots & known as Halls, Giddings,
> Linns & Jennings Surveys. Side of Blocks = 100 varas each (square blocks). Streets =
> 21 43/100 ". 4 (4 sqr. lots)"**

Readings are `[legible]` for *Side of Blocks = 100 varas each (square blocks)* and
*Streets = 21 43/100*, `[probable]` for *4 sqr. lots* and for the surveyors' names.

Converting at 33⅓ inches to the vara:

| Dimension | Varas | Feet | Metres |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Block, each side | 100 | 277.8 | 84.67 |
| Street width | 21.43 | 59.5 | 18.14 |
| **Block-plus-street module** | **121.43** | **337.3** | **102.81** |
| Lot (four square lots to a block) | 50 | 138.9 | 42.3 |

The rest of the sheet: a **north arrow pointing up the sheet**; the tract's west line labelled
**N. 5° W.** and its north line **N. 85° E.**, with **S. 70° E.** on another boundary; the
blocks numbered in a grid with lot numbers running to 32; and, across the bottom-left corner,
**the corner of the town of Victoria itself**, with "Victoria" written across it, **Main St.**
named, numbered town blocks (68, 72, 73, 88, 89, 104, 113, 117, 118, 133, 140, 147 are legible)
and **two small buildings drawn in plan** — one a rectangle with wings, neither labelled.

**The caveat that matters.** This sheet's subject is *Block 1, Range 2, East above Town* — an
outer subdivision, not the town. Its bearing, N 5° W, is **not** the town's bearing (§2.6), and
the two grids are visibly at different angles on the same sheet. So the 100-vara note is
documented **for the outer blocks**. That it also describes the town grid is an inference —
but a strong one, because 121.43 varas is 102.81 m and the surviving town grid measures
**102.2 m** (§2.6), a 0.6 % difference, and because the town blocks and the outer blocks are
drawn at the same size on the same sheet.

### 2.6 The grid on the ground, measured

The grid survives. Every street centreline in downtown Victoria was pulled from OpenStreetMap
(S17) and projected into a local frame. Results:

- **Bearing.** The "north–south" streets run at a median **N 20.7° E**; the cross streets at
  **110.7°** — exactly perpendicular. Every one of the fifty-odd downtown segments measured
  falls within about a degree of those two values. **The grid's orientation is therefore not
  unknown, as San Felipe's is. It is 20.7° east of north.**
- **Module.** Consecutive parallel street centrelines are **102.2 m apart on average**
  (individual spacings 100.8–102.9 m) **in both directions**. The blocks are square. At 0.8467 m
  to the vara that is **120.7 varas**, against the plat's stated 121.43.
- **The thirteen named streets form one unbroken rectangle.** Ordered by position, the modern
  equivalents of the thirteen fall into two contiguous runs with no gaps:
  - **five** streets across, west to east: Glass (*Victoria*), Bridge (*Bustamante*), Main
    (*Diez Amigos*), Liberty (*Guerrero*), William (*Santa Anna*)
  - **eight** streets along, south to north: Murray (*Manchola*), Convent (*De León*), Church
    (*Empresario*), Juan Linn (*Terán*), Santa Rosa (*Ahumada*), Constitution (*Chovel*),
    Forrest (*Artiaga*), Goodwin (*Libertad*)

## 3. Documented — the river, the road and the site

### 3.1 The river and the ford

- The town stands on the **lower Guadalupe River** (S1, S2, S20). The 1936 Guadalupe River
  marker states the reason it stands where it does: "Here at a ford, used since Indian days,
  Empresario Martín de León founded the town of Victoria in 1824" (S3-Guadalupe River, quoted).
  **Victoria, like San Felipe, was platted at a crossing.**
- The marker stands on Business U.S. 77 south of West Water Street, at 28° 47.612′ N,
  97° 0.75′ W — about **66 m from the modern channel centreline** and about **630 m (0.39 mi)
  south-west of Market Square**.
- Victoria sat on the **La Bahía road** (S9), the Louisiana–San Antonio trade route, and later
  on the supply line from the Lavaca Bay ports to San Antonio and Austin (S19). The project's
  own `HIST-TEX-008` already puts the Atascosito road through Goliad and Victoria; **the two
  road names are both on the record for this town and are not reconciled here.**
- Steamboats worked the Guadalupe from Victoria to the coast despite the river being declared
  unnavigable for log jams (S3, *Steamboating and the Guadalupe River*, read in the marker
  index only). That is 1850s, not 1835.

## 4. Documented and strongly supported — the buildings

### 4.1 What the town was built of — the one thing this town's sources are good on

This matters more here than at San Felipe, and the sources are unusually direct.

- **Jacales first, and only jacales.** Rose: "Jacals were the sole kind of houses erected"
  (S7, quoted). Hammett says the same and describes them: houses "adobe type, were known as
  'jacals' and were only temporary, containing dirt floors and other inconveniences" (S6).
  Wikipedia's De León's Colony article records the settlement as beginning as a hamlet of
  jacales (S13).
- **Then hand-hewn logs.** "Later more pretentious and better houses were built of hand hewn
  logs" (S6, quoted). Patricia de la Garza De León "lived in a house of hand-hewn logs with a
  dirt floor" and put fine furniture in it (S2/TSHA entry on her, read through search summary;
  S6 gives the same).
- **Hewn log for the public buildings too.** The first church and the Round Top House were
  both of hewn logs (S7). The town's 1843–44 jail was of hewn logs "about a foot square, and
  pinned together by wooden pins" (S15, quoted) — a decade late, but the same technique.
- **Cypress.** TSHA says settlers built primarily from **cypress lumber** until stone and brick
  replaced it in the 1880s (S1). The founding site's earlier name was **Cypress Grove** (S1).
  Cypress and hewn log are not in conflict — cypress is what the logs and the sawn boards were
  — but no source read says which buildings were sawn and which were hewn round.
- **Nothing read describes adobe or stone construction in the town**, beyond Hammett's loose
  use of "adobe type" for the jacal. **There is no evidence Victoria was an adobe town.** It
  was a jacal-and-hewn-log town on a Spanish grid — which is exactly what makes it look
  different from San Felipe, and different from Béxar too.

### 4.3 Who governed, and where from

The municipality was run by a **Council of Ten Friends from 1824 to 1828**, then by **four
alcaldes from 1828 to 1836: Martín and Silvestre De León, Plácido Benavides (twice), and
John J. Linn** (S1). **No source read names a building the ayuntamiento met in.** The
government square is documented; what stood on it in 1835 is not.

**The 59 town-lot titles are the closest thing to a building count that exists**, and they are

### 4.5 What happened to the town in 1835–36

Relevant because a student may walk into it in October 1835 and because it shapes what the
place should feel like:

- Victoria contributed **volunteers, supplies and arms** to the Texian cause; Linn represented
  the town at the Consultation of 1835 and sat on the General Council (S1, S3-Linn).
- Houston ordered Fannin to fall back **from Goliad to Victoria** in 1836 for its defensive
  position; after Coleto, **Urrea's Mexican army occupied Victoria** until San Jacinto (S1).
- In **July 1836 General Thomas J. Rusk ordered the Carvajal and De León families out of
  Victoria**; both families left for New Orleans "stripped of everything they owned"
  (S3-Carvajal). Most of the original Mexican families never came back. **The Tejano town this
  research describes was destroyed as a community within a year of the period the game
  covers** — which is a fact about the place, and belongs in any text shown to a student.

## 5. Unknown — what a layout would need and no source read supplies

This section matters as much as §2–4: everything in it would have to be invented and
registered as fiction.

1. **The plat itself.** No 1824 or 1830s plan of Guadalupe Victoria was located in any archive
   (§1b). Every statement about what the plat contained is second-hand.
2. **Whether the thirteen named streets are the whole platted town.** They form a clean 5 × 8
   rectangle (§2.6), which is suggestive, but **no source says the original town was 28
   blocks**, and the burial ground lies outside it (§5.4). The plat drawn in 1850 numbers
   blocks into the 140s.
3. **The dimensions of the town's own blocks and streets.** §2.5's 100-vara block and
   21.43-vara street are documented for an **outer** subdivision on a c. 1850 sheet, and match
   the surviving grid to within 0.6 %. **Nobody states them for De León's town.**
4. **Why the burial ground sits outside the named-street core.** Documented as part of the 1824
   plat (S3, S9); measured here as lying about 1½ blocks beyond the core's north-east corner.
   Either the platted town was larger than the thirteen streets, or the cemetery was
   deliberately set outside the built town, or one of the two records is wrong. **No source
   read resolves it.**
5. **The size and shape of every square.** Each is treated here as one block because that is
   how the surviving blocks are shaped; **no source gives a plaza dimension.** The only figure
   found, 1.77 acres for De Leon Plaza, is from a source that contradicts itself in the same
   article (§2.3) — although 1.77 acres happens to be exactly a 100-vara square, which is why
   §6, I-2 leans on it lightly.
6. **Which corner of Main × Church held the church, and which held De León's house.** S8 puts
   the first church on the post office site at Main and Church; S6 and S7 put De León's *house*
   on the St. Mary's / Nazareth Convent site at the same crossing; S7 separately puts the
   church on the ground later covered by St. Joseph's College; the 1936 marker for De León's
   house stands two streets west at Church & Bridge. **Four statements, at least three
   different corners. Unresolved.**
7. **Which block the Round Top House stood on**, and Linn's house, and the church. The 1936
   markers fix a crossing and say "across the street" — which leaves two candidate blocks each.
8. **Any dimension of any building.** Not one. No length, width, height, or roof pitch. The
   whole architectural record is: jacales with dirt floors; hewn-log houses; a round
   fortified house with first-floor gun slits and a reinforced door; and De León's plan of one
   big room with a large chimney, a ten-foot hall, and a small back room. **Every footprint
   would be invented.**
9. **The number of buildings, houses or households in 1835.** Nothing. §4.4 is the whole of it.
10. **Where the ayuntamiento met, where any store stood, where the school was.** All three
    institutions are documented; none has a location.
11. **Where the ford actually was.** A 1936 marker stands near it; the 1835 channel is not the
    modern one; and nothing read gives the crossing's relation to the grid.
12. **Any archaeology.** Nothing read describes a single excavated feature in the original
    townsite. Compare San Felipe, where at least one building position is confirmed by dug
    evidence. **For Victoria there is none.**
13. **Whether jacales or hewn-log houses predominated by 1835.** The sequence is documented;
    the proportion eleven years in is not.
14. **What a jacal in Victoria looked like in detail** — post spacing, wall fill, roof
    covering, size. Hammett gives dirt floors and calls them "adobe type"; nothing else.
15. **Whether the 1835 town was fenced, walled, or open.** A fort and a militia are recorded;
    no enclosure is.

## 6. Inferred — things that follow but that nobody states

Each is an inference of this document and must be labelled as one if it is used.

- **I-1. The original platted core is 4 × 7 = 28 blocks, bounded by Calle Victoria, Calle Santa
  Anna, Calle Manchola and Calle Libertad.** This follows from the thirteen documented street
  names (S4) falling into two contiguous runs of five and eight with no gap, measured on the
  surviving grid (S17). **Nobody says it.** It is the load-bearing inference of this document:
  if it is wrong, the extent of the drawn town is wrong, though the squares and the street
  names are not.
- **I-2. A town block is 100 varas square and the streets are about 21½ varas wide.** The plat
  note (§2.5) says this of the outer blocks; the surviving town grid's 102.2 m module matches
  the implied 102.81 m to 0.6 %; and the one plaza acreage on record, 1.77 acres, is within
  0.3 % of a 100-vara square. Three weak-to-moderate arrows pointing the same way.
- **I-3. The grid bears N 20.7° E.** Measured off the modern streets (S17), which are
  demonstrably the same streets under new names. The inference is that the modern bearing is
  the original bearing — i.e. that the grid has never been re-laid. Nothing read suggests it
  has, and the block module's survival argues it has not.
- **I-4. The town sits on a terrace about 55 ft above the Guadalupe bottom, and the grid's
  south-west quarter runs off the edge of it.** Measured (S18). **No source calls it a bluff or
  a terrace**, unlike San Felipe, where the bluff is documented and the line is not.
- **I-5. The four squares stand in the Spanish relationship S12 describes** — public square,
  government square beside it, market square, church square beside the market. Each square's
  position is separately documented (§2.6); their arrangement matching the rule is this
  document's observation.
- **I-6. Market Square was the town's centre of gravity in 1835, not De Leon Plaza.** The
  Carvajal marker says Market Square was the centre "for the first twenty-five years"
  (1824–49); Hammett records dancing in the early market square; the town's three documented
  private buildings — the church corner, the Round Top House, Linn's house — all sit on or
  beside it, and none sits on Plaza de la Constitución. **A drawn 1835 Victoria centred on
  De Leon Plaza would be centred on the wrong square.**
- **I-7. Most of the grid was empty in 1835.** 59 town-lot titles against a grid whose named
  core alone holds 28 blocks of four lots each — 112 lots — and whose outer square holds far
  more. Nobody counts unbuilt lots; the arithmetic is this document's.
- **I-8. The built town in 1835 was a small cluster around Market Square, not a filled grid.**
  Follows from I-6 and I-7 and from the town's ~300 municipal residents. Same warning as
  San Felipe: **a Victoria drawn as a filled grid would be wrong.**

### 7.7 What must be drawn *around* the town

Each settler held **a town lot, a league of grazing, and a labor of 177 acres** (S13), and the
colony's wealth was cattle and horses — 5,000 branded cattle on De León's own ranch by 1824
(S9), the family's holdings valued over a million dollars at his death (S2). **A Victoria drawn
as a town alone would misrepresent a ranching colony.** The 177-acre labor is the same unit
already used in `docs/town-research/san-felipe.md` §7.7 and in `HIST-TEX-` material.

### 7.8 Art gaps this sketch creates

Under the standing practice, each needs a row in `docs/ART_REQUESTS.md` and a stand-in marked
`stand-in:` in code.

- **The Round Top House** — a *round* fortified house with gun slits at first-floor level and a
  heavy door. There is no round building in the atlas. Stand-in: `storehouse` or
  `house-hewn-log` drawn taller, marked `stand-in:` naming the request. This is the one
  building in Victoria that nowhere else in the game has an equivalent, and it is the town's
  own landmark.
- **A jacal row.** `house-jacal` exists (with `-site`, `-walls`, `-roofing` stages), which is
  the single luckiest thing about drawing this town — but the atlas has one jacal. A town whose
  houses were "the sole kind" jacal needs **variation**: two or three jacal silhouettes, and a
  jacal with a ramada or brush porch. Stand-in: reuse `house-jacal` at varied scale, marked
  `stand-in:`.
- **A Spanish-colonial plaza surface** — an open square with a market on it, distinct from the
  street. Béxar's plazas are drawn as open rectangles and the same treatment will serve;
  a **market house** on Plaza de Mercado is 1850s and should not be drawn for 1835.

## 8. Verdict

**Can Victoria be drawn honestly? Yes — and the frame can be drawn more truthfully than
San Felipe's.** The grid's bearing is measured, the block module is documented in a surveyor's
own hand and confirmed on the ground, all thirteen original street names are known and can be
placed street by street, and four of the town's squares can be put on specific blocks. Nothing
comparable was recoverable for San Felipe, where the bearing, every dimension and every plaza
size are unknown.

**But the buildings are thinner than San Felipe's, and by a lot.** San Felipe has Smithwick's
building-by-building walk down the road and twenty named businesses on numbered lots. Victoria
has **four buildings with a crossing and no block, no building count, no eyewitness description
of the town, and no archaeology at all.** Everything above the ground plane would be invention.

**What that means for the game.** A student who walks into Victoria in October 1835 can be
walked down streets with their real names, past real squares in their real relationship, on a
grid at its real angle, and can be told truthfully that the houses are jacales with dirt floors
and a few better ones of hand-hewn logs. What they cannot be shown is a particular house
belonging to a particular person on a particular lot — except Linn's, the church, De León's and
the Round Top House, each of which is only as precise as a street corner. **That is a good
trade**: the arrangement is real and the furniture is admitted invention, which is the same
contract `BEXAR_LAYOUT`'s note already makes.

**The four things most worth doing before drawing it**, in order of value:

1. **Get into the Portal to Texas History**, which this research could not reach (§1b). It is
   the likeliest home of a digitised Victoria town map and of the Shook research behind the
   thirteen street names, and it is the one obvious stone left unturned.
2. **Read John J. Linn's *Reminiscences of Fifty Years in Texas* (1883).** Linn lived on the
   market square from 1829, was alcalde in 1836, and entertained the Greys and the Red Rovers
   in his own house. If a description of the town in 1835 survives anywhere, it is there.
3. **Search the Victoria County Clerk's plat records and the Victoria Regional History Center**
   for the original plat. The c. 1850 sheet numbers town blocks, so a numbered plat existed;
   somebody holds it.
4. **Read Crimm, *De León: A Tejano Family History* (2003).** It is the standing scholarship,
   it is unread here, and it would settle at least the 1824-versus-1830s survey question and
   probably the De León house site.
