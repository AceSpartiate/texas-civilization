## The one-line answer

**Liberty is the only town in this series that was laid out under Mexican law as a Mexican town, and
its plan survives almost completely: a square of forty-nine blocks, seven by seven, on a module of
140 varas, laid to the cardinal points, with five public plazas — four of them in one row across the
middle of the town.**

Four independent things say so and they agree:

1. **A stated block count.** The 1968 *Plaza Constitucional* marker says Madero "platted Liberty town
   tract, 1831, with 49 squares in inner city. Five were set aside for public usage" (S3-Constitucional).
   The 2002 National Register nomination for the courthouse independently calls it "the 49-block
   Villa de las Santisima Trinidad de la Libertad" (S4).
2. **A stated block dimension.** The Liberty County Historical Commission's own account of the
   courthouse states a plaza "measuring 120 varas (333.33 feet) on each side" for the Casa
   Consistorial (S5, quoted). **A 120-vara block is the only plat dimension stated for any town in
   this series except Mina's street width.**
3. **The Sanborn key sheet of May 1927** (S6) draws the town as a block of forty-nine squares, seven
   by seven, labels the whole of it **ORIGINAL TOWN**, numbers forty-eight of them 1–48 in a
   serpentine, **leaves the forty-ninth unnumbered and marks it COURT HO.**, and labels everything
   outside it **O.B.** — *Outer Block*. It also annotates the street widths: **55.5 ft**, which is
   **20 varas to within a quarter of an inch.**
4. **Five markers on the ground, at five block corners, carrying the plazas' Spanish names.** The
   Texas State Historical Survey Committee marked them in 1968: *Plaza Constitucional*, *Casa
   Consistorial*, *Plaza de Cárcel y Casas de Corrección*, *Plaza de Mercado*, *Plaza Iglesia
   Parroquial*. Every one of their coordinates, converted into the grid frame here, falls inside the
   block the Sanborn numbering predicts (§2.4). **Four of the five are the four consecutive blocks of
   row 4; the fifth is directly below the last of them.**

**The grid, measured.** Off OpenStreetMap centrelines the surviving grid bears **S 88.78° E** and
**N 1.25° W**, square to **0.03°** — the tightest right angle measured in this series, and within a
degree and a quarter of true north. The seven north–south streets stand on a module of **391.3 ft**
over six consecutive intervals. **140 varas is 388.9 ft.** The plat is a 140-vara module — a
120-vara block with 20-vara streets — and the measurement is 0.6 % over it.

**Five corrections to the brief, and two to the repository.**

- **The brief says Liberty was founded "on the site of an older Atascosito settlement." It was not.**
  TSHA places the new seat of government "about three miles southwest of old Atascosito" (S2), and
  the 1936 Atascosito marker stands 3.2 miles north-east of the courthouse square (§3.1). The town
  was platted on **the league of land occupied by Juan M. Smith** — Madero picked it by 30 March 1831
  (S5) — and the 1968 Casa Consistorial marker says the colonists chose **Smith's plantation over
  Moss Bluff "by a narrow vote"** (S3-Consistorial). **Liberty is a new town on a planter's league,
  not a Spanish post that grew.**
- **The brief says Madero established the town. He commissioned it; his surveyor laid it out.**
  Madero's surveyor was **José María Jesús Carbajal**, who was arrested with him by Bradburn (S11).
  Nothing read says in words that Carbajal ran the lines of the town of Liberty, but he is the only
  surveyor in the commission.
- **The brief asks about "any church." There was no church building in Liberty in 1835, and the
  church square was empty ground.** The Plaza Iglesia Parroquial was "set aside and designated for
  Catholic use in 1831" (S3-Iglesia) and **nothing was built on it until a Methodist log church went
  up on its north-east corner in 1846**; the Catholic Church got the south-east corner in **1853**
  (S3-Iglesia). The oldest church *organisation* in Liberty County is the Methodist congregation of
  **1841** (S3-Methodist). **This is the third town in the series where the brief's church postdates
  1835** — Matagorda's was 1841, Columbia's were 1840.
- **The brief says Liberty stood at the head of navigation on the Trinity. The town is 1.3 miles
  from the river** (§3.2), and in 1835 there were no steamboats on the Trinity at all: the
  *Branch T. Archer* was the first, in **May 1838** (S3-Trinity, S13). What stood at the head of
  navigation was a **landing**, not the town.
- **The brief asks for the ayuntamiento, the jail, stores, taverns and the ferry house. Only one
  building in 1835 Liberty has a position and a dimension**, and it is the first courthouse: **a
  hewn log building 22 feet square** (S3-Consistorial), standing on the Casa Consistorial square and
  already in use by **May 1831** (S5). Everything else is unknown (§5).
- **`HIST-TEX-013` mis-dates its own best sentence.** It records "ten or twelve houses when it became
  county seat in 1837." TSHA says **"by 1840 only ten or twelve houses stood at the townsite"** (S1,
  quoted). The town became county seat and was incorporated in 1837; the house count is a **1840**
  figure. §8 says what would change.
- **`HIST-TEX-013` also carries the brief's Atascosito error implicitly** by saying the municipality
  was formed "there" — it is not wrong as written, but anything drawn from it should not put the
  town on the Atascosito site.

**And the trap is the record itself.** Every other town in this series has had a physical trap — a
moved town, a silted bay, a waterfront in the river. Liberty's ground has not moved and its grid is
intact. **What burned is the paper.** The brick courthouse burned on **27 December 1872** and took
most of the county records with it; on **12 December 1874** a second fire at Key's Hall, the
temporary district courtroom where the *salvaged* records were being kept, "All were consumed by
fire" (S5, quoted). Liberty County's land records begin in **1874** (S14, and the Sam Houston
Center's own inventory holds nothing for this county before 1875). **The 1831 plat is almost
certainly not in the county clerk's office, and that is why a 1927 fire-insurance map and a set of
1968 roadside markers are the best surviving description of a Mexican town plan.**

**What these markers are, and how far they can be trusted.** The five plaza markers are a **single
1968 campaign** by the State Historical Survey Committee — they are unfootnoted, and where they
overlap with S4 and S5 they agree. They are treated here as **documented for the existence, the
Spanish names and the positions of the five plazas** (because the National Register nomination,
written thirty-four years later from an independent typescript, reaches the same 49-block plat with
the same casa consistorial), and as **strongly supported** for anything else they say. The two 1936
Centennial markers contradict each other about where the Atascosito crossing was (§3.4) and are used
only as flagged.

### 1b. Sources looked for and not found

- **The Portal to Texas History could not be reached.** One attempt, as the brief directed: it served
  a "Validating your request" interstitial and never resolved. **That is six reports in a row.** The
  Portal is the digital home of the **Sam Houston Regional Library and Research Center**, which is
  itself one of its listed partners — so the single richest archive for this particular town is
  behind the one door this series has never opened. **It is by a distance the largest unopened door
  for Liberty.**
- **The 1831 plat itself was not located, and it may not exist.** S4 and S5 both work from it at one
  remove; neither reproduces it. **The county's own records burned twice, in 1872 and 1874** (S5),
  and the Texas State Library's inventory of Liberty County records at the Sam Houston Center holds
  nothing before 1875 and no map series before 1929 (S14). If a copy survives it is most likely in
  **Béxar or Coahuila archives** (Madero reported to the chief of the Department of Béxar) or in the
  **Texas General Land Office** as a town tract sheet.
- **Fisher, *The Seven Courthouses of Liberty County (1831–1996)*, typescript on file at the Sam
  Houston Regional Library**, is cited by the National Register nomination as the authority for the
  49-block plat (S4) and was not read. **It is the single most valuable unread document**, and it is
  three miles from the townsite.
- **Miriam Partlow, *Liberty, Liberty County, and the Atascosito District* (Austin: Pemberton, 1974)
  was not read.** It is the county's standing history and the first item in TSHA's bibliography for
  both S1 and S2. So was **Arlene Pickett, *Historic Liberty County* (Dallas: Tardy, 1936)**.
- **Elton R. Prewitt et al., *Channel to Liberty: Archeological Survey and Historical Steamboat
  Investigations along the Lower Trinity River, Chambers and Liberty Counties* (Prewitt and
  Associates, Report of Investigations 54, 1986) was not found online.** It is the archaeology of
  exactly the reach of river this town used. **The only lower-Trinity archaeology that could be read
  about is the wreck of the *Black Cloud* (site 41LB37), an 1870s steamboat** — two generations too
  late.
- **David Carlton Hardee, *Reminiscences of Texas as a Republic*, published in *The Patron and
  Gleaner* (Northampton County, N.C.), was read only through S5's quotation of five words.** It is
  a first-hand description of Liberty about 1840 by a man who was there, and it is the nearest thing
  to an eyewitness account of this town that anybody cites. **Jesse D. Lum's letter of 1891**, the
  source of the best construction description in the whole report, is likewise known only through S5.
- **The city of Liberty's own history page is unreadable.** `cityofliberty.org` returns **403 to
  WebFetch and a Cloudflare JavaScript interstitial to curl**, with or without a browser user-agent.
  Search engines index its text; the page itself was never served.
- **The General Land Office map database was not used**, on the brief's warning. GLO certainly holds
  the **Juan M. Smith league** and probably a Liberty town tract sheet.
- **The 1938 and 1945 Sanborn sheets of Liberty were listed but not opened.** Only the four sheets of
  May 1927 were read.
- **No antique-map dealer page carrying a manuscript plat of Liberty was found**, though the method
  says to look first. The dealer trade has Liberty *County* maps; the town plat is not in commerce.
- **No Almonte original was read**, only `HIST-TEX-011`'s existing summary (Liberty municipality
  1,000 in 1834).

| **Surveyor** | **José María Jesús Carbajal**, Madero's surveyor, arrested with him by Bradburn (S11) |
| **Site chosen** | **the league of land occupied by Juan M. Smith** — by **30 March 1831** (S5); the colonists chose **Smith's plantation over Moss Bluff by a narrow vote** (S3-Consistorial); TSHA dates the choice **1830** (S12) |
| **Titles granted** | **thirty-six, on 5 May 1831** (S1); Madero issued sixty in all, 2 March – 12 May 1831 (S11) |
| **First officers** | ayuntamiento elected **May 1831**; first alcalde **Hugh B. Johnston** (S1, S3-Constitucional) |
| **Name** | **Villa de la Santísima Trinidad de la Libertad**, shortened by the colonists to **Liberty** (S1, S4) |
| **Attacked by** | **John Davis Bradburn**, who tried to dissolve the ayuntamiento **10 December 1831** (S1) and arrested Madero and Carbajal (S2, S11) |
| **Legal frame** | the **1825 Law of Colonization** (S3-Consistorial) and, for the plan itself, **"the Law of the Indies"** (S4, quoted) — and the town lay **inside the ten-league coastal reserve**, which is what Bradburn objected to (S11) |

**The only real disagreement is the date the site was chosen** — 1830 in S12, 30 March 1831 in S5.
The likeliest reading, and it is a reading and not a source, is that **the colonists voted in 1830
and Madero confirmed and surveyed in March 1831**; the Casa Consistorial marker describes the vote
as a colonists' act taken under a right of local self-government that Madero agreed to, which fits
its happening before he arrived.

### 2.2 Forty-nine squares, five of them public — the plat as three sources give it

> *"platted Liberty town tract, 1831, with 49 squares in inner city"* — S3-Constitucional, quoted.

Three independent statements, none of them derived from the others as far as can be told:

| Source | Date | What it says |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Plaza Constitucional marker** (S3) | **1968** | **49 squares in the inner city; five set aside for public usage** |
| **National Register nomination** (S4), citing Fisher 1996 | **2002** | **"the 49-block ViUa de las Santisima Trinidad de la Libertad"**; "Following the Law of the Indies … several plazas, one of which was … the casa consistorial" |
| **Liberty County Historical Commission** (S5) | **2022** | Madero "designated several plazas or squares, according to Mexican law", including one of **120 varas (333.33 feet) on each side** for the Casa Consistorial |

**Forty-nine is seven by seven, and the 1927 Sanborn draws exactly that** (§2.3). **The phrase
"inner city" matters**: the Sanborn labels everything outside the forty-nine as **O.B.**, which its
own key expands — "O.B. stands for Outer Block". **The plan is a Mexican *traza* with an outer ring,
not a speculative American grid**, and Liberty is the only town in this series that is one.

### 2.3 The Sanborn key sheet — the plat, block by block

The May 1927 key sheet (S6, sheet 1) draws the historic core as a seven-by-seven square of blocks,
letters **ORIGINAL TOWN** across it in outsize type, and numbers the blocks in a **serpentine**:

| | **Fonda→Crockett** | **Crockett→Austin** | **Austin→Milam** | **Milam→Travis** | **Travis→Main** | **Main→Fannin** | **Fannin→San Jacinto** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Grand → Webster** | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
| **Webster → Cos** | 14 | 13 | 12 | 11 | 10 | 9 | 8 |
| **Cos → Trinity** | 15 | 16 | 17 | 18 | 19 | 20 | 21 |
| **Trinity → Liberty** | 27 | 26 | **25** *Iglesia* | **24** *Constitucional* | **— COURT HO.** *Consistorial* | **23** *Cárcel* | 22 |
| **Liberty → Jefferson** | 28 | 29 | 30 | 31 | 32 | **33** *Mercado* | 34 |
| **Jefferson → Santa Anna** | 41 | 40 | 39 | 38 | 37 | 36 | 35 |
| **Santa Anna → Washington** | 42 | 43 | 44 | 45 | 46 | 47 | 48 |

Rows run west→east, east→west, west→east, and so on. **Row 4 runs east→west — 22, 23, then straight
past the courthouse block to 24, 25, 26, 27.** The courthouse block carries no number at all, only
the words *COURT HO.*, while every block touching it (19, 20, 23, 24, 31, 32, 33) is numbered.
**A block with no lot number on an insurance surveyor's sheet is a block that was never subdivided
for sale**, and this one is the *casa consistorial* square of 1831.

Everything outside the rectangle is numbered **O.B.** — O.B. 2, 4, 7, 15, 16, 19, 20, 23, 31–48 were
read on the sheet — and the outer blocks are visibly **larger than the inner ones and irregular in
size at the edges**. Blocks 49–53, 66–73, 78–83, 99–103, 117–121, 131 and 207–209 belong to that
outer ground and to later additions.

**The five plaza markers land in five of these squares and no other** (§2.4). **That is the join
between a 1927 fire-insurance map and a 1831 Mexican plat, and it is the whole basis of this
report.**

### 2.4 The five plazas, marker by marker, block by block

Each 1968 marker's own coordinate was converted into the frame of §7 and compared with the block the
Sanborn numbering predicts. **All five fall inside, and four of them fall within twenty feet of the
block edge the marker text names.**

| Plaza | Sanborn block | Block in frame (ft) | Marker at | What the marker says it was |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Plaza Iglesia Parroquial** | **25** | x 806–1139, y 1195–1528 | **1087, 1506** | "set aside and designated for Catholic use in 1831 by J. Francisco Madero" |
| **Plaza Constitucional** | **24** | x 1195–1528, y 1195–1528 | **1539, 1314** | "this square has always been site of municipal buildings" |
| **Casa Consistorial** | **unnumbered** | x 1584–1917, y 1195–1528 | **1771, 1244** | "one of the five squares platted for public use in 1831"; the first courthouse |
| **Plaza de Cárcel y Casas de Corrección** | **23** | x 1972–2305, y 1195–1528 | **1994, 1374** | "Originally mapped 1831, according to laws of 'Coahuila and Texas'"; "Prison sentences were to be worked out here" |
| **Plaza de Mercado** | **33** | x 1972–2305, y 1584–1917 | **1994, 1559** | "Originally mapped in 1831 as a public plaza by the Mexican government" |

**Look at the shape that makes.** Four of the five plazas are the **four consecutive blocks of row 4**
— church, municipal square, courthouse, jail — running west to east across the middle of the town,
and the fifth, the market, sits **directly south of the jail square**. **The public ground of Liberty
is a bar of four blocks with a fifth hanging below its east end**, about **1,530 ft long by 333 ft
deep** with a 333 × 333 ft foot. No other town in this series has anything remotely like it.

**And the centre of the seven-by-seven grid is the Plaza Constitucional** — column 4 of 7, row 4 of 7
— **the municipal square, with the courthouse square beside it to the east.** `[That the centre
block is the Constitucional is arithmetic on the Sanborn grid, not a statement by anybody. §6, I-3.]`

**Two of the five never became what they were platted for.** The Plaza de Mercado "was sold early to
private individuals and did not become a public square" (S3-Mercado); the Plaza de Cárcel was in the
mercantile section by 1885 and "the plaza to the east became 'Jail Square'" (S3-Cárcel). **Both were
still public ground on the plat in 1835**, which is the year that matters here, and the Sanborn's
block numbers 23 and 33 record the later sale.

### 2.5 The grid on the ground, measured

Every named street centreline within about 700 m of the courthouse square was pulled from OSM (S7),
broken into segments, and binned by length-weighted bearing.

- **Bearing.** Two families and nothing else. The north–south streets run at a length-weighted mean
  of **−1.25°** (N 1.25° W) over 6.5 km of centreline; the east–west streets at **88.78°**
  (S 88.78° E) over 7.9 km. **The included angle is 90.03° — square to three hundredths of a
  degree.** This is the truest right angle measured anywhere in this series, and **the grid is laid
  to the cardinal points, one and a quarter degrees west of true north.**
- **Module, the north–south streets (which fix the block width east–west).** Positions in feet east
  of Milam Street: Crockett −767, Austin −383, **Milam 0**, Travis +403, Main +790, Fannin +1,183,
  San Jacinto +1,581. **Six consecutive intervals of 384, 383, 403, 387, 393 and 398 ft** — mean
  **391.3 ft**.
- **Against 140 varas.** 140 varas is **388.9 ft**. The measurement is **0.6 % over it** across six
  intervals. **With the street width of 55.5 ft that the Sanborn states (§2.6), the block is
  335.8 ft = 120.9 varas** — and S5 states the square was **120 varas on a side**. **Three numbers
  that were arrived at three different ways close to within one per cent.**
- **Module, the east–west streets.** The modern centrelines scatter badly: Grand −1,241, Webster
  −787, Cos −416, **Trinity 0**, Liberty (now Sam Houston St) +388, Jefferson +815, Santa Anna
  (now carrying US 90) +1,136, Washington +1,738. Intervals of 454, 371, 416, 388, 321 and 602 ft.
  **Trinity→Liberty is 388 ft, dead on the module; Grand→Webster and Santa Anna→Washington are not.**
- **The Sanborn settles it.** Measured in pixels off the key sheet and scaled by the north–south
  street positions above, the east–west streets sit at **105, 103, 107, 107, 106, 105 and 105 px**
  intervals — **the same module as the columns, uniform to 2 %**, and Grand→Washington measures
  2,745 ft against an ideal 2,722. **The plat is regular in both directions; it is the modern
  centrelines of Grand Avenue, Washington Street and the US 90 corridor that have drifted**, which
  is what a railroad, a highway and a century of widening do.
- **Overall: a module of 140 varas (388.9 ft) in both directions, and an inner town of
  2,722 × 2,722 ft — 980 varas square, a little over half a mile on a side.**

### 2.6 Street and block dimensions

**The Sanborn detail sheets annotate the street widths, and this is the only measured dimension in
the town's record that was not stated by a historian:**

| Thing | Value | In varas | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Street width** | **55.5 ft**, written in the roadway at Cos, Trinity, Liberty and Jefferson Avenues and at Travis and Fannin Streets | **19.98 — i.e. 20 varas** | **S6, sheet 2** |
| **Block** | **120 varas = 333.33 ft** on each side | 120 | **S5, stated** |
| Block, measured | **335.8 ft** (module 391.3 minus street 55.5) | 120.9 | this report, §2.5 |
| Block, measured on the Sanborn | **≈ 329 ft** (block/street = 5.93 in pixels) | 118.4 | this report, S6 sheet 2 |
| **Module** | **140 varas = 388.9 ft** | 140 | block + street |
| **Inner town** | **980 × 980 varas = 2,722 × 2,722 ft** | 980 | 7 × module |

**20 varas is the same street width Mina's surveyor was instructed to use, and the same width
Columbia's 1926 Sanborn records.** Three towns, three surveyors, three different legal systems, one
street width.

**One figure does not fit and it is worth naming.** The National Register nomination says "the
original perimeter dimensions of the square (about 295' x 295')" (S4). **That is 38 ft short of the
120 varas S5 states and of the 330-odd feet measured here.** The nomination says in the same sentence
that the square was modified in 1931 to take diagonal parking on all four sides; taking about 19 ft
off each side is exactly what a rank of diagonal parking needs. **The likeliest reading is that the
nomination gives the post-1931 green space and calls it original.** `[§6, I-5.]` **Use 120 varas.**

### 2.7 The lots — four to a block

On the Sanborn detail sheets (S6, sheet 2) **every block is divided by a cross into four quarters
numbered 1, 2, 3, 4** — block 19, block 20, block 23, block 32, block 33 all read this way, with 1
and 2 in the north half and 4 and 3 in the south. **The courthouse block carries no such division.**

**A quarter of a 120-vara block is 60 varas square — 166.7 ft each way, a shade under two-thirds of
an acre.** Four solares to the manzana is the standard Spanish–Mexican subdivision, and it is what
the surviving map shows. `[That the quartering is Madero's and not a later abstractor's is an
inference; nobody states a lot dimension for Liberty. §6, I-4.]`

**Forty-nine blocks less five public ones is forty-four, at four lots each: 176 town lots.** For
comparison, Columbia's plat held 160 and San Felipe's 582.

### 2.8 The street names — and four Spanish ones that survived

**The modern names of every street in the inner town are on the 1927 Sanborn**, and they are a
layered record rather than a single moment:

| Running north–south, west to east | Running east–west, north to south |
| --- | --- |
| **Fonda** · Crockett · Austin · Milam · Travis · Main · Fannin · San Jacinto | Grand · Webster · **Cos** · Trinity · Liberty · Jefferson · **Santa Anna** · Washington |

**Two of them are Mexican.** **Cos Avenue** and **Santa Anna Avenue** are named for Martín Perfecto
de Cos and Antonio López de Santa Anna — the two men Texans fought in 1835 and 1836. **No town in
the Republic of Texas named a street for either of them.** These are names that were on the ground
before the revolution and were never taken off; Cos himself was held a mile away at Mexican Hill in
1836 (S3-Mexican Hill). **Fonda** — *inn* — is the eighth name and is not an American street name
either. Crockett, Austin, Milam, Travis, Fannin, San Jacinto and Liberty are plainly post-1836 and
must have replaced something.

**And the National Register nomination preserves four of the things they replaced.** Its verbal
boundary description gives the courthouse square as bounded

> *"on the north by Trinity Ave (Hidalgo), on the east by Main St. (Socrates), on the south by
> Liberty Ave (Allende), and on the west by Trinity St. (Jimenez)"* — S4, quoted.

**Hidalgo, Allende and Jiménez are Miguel Hidalgo, Ignacio Allende and Mariano Jiménez — three of the
four leaders of the Mexican independence rising, executed together at Chihuahua in 1811.** A town
platted by a Coahuila y Texas land commissioner in 1831 naming its central streets for them is
exactly what one would expect, and it is the fourth independent sign that this plan is Mexican.

**Two cautions, both real.** First, the nomination gives **no date and no source** for the
parentheses; it does not say these are 1831 names, only that the streets have them. Second,
**"on the west by Trinity St." is wrong on its face** — Trinity Avenue is the street on the *north*,
and the street on the west of the courthouse square is **Travis Street**. The likeliest reading is
that **Travis Street was Jiménez**. **"Socrates" for Main Street fits no pattern**, is not a Mexican
independence name, and is reported here exactly as printed because it may be a transcription of
something else. `[§6, I-6.]`

## 3. Documented — the river, the roads and the ground

### 3.1 Where the town is, and what it is not on

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Official coordinate** | **30.05799, −94.79548** (GNIS, as `HIST-TEX-010` already carries it — and it is right, unlike Columbia's) |
| **Distance to the Trinity** | **1.28 miles** from the centre of the courthouse square to the nearest point of the modern channel, **due west** (S7) |
| **Distance to old Atascosito** | **3.20 miles**, bearing north-east, to the 1936 marker (S3-Atascosito) |
| **Distance to the Atascosito crossing** | **about three miles north** (S1); or **4½ miles west of the Atascosito marker** (S3-Atascosito), which computes to about 3.1 miles north-west of the square |
| **Champ d'Asile (1818)** | marked at the US 90 bridge, **0.86 miles west** of the town's west edge (S3-French Settlers) |
| **Elevation** | **30–33 ft** across the whole plat (S8); the 1927 Sanborn's own note reads "Elevation: level" (S6) |

**The single most important geographical fact about Liberty is that it is not on the river.** The
courthouse square is a mile and a quarter east of the Trinity, on flat ground, with the whole of that
mile and a quarter also flat. A student walked into the Plaza Constitucional in October 1835 **cannot
see the Trinity and cannot hear it.**

### 3.2 The Trinity, and where the landing must have been

The modern channel runs roughly north–south past the town at a distance of 1.28–1.45 miles. Its
nearest approach to the plat is at frame **(−5,007, 2,029)** — that is, **5,000 ft west of Fonda
Street, on the latitude of the middle of the town** (§7.4 gives the course).

**What is documented about the river here:**

- **Liberty "once stood at the head of navigation on the Trinity River"** (S1). Everything above it
  was for rafts and small craft.
- **There was a place called Liberty Landing**, distinct from the town: the English firm **Jones and
  Company** ran a beef-packing business there in the early 1840s, and James Taylor White furnished
  most of the beef (S1). Barrels went to Galveston and on to the West Indies (S3-Trinity).
  **Nothing read gives Liberty Landing a position.**
- **"A trading post and warehouse served local residents"** (S1, quoted) — undated, and in a sentence
  otherwise about the 1840s.
- **The town had ferry service across the Trinity** (S1) — undated, and in the same late sentence.
- **There were no steamboats on the Trinity in 1835.** The *Branch T. Archer* ascended 350 miles in
  **May 1838** and is called one of the first attempts (S13); the 1969 Trinity River marker says
  flatly that in 1838 it "became the first to navigate the Trinity" (S3-Trinity). Packet boats begin
  "about 1836" in one TSHA account (S13). **What came up to Liberty in 1835 was schooner and
  keelboat and lighter traffic from Galveston Bay, not a steamer.**
- Madero himself **petitioned the legislature of Coahuila y Tejas for exclusive navigation rights on
  the Trinity in 1831, and the revolution came before he could use them** (S3-Trinity). **A land
  commissioner who plats a town a mile from a river and then asks for that river's navigation
  monopoly was thinking about a port.**

### 3.3 The ground — a terrace with its southern lip on the town's south line

Ninety-seven elevation points were taken from USGS 3DEP (S8) on three east–west transects and one
north–south transect through the plat. The pattern is clean and it is not what the Sanborn's word
"level" suggests:

- **The town stands on a terrace at 30–33 ft**, flat to within three feet across the whole
  2,722-ft square and for 1,300 ft north of it.
- **Due west the terrace holds all the way to the river** — 33, 30, 31, 29, 33, 30, 30, 30, 29, 27 ft
  at 200-m steps — and then **drops to about 5–10 ft at the channel. The east bank of the Trinity
  opposite Liberty is a cut bank of about twenty feet.**
- **South of the town the terrace ends.** On the north–south transect the ground reads 32.1 ft at the
  latitude of Trinity Avenue, **23.8 ft 650 ft south of Washington Avenue's position, and 14.3 ft
  1,300 ft south of it.** The bottom below stays at 14–19 ft for a mile.
- **North-west of the town it ends sooner.** On the transect through the latitude of Grand Avenue the
  ground falls to 17–21 ft from about 550 ft west of Fonda Street — **the bottom of Big Bayou**,
  which OSM puts within 500 ft of the plat's north-west corner (§7.4).

**So the forty-nine blocks sit on a tongue of high ground about 30 ft above the sea, with the Trinity
bottom below them to the south and the Big Bayou bottom below them to the north-west, and the terrace
running unbroken west to the river bank.** The south line of the plat — Washington Avenue — is very
nearly **on the lip**. `[That Carbajal ran the south line along the terrace edge on purpose is an
inference. §6, I-7.]`

**There is no bluff at Liberty in the sense the brief means.** The town is not perched above the
water. It is on a flat terrace a mile and a quarter back from a river that has cut twenty feet below
it. Drawing a river-bluff town here would be wrong; drawing a flat town with a twenty-foot cut bank
a mile off is right.

### 3.4 The roads — and two 1936 markers that contradict each other

**Two roads are documented through or past this town, and they cross at right angles.**

**The Atascosito Road**, east–west, which the game already draws (`HIST-TEX-008`). S9 gives its
course from Refugio and Goliad by the Atascosito Crossing on the Colorado, to the Brazos near San
Felipe, across northern Harris County to the Trinity; its eastern continuation is the Opelousas or
La Bahía road into Louisiana. **Where it crossed the Trinity is stated three ways and they do not
agree:**

| Source | Where the Atascosito Road crossed the Trinity |
| --- | --- |
| **TSHA, *Liberty, TX*** (S1) | **"three miles to the north"** of Liberty |
| **1936 Atascosito marker** (S3) | **"Four and one-half miles west of here"** — i.e. about 3.1 miles NW of the square |
| **1936 Liberty County marker** (S3) | at the crossing "the Spaniards made the first settlement, Atascosito, about 1758" — putting **settlement and crossing at the same place**, 3.2 miles NE of the square |
| **TSHA, *Liberty County*** (S2) | Spanish maps of 1757 show the road **"crossed the Trinity near the present site of Liberty"** |

**The first two agree to within a quarter mile and should be used**: the crossing was about
**three miles north or north-west of the town**, and the Spanish settlement of Atascosito stood
about three miles *east* of the crossing, on the road. The 1936 county marker conflates the two and
S2's "near the present site of Liberty" is loose. **The consequence for the game is concrete: the
road the game already draws did not go through the town of Liberty.** A family at Liberty reached it
by going north.

**The Liberty-Nacogdoches Road**, north–south, which the game does not draw. S10 traces it from
Nacogdoches by the Neches crossing at Fort Terán, down the east side of the Trinity past Colita's
Village and a Coushatta village in the Edward Tanner survey, and says it **"went through what is now
the site of Liberty, Texas, and continued south to El Orcoquisac"** (S10, quoted). **This is the road
that actually entered the town**, and it is a Spanish military road of 1805 or earlier that the
Alabamas and Coushattas used to go to Nacogdoches to trade. **A north–south road through the middle
of the plat is a real feature that nothing in the repository yet carries.**

**No source read says which street either road became.** `[§5.8.]`

### 3.5 The country around it, and what the timber was

- **The northern fourth of Liberty County is the Big Thicket**, "a once-impenetrable wilderness
  wooded with pine, oak, ash, hickory, cypress, and walnut" (S2). The town is south of it, in the
  plantation country.
- **The Trinity bottom supplied cypress and the uplands pine**, and the buildings prove it: the 1843
  courthouse was "studded with peeled pine poles and weatherboarded with split cypress boards"
  (S5, quoted from Jesse D. Lum's 1891 letter), and the third was of cypress and pine lumber on
  **cypress blocks two feet above the ground** (S5).
- **Liberty shipped cotton, hides, Indian corn, cattle and lumber down the Trinity** (S2); it was "a
  shipping point for plantations along the Trinity, for lumber operations, and for a variety of
  shipments from farmers" (S1).
- **Two sawmills at Liberty are a 1850s fact, not an 1830s one** (S1). **In 1835 there was no mill
  here**, and everything built was hewn, riven or split on the spot, or came up the river sawn.
- **The 1927 Sanborn's outer blocks are open ground with scattered single buildings**; the 1900
  description — "roughly seventy houses, many of which stood alone on their respective city blocks",
  with livestock roaming the streets legally (S1) — is the best picture of how empty this grid stayed
  for seventy years.

## 4. Documented and strongly supported — the buildings

### 4.1 The only 1835 building with a position and a size

**The first courthouse — the *casa consistorial*.**

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **What** | "a small hewn log building **22 feet square**" (S3-Consistorial); "hewed log building" in David Carlton Hardee's own words (S5) |
| **Where** | **on the Casa Consistorial square** — the unnumbered block of the 1927 Sanborn, Travis to Main, Trinity to Liberty. The 1956 Liberty Bicentennial marker stands at its **north-east corner**, "next to the artillery cannon" (S3-First Court House), at frame **(1881, 1242)** |

**Twenty-two feet square of hewn logs, standing alone in the middle of a 333-foot square, is the
government of a municipality that ran from the San Jacinto to the Sabine and from Nacogdoches to the
Gulf.** That is the single most drawable fact in this report.

### 4.4 What they were built of

**This town's construction record is unusually good for a place with no surviving buildings**, and
all of it comes through S5:

- **Hewn logs** for the 1831 courthouse — squared, not round.
- **"studded with peeled pine poles and weatherboarded with split cypress boards"** (S5, quoted from
  Lum, 1891) for the 1843 courthouse. **Peeled pine poles as studs and riven cypress as siding is a
  complete description of frontier Trinity-bottom carpentry**, and it is the best single sentence of
  building description anywhere in this series.
- **Cypress and pine lumber** for the 1849 courthouse, **set on cypress blocks two feet above the
  ground** — a bottomland detail, and the reason nothing here has a cellar.
- **Brick** only from 1857, and the brick came from the sale of town land.
- **No sawmill at Liberty until the 1850s** (S1). In 1835 the pine was felled, the cypress riven and
  the logs hewn within a few miles.

`[The 1843 and 1849 details are eight and fourteen years after 1835. They are used here as evidence
of local practice and materials, not as the appearance of any 1835 building. §6, I-8.]`

### 4.5 How big the place was in 1835

Every number the record gives, in order:

| Date | Figure | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **5 May 1831** | **thirty-six land titles granted**, forming the municipality | S1 — and this is `HIST-TEX-013`'s figure, confirmed |
| 1831 | Madero granted **sixty titles in all**, most along the lower Trinity | S11 |
| **1834** | **Liberty municipality 1,000** (Almonte, by department) | `HIST-TEX-011` |
| **1835** | municipality "more than a thousand" / "exceeded 1,000" | S2, `HIST-TEX-013` |
| **1840** | **"only ten or twelve houses stood at the townsite"** | **S1, quoted** |
| 1845 | town population **200** | S1 |
| 1858 | **651**, of whom 189 Black | S1 |
| 1880 | 497; four churches, three schools, a hotel | S1 |
| **1900** | **"roughly seventy houses, many of which stood alone on their respective city blocks"**; livestock roamed the streets legally | **S1, quoted** |
| 1927 | **1,500** | S6 |

**The reading.** Thirty-six titles are titles to *land in the municipality*, not town lots — the
grantees lived on their leagues along the Trinity, not in the town. Almonte's thousand is the
municipality, which reached the Sabine. **The town itself had ten or twelve houses in 1840, nine
years after it was platted, and about seventy in 1900.** In 1835 — four years after the plat, four
years after Bradburn tried to abolish the ayuntamiento, with the settlers' titles contested — **there
is no reason to think there were more than ten houses, and there may have been fewer.**

**A plat of 176 saleable lots with under a dozen houses on it is about ninety-five per cent empty.**
`[§6, I-9.]` **Liberty drawn as a filled grid would be the worst error available in this town**, and
the 1900 sentence about houses standing alone on their own blocks is the picture to hold on to.

### 4.6 What happened here in 1835–36, and what the game already carries

- **Liberty was represented at the Consultation of 1835** (S1). **James B. Woods**, elected alcalde of
  the Liberty District in 1834, represented it, and in March 1836 was one of five Liberty delegates
  at Washington-on-the-Brazos and a signer of the Declaration (S3-Woods). The 1936 Liberty County
  marker names the Consultation delegates as **Henry Millard, George M. Patrick, A. B. Hardin and
  James B. Woods** (S3-Liberty County).
- **Andrew Briscoe's Liberty Volunteers**, organised 1835, fought at Concepción and in the siege of
  Béxar (S1, S2). `HIST-TEX-018` already records the negative that matters: TSHA places their
  organisation "in the Harrisburg area", first attests them at Concepción on **28 October**, and the
  earliest roster is **21 November** — so **no Liberty company appears in any October 1835 letter**,
  and `docs/COLONIES.md` §5.5's Liberty volunteer who joins on the road is a gameplay construction.
  **Nothing found here changes that.** One name can be added: **Benjamin Franklin Hardin**, named
  **surveyor of the Atascosito District in 1834**, was a Liberty Volunteer at Concepción, at the
  siege of Béxar and at San Jacinto (S3-Seven Pines).
- **William M. Logan**, who moved to Liberty in 1832, organised and commanded the 3rd Company, 2nd
  Regiment Texas Volunteers at San Jacinto and was the first sheriff of Liberty County (S3-Logan).
- **In February 1836 one of Travis's letters from the Alamo was carried to Liberty by Joseph
  Dunman** (S1). `HIST-TEX-013` already has this.
- **In August 1836 about sixty Mexican prisoners from San Jacinto, including General Cos and Lt Col
  Pedro Delgado, were brought by schooner to Anahuac and on to William Hardin's place at Liberty**,
  where they stayed until 25 April 1837 (S3-Mexican Hill). **Delgado wrote an account of his time in
  Liberty** — an eyewitness description of this town, sixteen months after the year the game plays,
  and it was not read (§1b).

## 5. Unknown — what a layout would need and no source read supplies

Everything here would have to be invented and registered as fiction.

1. **The 1831 plat sheet itself.** Its drawn form, its scale, whether the outer blocks were on the
   same sheet, whether lots were numbered, and whether the block numbering that the 1927 Sanborn uses
   is Madero's or an abstractor's. §2.3 establishes that the grid and the five plazas survive; **it
   does not establish that a single number or word on the 1927 sheet is an 1831 one.**
2. **Any lot dimension, stated.** §2.7 is a division of a stated block by an observed quartering.
3. **The 1831 names of the other twelve streets.** Four alternative names survive in one sentence of
   one document (§2.8) and one of those four is attached to the wrong side of the square. **No
   street in Liberty is named in any 1830s document that was read.**
4. **How far the outer blocks ran, and what they were for.** The Sanborn numbers O.B. blocks past 48
   and they are visibly larger and irregular, but **no source read says how many there were, how big
   they were, whether they were farm lots or *suertes*, or whether the town tract was the whole of
   Smith's league.**
5. **Where Liberty Landing was.** The town's whole reason for existing at this spot, and there is no
   position for it in anything read. **This is the single most valuable missing location.**
6. **Where the ferry crossed**, and whether there was one at the town at all in 1835 as against at
   the Atascosito crossing three miles north.
7. **Whether there was a wharf, a warehouse, or a road cut down the bank**, and what the bank looked
   like where boats tied up.
8. **Which street the Liberty-Nacogdoches Road became inside the town**, and where the Atascosito
   Road ran in relation to the plat.
9. **Where any store, tavern, blacksmith or dwelling stood in 1835.** Not one has a position. The
   whole built town of 1835 outside the courthouse is undocumented.
10. **Any dimension of any Liberty building except the three courthouses** — 22 ft square, 24 × 28 ft,
    32 × 40 ft.
11. **The number of houses in 1835.** §4.5 reasons back from a 1840 count; **nobody counts Liberty's
    houses in the 1830s.**
12. **Who held which lot.** There is no lot-sale record, no title list. The county's records burned
    twice, and the surviving series begin in 1874 (S5, S14).
13. **What the *casa consistorial* square looked like around the log courthouse** — fenced, planted,
    bare, a well, a whipping post, a pillory. Nothing.
14. **Whether the jail square ever held a jail before 1885**, and where prisoners were kept.
15. **The 1835 line of the Trinity's east bank.** The channel has meanders and oxbows here (Twin Lake
    and Gum Slough lie between town and river), and **nothing read establishes whether the modern
    channel is the 1835 channel.** It was not testable in the time available.
16. **What Champ d'Asile left on the ground**, if anything, thirteen years before the town was
    platted and a mile west of it.

## 6. Inferred — things that follow but that nobody states

Each is an inference of this document and must be labelled as one if it is used.

- **I-1. The modern Liberty street grid is Carbajal's 1831 grid.** The load-bearing inference.
  Supporting it: **five 1968 markers whose own coordinates fall in the five blocks the 1927 Sanborn
  numbering predicts** (§2.4); the 1927 sheet's own label **ORIGINAL TOWN** over exactly a
  seven-by-seven rectangle, with **O.B.** outside it; a **stated** block count of 49 from two
  independent later sources; a **stated** block dimension of 120 varas that the measured grid
  reproduces to within one per cent; **Cos Avenue and Santa Anna Avenue**, names no Republic-era
  Texan would have chosen; and a grid square to 0.03°. **Nobody says the town was never re-platted.**
  If this is wrong, §7 is wrong.
- **I-2. The module is 140 varas: a 120-vara block between 20-vara streets.** The block is stated
  (S5); the street width is annotated on the Sanborn (S6); the module is measured at 391.3 ft against
  an arithmetic 388.9. **Nobody states the module and nobody states that the street width of 1927 is
  the street width of 1831.**
- **I-3. The Plaza Constitucional is the geometric centre of the town.** Column 4 of 7, row 4 of 7.
  This is arithmetic on the Sanborn grid, and it is the kind of thing a Laws-of-the-Indies plan does
  on purpose, but **nobody says it.**
- **I-4. A Liberty lot is 60 varas square, four to a block.** The quartering is drawn on the 1927
  Sanborn; the dimension is the stated block divided. **Nobody states a lot dimension**, and the
  quartering may be a later abstractor's convenience.
- **I-5. The courthouse square was a full 120-vara block and the National Register's "about
  295' x 295'" is the square after 1931.** §2.6. **The nomination says the opposite in as many
  words**, and it is the only source that gives a figure in feet.
- **I-6. Travis Street was Jiménez.** From the nomination's own geography, since the street it calls
  "Trinity St." on the west of the square is Travis. **The nomination says Trinity.**
- **I-7. The south line of the plat was run along the lip of the terrace.** Washington Avenue's
  position and the 3DEP break coincide to within a few hundred feet (§3.3). **Nobody says the
  surveyor used the ground.**
- **I-8. Liberty's 1835 buildings were hewn log, with riven cypress and pine where anything was
  finished.** From the 1831 courthouse's hewn logs and the 1843 and 1849 courthouses' peeled pine
  studs, split cypress weatherboard and cypress blocks (§4.4) — **evidence from eight and fourteen
  years later.**
- **I-9. Liberty in October 1835 held at most about ten buildings on a plat of 176 lots — about
  ninety-five per cent empty, and emptier than Columbia.** Reasoned in §4.5 from the 1840 count
  backwards and the 1900 count forwards. **Nobody counts Liberty's houses in the 1830s.**
- **I-10. The landing lay due west of the town, at the foot of the twenty-foot cut bank, with a road
  running west out of the plat to reach it.** The terrace runs unbroken west from the town to the
  bank (§3.3); the modern streets on that line are called **Navigation Street** and **Port Drive**;
  and the only other candidate direction, north to the Atascosito crossing, is three miles off and is
  a *road* crossing, not a landing. **Nobody read gives Liberty Landing a position**, and the modern
  street names may be twentieth-century, from the 1940 inland port.
- **I-11. The town tract was Smith's league and the outer blocks fill it.** S5 says Madero selected
  "the league of land occupied by Juan M. Smith" for the town. A Texas league is 5,000 varas square;
  the inner town is 980 varas square; the Sanborn draws outer blocks out to a mile from the
  courthouse. **Nobody says the town tract and the league are the same thing.**
- **I-12. Nothing stood on four of the five plazas in 1835.** An argument from silence across every
  source read, supported by the fact that each of the four has a *later* first building that its own
  marker names. **Treat as probable, not proved.**

### 7.7 What must be drawn *around* the town

- **A flat, level townsite.** No terrace visible inside the plat, no slope on any street. The
  Sanborn's own word is "level" and the lidar agrees to within three feet across half a mile.
- **The southern edge.** Immediately south of Washington Avenue the ground falls twelve to eighteen
  feet into bottomland that stays wet. This is the one place in the town where there is any relief
  at all, and it is at the plat's own boundary.
- **Bottomland timber to the south and north-west**, and open pine-and-oak upland elsewhere: pine,
  oak, ash, hickory, **cypress**, walnut (S2). **Cypress in the sloughs is the signature tree of this
  town** — its courthouses were sided and blocked with it, and its later church bell came off a
  steamboat that sank in the river.
- **A mile and a quarter of nothing between the town and the river**, then a twenty-foot cut bank.
- **Big Bayou** running past the north-west corner, five hundred feet out.
- **Stock in the streets and single houses standing alone on their own blocks.** This is documented
  for 1900 and is a fortiori true of 1835.
- **No steamboat.** In 1835 the Trinity carried schooner, lighter and keelboat traffic up from
  Galveston Bay. The first steamer was 1838.
- **No church, no printing office, no school, no post office, no jail, no market house.** All of them
  postdate 1835 here (§4.3).

2. **An empty public square.** Four of Liberty's five plazas were reserved ground with nothing on
   them. The library has plazas with buildings around them (Béxar); it needs a **blank block inside
   a grid**, distinguishable from an unsold lot — grass, a few trees, a fence or no fence.
3. **A single house standing alone on a 333-foot block**, with a fenced garden patch and the rest of
   the block open. This is the characteristic view of Liberty and of every under-built Texas town in
   this series, and it is the opposite of a street frontage.
4. **Riven-cypress weatherboard and peeled-pine framing**, as a wall treatment distinct from sawn
   clapboard. Documented for this town by 1843 (S5).
5. **A building set on blocks two feet above the ground** — a bottomland detail, documented here,
   and probably right for Columbia and Matagorda too.
6. **A cypress slough** — standing water, knees, hanging moss — for the ground south and north-west
   of the town.
7. **A twenty-foot earth cut bank with no structure on it**, seen from the top, for the Trinity west
   of town. Distinct from Columbia's request, which was a bank *with* timbered steps.
8. **A keelboat or river lighter under pole and oar** — not a steamboat, not a sea-going schooner.
   What reached Liberty in 1835.

## 8. Verdict

**Can Liberty be drawn honestly? Yes — and it is the most *legible* plan in the series, on a
different axis again from Columbia's and Matagorda's.**

Matagorda's plat survives and no building in it has a position. Columbia's plat is gone and was
recovered from a 1926 insurance map and a chain of title. **Liberty's plat is gone, the county's
records burned twice, and the plan is nevertheless recoverable in more detail than any of them** —
because three things happened to line up: a stated block count (49), a stated block dimension
(120 varas), and a 1968 marker campaign that went and put five stones on five specific blocks with
their Spanish names on them. **Add a 1927 Sanborn that labels the whole rectangle ORIGINAL TOWN and
leaves the courthouse block unnumbered, and the 1831 plan can be reconstructed square by square.**

**And the shape it makes is worth having.** This is the only town the game plays that was laid out
as a Mexican *villa* under the Law of the Indies: forty-nine blocks, seven by seven, square to the
compass, half a mile on a side, with an outer ring of larger blocks around it, and **a bar of four
public squares straight across the middle — church, municipal square, courthouse, jail — with the
market hanging below the east end.** A student walking east along Trinity Avenue in 1835 walks past
four consecutive public blocks in four hundred paces. **No other settlement in this series has a
civic plan at all.**

**What is missing is the town.** The plan is magnificent and there was almost nothing standing in it.
Four of the five plazas were empty ground. The fifth held a hewn log building twenty-two feet square.
Ten or twelve houses stood on a plat cut for a hundred and seventy-six lots, and seventy years later
there were still only seventy, many of them alone on their own blocks. **There is no church, no
jail, no market house, no school, no post office, no printing office and no Sam Houston in Liberty in
1835** — every one of those is 1836 or later. **Not one store, tavern, dwelling or warehouse in 1835
Liberty has a position in anything read.**

**And the river is a mile and a quarter away.** The thing everyone knows about Liberty — the head of
navigation on the Trinity — describes a landing nobody can locate, reached across a mile and a
quarter of flat terrace, below a twenty-foot cut bank, by boats that were not steamboats yet.

**The five things most worth doing before drawing it**, in order of value:

1. **Read Fisher, *The Seven Courthouses of Liberty County (1831–1996)*, typescript at the Sam
   Houston Regional Library.** It is the National Register nomination's authority for the 49-block
   plat, it is what Linda Jamison's 2022 account is built on, and it is three miles from the
   townsite. **It very probably contains the plat's dimensions, the plaza list and the source for
   all of it**, and it would turn half of §6 into §2.
2. **Get into the Portal to Texas History** — which is also the Sam Houston Regional Library's own
   digital home, and which holds Liberty County's newspapers and manuscript collections. **Six
   reports in a row have been stopped by this one site.** For Liberty it is worse than usual,
   because the archive it fronts is the town's own.
3. **Ask the Sam Houston Regional Library and Research Center for three things**: any copy of the
   1831 town tract plat or Madero's survey; Miriam Partlow's working papers; and **Pedro Delgado's
   account of his months in Liberty in 1836–37**, which is the only eyewitness description of this
   town anywhere near the right decade. **Nobody was contacted.**
4. **Find Prewitt, *Channel to Liberty* (1986).** It is the archaeology of the exact reach of the
   Trinity this town used, and it may be the only thing that can put Liberty Landing on a map — the
   one unknown in §5 that changes the drawing most.
5. **Fix `HIST-TEX-013`.** Two words: the "ten or twelve houses" is a **1840** figure, not an 1837
   one (S1). While it is open, the claim would also be improved by recording that **the town stands
   about three miles south-west of old Atascosito and about a mile and a quarter east of the
   Trinity**, since `docs/COLONIES.md` seats a family at Liberty in every class and `FIC-GONZ-027`
   already invents a Trinity crossing "at Liberty" that the Atascosito Road did not use.
