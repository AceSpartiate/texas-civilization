# Refugio in 1835: a ruined stone church on a bluff, a new Mexican plat beside it, and a town of huts

Research only. Nothing here is built, and no claim below has a `HIST-` or `FIC-` ID yet. IDs are
assigned when something is drawn. Read alongside `docs/town-research/goliad.md` (the Refugio road
ran into La Bahía from the south-east, and Refugio's men were in the assault of 9–10 October 1835)
and `victoria.md` (the other road to Refugio ran from Victoria by Carlos Rancho). The layout
vocabulary in §9 is that of `public/bexar-layout.js` (feet, a local plane, +y south, `plazas`,
`river`, `roads`, `building(id, sprite, x, y, heightFeet)`), with the mission church in the
`room`/`wall` terms of `public/alamo-layout.js`. `public/gonzales-art.js` (`GONZALES_BUILDINGS`) was
read. There is no `public/gonzales-layout.js`, as the brief itself said.

Refugio is **not a start**. `docs/COLONIES.md` §5.1 keeps it on the map as a place because
Almonte's 1834 report gives it no population. This report is for drawing it as a place, for knowing
what trades it had, and for checking its coordinate.

Researched 2026-09-16. Every source in §1 was opened and read on that date, except where §1b says
it was not. Every measurement in §2, §3 and §9 was computed here on that date from the sources named.

Labels used throughout: **DOCUMENTED** (a source read here says it), **INFERRED** (follows from
documented things; numbered `I-n` in §8), **INVENTED** (nothing supports it; needed only to draw).

A note on quotation. Hobart Huson's *Refugio* (1953) and the TSHA *Handbook* are in copyright and
are paraphrased here, not quoted. Short quotations are taken only from works published before 1929
(Foote 1841, Beales's journal as printed by Kennedy in 1841, Duval) and from the 1835–36 newspapers.

## The one-line answer

**In October 1835 Refugio was two things side by side on the north bank of the Mission River. One
was the old mission church: a large stone building with a weathered wooden roof, a cupola, glass
windows and a walled graveyard, standing near the edge of a bluff about 40 feet above the river.
The other was a brand-new Mexican town, surveyed only fourteen months earlier, in the summer of
1834. It had a square plaza on the highest ground two blocks north-east of the church, and streets
83⅓ feet wide laid out square to the compass. On that grid stood perhaps two dozen huts of adobe,
upright poles or brush. Only one house in town was built of sawn lumber. About 170 lot grants had
been handed out in the summer of 1834, to Irish colonists and to Mexican families in about equal
measure. The mission church held the colony's business offices. At the start of October General
Cos's army camped on the river on its way from El Copano to Goliad, and used the mission as its
headquarters. Ten days later Refugio's militia rode to join the attack on Goliad.**

What carries the report:

1. **The 1834 plat survives on the ground, and it measures exactly.** Huson (S2) read the
   surveyor's plat of 1 August 1834 in the county deed records. It shows blocks of 120 varas
   (333⅓ ft), each split into four lots of 60 varas, streets of 30 varas (83⅓ ft), and a
   constitutional plaza in the exact centre of a 7 × 7 block town. **Measured here on
   OpenStreetMap, today's east–west streets are 416 ft apart, centre to centre (ten gaps, 406–425
   ft).** That is 150 varas, one block plus one street, to within a foot. The 1935 Sanborn map
   (S9) prints "83⅓" as the street width and labels the plaza block PARK. **The plaza is King
   Park**, between Commerce, Osage, Purisima and Empresario streets, and it is still the highest
   ground in town at 52.8 ft (§3).
2. **The grid is turned 9.6° clockwise from true north, and in August 1834 the compass there
   pointed 9.3° east of true north** (NOAA's historical field model, S10). The 1827 instructions
   told commissioners to run the main streets north–south and east–west (S2). **So the surveyor
   laid the town out by compass** (`I-1`), and anyone drawing it square to true north will be ten
   degrees wrong.
3. **The mission church stood on block 11, two blocks west and two blocks south of the plaza**, in
   the lot now occupied by Our Lady of Refuge Church (1901). The church, the 1936 state monument
   and the 1994 marker all stand there (S5, S6, S9). **The church site is 1,205 ft south-west of the
   plaza's centre, at 40–42 ft elevation.** From there the ground holds level toward the river and
   then drops about 35 ft in the last 100–150 ft to water at 3 ft (§2.2).
4. **What the church looked like is known only in outline, from accounts of the March 1836
   fight.** Every account read describes one stone building. Nobody who was there in 1835 or 1836
   mentions stone walls round the mission or any other stone building (S2). Ehrenberg saw high,
   decayed walls and a weather-worn roof in January 1836 (via S2). Foote (1841, S4) calls it "the
   ruins of a stone church". On one side was a walled churchyard about fifty yards long, with tombs
   in it. From the far end of the churchyard the ground sloped away. Later accounts add a cupola,
   three doors, glass windows on the north side, and a 16-ft arched gate at the south end of the
   churchyard wall (S2).
5. **The houses are described four times between 1833 and 1836, and the descriptions grow with the
   colony.** In December 1833 Beales saw "five or six miserable huts" of Irish families (S3). In
   1834 a colonist remembered only a few jacals; Quirk had the one lumber house and the rest were
   adobe or palings (S2). In February 1836 Duval counted "about two dozen adobe huts". In January
   1836 Ehrenberg saw little houses scattered about the church (both via S2). **Power's own town
   house was a two-room picket house with a thatched roof**, and it had big paling cattle pens on
   the river (S2).
6. **Refugio's trades in 1834–36 were few, and they are named** (§6). Three merchants appear:
   Martin Power, Henry Foley and John Dunn. The first two stores were plundered in February 1836.
   There was one gunsmith, Antonio Sayle, called "Silers". Add the colony surveyors, a postmaster
   from the winter of 1835–36, the carters who hauled for the army, and the cattle. **No
   blacksmith, tavern, doctor, mill, priest or school is documented in the town for 1834–36.**
7. **`HIST-TEX-010`'s coordinate for Refugio is not the 1835 town.** It lies **4,110 ft (0.78 mi)
   north of the mission church site, bearing 11°**, and **3,213 ft (0.61 mi) due north of the
   plaza**. That puts it on Commons Street, about 1,000 ft beyond the north edge of the town as it
   was platted in 1834 (§2.1). Unlike Goliad, it is on the same bank of the same river.

### Corrections to the brief

- **"Founded 1793 and moved to its final site on the Mission River by about 1795": right on the
  standard account, and disputed.**
  - TSHA (S1c): founded 4 February 1793 on Goff Bayou in present Calhoun County. Moved in June 1794
    to Rancho de los Mosquitos. Moved in January 1795 to the site of present Refugio, where it was
    dedicated on 10 January 1795.
  - The 1936 state monument gives 31 January 1793 "at the junction of the Guadalupe and San
    Antonio rivers" (S6). The 2018 National Register nomination (S5) says both details are wrong,
    and so is the missionary's name.
  - The 1994 church marker and the 1936 county marker say "1791" (S6). That is the year Silva and
    Garza toured the coast looking for a site (S1c), not the founding.
  - Huson (S2) records a dissent he half believed. George C. Martin argued that the 1795 site was
    near El Copano, at the head of Mission Bay, and that the move to the town site came later,
    before 1807. On that view the stone church was not begun until after 1800: a coin of 1800 was
    found in its cornerstone, and a report of 1805 has Father Gaitán building a church with a
    French carpenter, a blacksmith and a mason. Father Oberste, who wrote the mission's history,
    rejected Martin's theory. **Unresolved. For 1835 it does not matter: the church standing in 1835
    was on the town site.**
- **"Secularized around 1830": right, in two steps.** The governor ordered it secularised on 10 July
  1824, and its goods were carted to La Bahía. Attempts to revive it failed between 1825 and 1829.
  The order was carried out under a decree of 7 January 1830, when fewer than two dozen Karankawa
  and Coco Indians remained (S1c). The last services for the Goliad and Refugio missions were held
  at La Bahía on 7 February 1830 (S2).
- **"Refugio was the seat of the McMullen–McGloin/Power–Hewetson region": wrong for McMullen and
  McGloin.**
  - McMullen and McGloin's Irish colonists sheltered at the empty mission from December 1829. Most
    moved west to the Nueces at the end of 1830, and laid out San Patricio in October 1831 (S1i, S2).
  - San Patricio was a separate municipality. Almonte counted it at 600 in 1834, and he gives no
    figure for Refugio (S12).
  - **Refugio was the centre of Power and Hewetson's colony only.** Its municipality was set up in
    1834 (S1a, S1e).
- **"Power and Hewetson's colony settled Irish families there from 1834": the main body, yes, but
  Irish families were there earlier.**
  - Scott, Brown and Ryan families came before 1829, and Power built a house in the town in
    1830–31 (S2).
  - Beales found five or six Irish families living in huts at the mission in December 1833 (S3).
  - The main body sailed from Ireland in 1834. Cholera killed many in New Orleans and on the coast.
    Power landed the rest at Aransas on 23 May 1834 (S1e).
  - **A roughly equal number of Mexican colonists** joined them. Many had come from Coahuila, and
    many had already lived on ranchos round the mission (S1b, S1e). The brief leaves them out.
- **"The town of Refugio was laid out around the mission": not around it. Beside it.**
  - Commissioner José Jesús Vidaurri chose a high, level piece of ground for the plaza and had a
    large rock set at its centre. James Bray surveyed from there (S2).
  - **The mission church ended up inside the grid, two blocks south-west of the plaza** (§3.3).
  - The site had been named in 1831, when Power and Hewetson acquired the mission and its lands.
    TSHA says the villa was "officially established" in 1831 (S1a, S1b). Huson shows it was actually
    surveyed, and its lots granted, in July and August 1834 (S2).
- **"In late 1835 Refugio was small, with the old mission church and some houses": right.** The
  "some houses" were huts, and one lumber house (§5.1). **Add what happened there in the game's
  month** (§5.3):
  - Cos's army camped on the river at the start of October. Its officers were billeted in the town,
    and the mission served as headquarters and baggage store.
  - The colony's business offices were in the mission.
  - The militia was reorganised under Ira Westover.
- **"The Irish settlers sent men to the Goliad garrison and the Béxar siege": right, and more.**
  - Refugio men under Hugh McDonald Fraser were in the assault on Goliad on the night of 9–10
    October 1835. James Power, Westover and more than a dozen others arrived about noon on the 10th
    (S2).
  - Refugians served in Dimmitt's garrison and were at the storming of Béxar (S2).
  - **Not in the brief:** Westover led the expedition that took Fort Lipantitlán on the Nueces in
    early November 1835 (S2, S6). Power was on that expedition, which kept him from his seat at the
    Consultation (S1f).
- **"In March 1836 King's and Ward's men fought at the mission, and the mission was damaged":
  right.** The details come from accounts collected by Huson (S2):
  - Artillery battered in the church's three doors, breached its south-west corner above the
    floor, and badly damaged the roof.
  - Ward's men burned every house within rifle range of the church.
  - The Mexican army then looted the town. Few houses were left standing: John Scott's, near the
    mission, is named as one.
  - The dates disagree (§1d).
- **"The modern town sits over the site, and Our Lady of Refuge church stands near it": the church
  stands *on* the site.**
  - The 1901 church was built on top of the mission site (S5). Traces of the mission's foundations
    lie beneath it and its lawn (S1c).
  - UTSA's excavations of 1998–99 found the 1796 church location and the mission cemetery (S14).
  - The mission church was pulled down in stages. The dates disagree: about 1859 (Rea, via S2); an
    1867 or 1868 stone church built from its stones on its foundation, then pulled down in 1900 (S5,
    S6).
  - **It is the modern town's official coordinate that sits away from the site** (§2.1).
- **"It lay on the road from Goliad to the coast (Copano Bay, El Copano)": right.** There were two
  Spanish and Mexican roads from El Copano to La Bahía, one direct and one by Refugio (S2).
  - Distances: Copano is fourteen miles by the old military road (S1h), or "about twenty miles"
    (Duval, via S2). Goliad is 28 miles (Beales, S3) or 25 miles by the march of Ward's men (S4).
    **Measured straight line: 25.2 mi to the presidio, bearing 345°.**
  - **The brief leaves out Refugio's other roads.** One ran to San Patricio, crossing the Mission
    River at a sand ford a few hundred yards above the mission. Two ran to Victoria, one by the de la
    Garza ferry at Carlos Rancho on the San Antonio and one by Anaqua (S2).

### Notes for the repository

Nothing is changed here.

1. **`HIST-TEX-010` / `scripts/build-colonies-map.mjs` place Refugio at 28.3052838, −97.2752704.
   That point is 0.78 mi north of the mission and 0.61 mi north of the 1834 plaza**, on Commons
   Street and outside the 1834 town (§2.1). Suggested points for the 1835 place:
   - the **plaza (King Park): 28.29648, −97.27489** (the town);
   - the **mission church site: 28.29421, −97.27761** (the church).

   At the scale of the regional map the error is small. It will matter to any drawing of the town,
   and the Goliad road in `ROADS` ends there. `docs/COLONIES.md` §5.2's list of offsets (San Felipe,
   La Bahía, Columbia) should add Refugio.
2. **`HIST-TEX-010` and `docs/COLONIES.md` §3a say "San Patricio ... and Copano lie south of 28°N,
   outside the game area". San Patricio does (27.954°N). Copano very probably does not.**
   - TSHA puts the old port at Copano Point on the north-western shore of Copano Bay, five miles
     north of Bayside (S1h).
   - The 1936 "Site of the Town of Cópano" marker was moved in 1978 to Bayside, at 28.0917°N. Its
     original site was about five miles north-east of there (S6).
   - Bayside is at 28.094°N (OSM). Copano Bay spans 28.05–28.21°N.
   - **So El Copano stood at about 28.13–28.16°N, north of the 28°N line** (`I-9`). This was not
     measured against a surveyed coordinate. Whether the game area really stops at 28°N is for the
     owner. If it does, Copano is inside it.
3. **`docs/COLONIES.md` §5.1 says Refugio has no 1834 figure. That is still true.** Almonte gives
   none (S12). What exists instead:
   - about 170 town-lot grants of July–August 1834 (S2);
   - "nearly 200" colony land titles by 1836, fewer than 200 families (S1e);
   - "about two dozen adobe huts" in February 1836 (Duval, via S2).

   **None of these is a population.** The `ceiling:` should stay until one is found.
4. **`HIST-TEX-018` (Goliad) can take two details from Huson (S2), both secondary:**
   - Refugio's advance party under Hugh McDonald Fraser was in the assault. James Power's party
     reached Goliad about noon on 10 October.
   - Huson gives the column as **58 men** when it crossed the Guadalupe at noon on the 9th, and the
     Mexican loss as **three killed and one wounded**. That is a fourth set of casualty figures.
     `HIST-TEX-018` is right to exclude the cost.

   **For `goliad.md` §3.3, which leaves the approach open:** Huson says the column reached the San
   Antonio River "about a mile below Goliad" at about 10 p.m. and sent out a scouting party, which
   met Milam there. That points to an approach from downstream, from the east. It is secondary, and
   it is not proof of the final line.
5. **`docs/TOWNS.md`:** if Refugio ever gets keepers, what is documented is **a store (three
   merchants) and a gunsmith**. Every other trade would be invented (§6.4).
6. **Outside the repository, and not to be copied into it:**
   - TSHA's *Refugio County* entry says King and his men were "summarily executed in the Goliad
     Massacre on March 16". They were shot near Refugio on 16 March. The Goliad massacre was on 27
     March, and TSHA's own battle entry says so (S1b, S1d).
   - The same entry has Cos land at Copano on **25** September. TSHA's *Copano* entry and Huson say
     **20** September (S1h, S2).
   - Refugio's City Hall marker says the hall stands on "the city's original public square". The
     plaza is King Park. Huson says the City Hall quarter-block was the reserved market place (S2,
     S6, S9).

**So the town stands on a low, broad rise that peaks at the plaza.** The plaza is about 12 ft above
the church. **The church stands near the lip of a bluff about 40 ft high above the Mission River.**
The bluff is level to within 100–150 ft of its edge and then drops steeply. **To the west a draw
cuts the rise**, falling to 13–27 ft within 1,200–1,800 ft of the plaza. This is Huson's arroyo
"a few hundred yards above the mission", which runs north past the Catholic cemetery (S2).
**Huson's 1953 figure for Refugio's elevation, 49 ft (S2), matches the town, not the church.**

**The river.** Measured from OSM (S7), the Mission River comes down from the west-north-west
and passes the south-west corner of the 1834 grid. **The nearest point of its channel is 560 ft from
the church, bearing 244°.** From there it turns south-south-east and runs away past the south
edge of the town. This is the modern channel. Huson (S2) says the channel once lay further south,
with a flat of bottomland below the north bluff. That flat was the mission's field. **Nothing
measures the 1835 channel** (§1d, §7).

**Timber.** Ehrenberg in January 1836 saw the river running down between high bluffs, navigable for
small craft from about a mile below the town, and winding to Copano Bay through dense woods.
Around the town he saw meadows and grazing herds (via S2). King's men fought from a timber mott on
the river below the town (S2, S1d). **So the town stood on open prairie with a wooded river bottom
below it** (`I-7`).

## 3. Documented: the town plan of 1834

### 3.1 How it was made (S2)

- **June 1834:** Commissioner **José Jesús Vidaurri** arrives. Establishing the villa is probably his
  first act. The site had been designated in 1831.
- **About 1 July 1834:** the ayuntamiento is installed. On **27 July** Captain Manuel Sabriego
  brings troops over from Goliad and quarters them in the mission.
- **Summer 1834:** Vidaurri picks high, level ground for the constitutional plaza and has a large
  rock set at its centre. **James Bray** surveys the plaza, streets, blocks and lots. Colonists act
  as chain carriers, among them Thomas O'Connor, aged 16.
- **1 August 1834:** Bray's plat of the four-league town tract, a square of 10,000 varas with the
  plaza at its exact centre.
- **July–August 1834:** the lots are given out, apparently by lottery, and recorded in the *Libro
  Becerro*. Huson prints the grantees, **about 170 entries with some names repeated**. Most are Irish
  or Anglo. Many are Mexican: Aldrete, Portilla, Garza, Galán, Castillo, Ríos, Navarro, Serna and
  others.
- **Later in 1834:** **Samuel Addison White** corrects the plat at Vidaurri's direction. The
  corrections add a tier of full blocks and a tier of half blocks on the east, west and north, and
  at least a tier of half blocks on the south. Lot titles were later issued in those tiers.
- **Before March 1836:** newcomers buy lots from grantees: Lewis Ayers, Sabina Brown, Philip
  Dimmitt, Patrick Downey, Hugh McDonald Fraser, Capt. William H. Living, Dr. Alexander Lynch, Ira
  Westover and Allen White.

  Ymbación–Plasuela, two blocks north of the plaza (S2, S6). **Plasuela (*plazuela*, "little
  square") runs along its north side** (`I-2`).
- **The mission church** stood in block 11, Power–Alamo × Federación–Roca, in its south-east part
  if the 1901 church stands on it (S5, S9).
- **The river** cuts the south-west blocks. Huson says they were platted "into and across" it (S2).
  Measured, the channel crosses the Elm–Power × Roca–South block.
- **Sabina Brown's house**, Urrea's headquarters in March 1836, stood near the north-east corner of
  Alamo and Purísima (S2). She is not among Huson's grantees, but she bought a lot before 1836, so it
  may have stood in October 1835.
- **Poland's lot** was fenced with a ditch (S6), next to the mission block across Alamo Street,
  if the 1973 marker stands where the ditch was.

### 3.4 Street names

**Documented for 1834–36:** **Purísima** (a lot title of 1834 reads "calle de Purísima"),
**Santiago**, **Norte** and **South** (S2), and **Alamo**, at Sabina Brown's corner (S2, in an 1836
context but under the modern name). **Not documented, and probably old:** Federación, Empresario,
Ymbación, Plasuela and Roca, all Spanish names on the Mexican plat (`I-2`). **Certainly later:**
King and Ward (named in 1876), Commons, Commerce, Osage, Palmetto (the railroad), Mesquite, Oak,
Elm, Pecan, Bayou and Power (S2, S9).

### 4.2 What else stood at the mission, and whether it stood in 1835

TSHA (S1c) says the mission compound once held workshops, a granary and quarters for padres,
Indians and soldiers, built of driven poles, adobe and thatch, inside a stockade. It lists a stone
blacksmith shop. The Register nomination (S5) lists the same buildings as completed in 1799.
**Nothing read says any of it was standing in October 1835.** Huson found no witness of 1835–36
who mentions walls or a second stone building. Beales in 1833 saw a ruin. Five winters had passed
since the mission closed, and the pole-and-thatch buildings would not have survived them (`I-3`).
**UTSA found "mission compound features"** (S14). What and where they are was not read.

### 4.3 What is not known about the church in 1835

- **Its footprint, its length and width, and which way it faced.** The 1901 church (about 44 × 95
  ft, facing east onto Alamo Street, S9) is not evidence for the mission church.
- **Which side the churchyard was on.** "South gate" (S2) points to the south, the river side, and
  so does "the ground sloped" (S4). But burials were dug up **under the highway in front of the
  church** in 1934 (S5), and UTSA's excavation started in the US 77 right-of-way (S14), which is on
  the **east**. The *campo santo* may have been larger than the walled yard of 1836.
- **How much of the roof was on**, and whether the cupola was sound.
- **The exact spot.** The 1901 church stands "on top of" the site (S5), and foundation traces lie
  under it and the lawn (S1c). Nothing read gives coordinates for the mission church itself.

## 5. Documented: the town in October 1835

### 5.1 How it looked

- **Houses.** Few in 1833, a couple of dozen by early 1836 (§1d). They were built of **adobe, of
  upright poles or palings (jacal and picket houses), and in one case sawn lumber** (Quirk's). Roofs
  of thatch are documented for Power's house (S2) and Beales's huts were "miserable" (S3). **No
  house of 1835 has a documented size except that Power's had two rooms.**
- **Where the houses stood.** Scattered about the church, according to Ehrenberg. At least three
  were close enough to the mission that Ward's men burned them to clear their field of fire (S2).
  Sabina Brown's stood at Alamo and Purísima, Westover's where the 1936 marker is, and Poland's lot
  was ditched (S2, S6). **So the houses stood on the south-west part of the plat, near the church,
  rather than round the new plaza** (`I-4`).
- **Fences and pens.** Colonel Power's paling cattle pens on the river, of post-oak rails (S2);
  Poland's ditch-fenced lot (S6).
- **Stock.** Each colonist owned from two to five hundred cattle, and the meadows round the town
  were full of them (Ehrenberg, January 1836, via S2). Beales saw cattle, pigs and fowls in 1833
  (S3).
- **Fields.** Corn Bend, below the town on the river, was the colonists' common farm, on ground the
  mission had farmed. Remains of ditches and dykes survived into the 20th century (S2).
- **People.** Irish, other Europeans, Americans and Mexicans. Duval called the population mixed
  Irish and Mexican (via S2). Many of the non-Irish colonists were Protestant (S2).
- **No plaza buildings.** Nothing read puts any building on or round the plaza in 1835.

**So for most of October 1835 Refugio was a town whose men were away.** It had been visited at the
start of the month by an army its Mexican neighbours welcomed and its Irish leaders did not.

### 5.4 NOT there in October 1835

- **No garrison.** Sabriego's troops of July 1834 went back to Goliad. Cos stayed a day or two.
- **No priest documented, and no church in use.** The mission had been closed since 1830, and the
  1830 chapel is unproved (S2).
- **No courthouse.** The mission served as the county courthouse from 1837 (S6). The courthouse
  square came about 1850.
- **No hotel, boarding house, tavern or school.** These appear in Doughty's 1859 description (S1b),
  not before.
- **No bridge and no ferry at the town.** The river was crossed at the sand ford (S2).
- **No post office until the Provisional Government's mail of December 1835 – January 1836** (S11).
- **Not the 1901 church, the 1936 King monument, the courthouse, City Hall, King or Ward streets,
  the railroad, or anything north of Santiago Street's half-block tier.**
- **No stone mission compound, no stockade and no mission Indians.**

### 5.5 How big the place was

**Nobody counts it.** Almonte gives no figure (S12). What there is:

- Beales: five or six Irish households, December 1833 (S3).
- About 170 lot grants, July–August 1834 (S2). **A grant is not a house.** The law gave two years
  to build (S2).
- Power landed about 350 immigrants less the cholera dead in May 1834. Some stayed at Copano, the
  rest went to the mission, and a roughly equal number of Mexican colonists joined them (S1e).
- Nearly 200 titles by 1836, fewer than 200 families (S1e). Most colonists settled in or round the
  town (S1e).
- Many families had left by the autumn of 1835 (S2).
- About two dozen adobe huts in early 1836 (Duval, via S2).

**A town of about two dozen houses and perhaps one to two hundred people in October 1835 is the
honest range** (`I-5`). It is an inference, not a count.

## 8. Inferred: things that follow but that nobody states

- **I-1. Bray ran the grid by compass.** The grid is turned 9.6°; the declination in August 1834 was
  9.28° E; the instructions required north–south and east–west lines (S2, S7, S10). A third of a
  degree is within the error of a surveyor's compass and of the historical field model. **Strongly
  held.**
- **I-2. The Spanish street names (Federación, Empresario, Ymbación, Plasuela, Roca) are probably
  those of 1834, and Plasuela names the market quarter-block.** Purísima, Santiago and Norte are
  documented as original; the others fit the same Mexican scheme; the reserved market quarter-block
  lies on Plasuela (S2, S9). Not documented.
- **I-3. Only the church and churchyard wall of the mission were standing in October 1835.** From
  Huson's silence of witnesses, Beales's ruin, and five years' decay of pole-and-thatch buildings
  (S2, S3).
- **I-4. The houses of 1835 clustered near the church, not round the plaza.** From Ehrenberg's
  houses "scattered about the church", the houses Ward burned within rifle range, John Scott's near
  the mission, Sabina Brown's two blocks from it, and the fact that the plaza was newly surveyed
  open ground (S2).
- **I-5. Perhaps one to two hundred people lived in the town in October 1835.** From about two dozen
  huts in early 1836 and many families gone by the autumn of 1835 (S2). An estimate, not a count.
- **I-6. Cos was at Refugio for a day or two, about 30 September – 1 October 1835.** Huson has him
  at Copano until 30 September or 1 October, then leaving Refugio on 1 October and reaching Goliad
  on the 2nd. His "week" does not fit his own dates (S2, §1d).
- **I-7. The town stood on open prairie above a wooded river bottom.** From Ehrenberg's meadows and
  dense forest down the river, King's timber mott, and the county's tall-grass coastal prairie with
  mesquite, live oak and huisache (S1b, S2).
- **I-8. The church was the only building that could be defended.** Garay called it the only
  defensible point in the village in March 1836 (via S2). Nothing suggests it was different in
  October 1835.
- **I-9. El Copano stood at about 28.13–28.16°N and about 13 miles south-east of the church.** From
  TSHA's "five miles north of Bayside", the marker moved from five miles north-east of Bayside, and
  Bayside's position (S1h, S6, S7). Not measured on a survey.

### 9.7 What must be drawn *around* the place

- **A low rise of open prairie**, highest at the empty plaza, running down on the south-west to a
  **40-ft wooded bluff** over a small clear river, and cut on the west by a **deep arroyo**.
- **The stone church near the bluff**, the one tall thing for miles: roofed but weathered, with a
  cupola and a walled graveyard.
- **Huts near the church**, not round the plaza. Adobe, pickets and thatch, with one lumber house
  among them.
- **Paling cattle pens and a great many cattle.** Two to five hundred head a family.
- **Survey stakes and nothing else** on most of the grid. The plat was fourteen months old.
- **Ox carts.**
- **In the first days of October, an army camp on the river**, with officers' horses at the better
  houses and baggage piled in the church. It was gone by the 2nd.

## 11. Verdict

**Can Refugio be drawn honestly? Yes, its plan better than almost any town in the series. Its
buildings, hardly at all.**

**What is solid.**
- **The 1834 plat is measured**: 83⅓-ft streets, 333⅓-ft blocks, a 416⅔-ft module, a 7 × 7 town
  with the plaza at the centre, all still on the ground and confirmed by the 1935 Sanborn.
- **The plat is turned 9.6° from true north**, and that matches the 1834 compass.
- **The plaza is King Park**, on the highest ground at 52.8 ft. It was open ground in 1835.
- **The mission church stood in block 11**, two blocks south-west of the plaza, on a 40-ft bluff
  about 560 ft from the modern river channel.
- **It was stone, with a wooden roof**, a cupola, three doors, glass windows and a walled graveyard
  about 150 × 50 ft with a 16-ft arched gate. By 1835 it was a ruin, but a roofed one.
- **The town was a couple of dozen huts** of adobe, pickets and thatch, and one lumber house. Power
  had a two-room picket house with cattle pens on the river.
- **Its trades were three merchants, a gunsmith, surveyors, carters and cattle.**
- **In the game's month** Cos's army camped there with the mission as headquarters. Refugio's carts
  went on to Goliad. Its militia rode to the assault on Goliad.

**What would have to be invented.**
- The church's footprint and orientation, and which side the graveyard was on.
- Where every house, store and pen stood.
- How many houses there were.
- The river's 1835 course.
- The ford, the spring and every road course.

**Is there enough to draw the town honestly? Yes, on three conditions.**
1. **Draw the grid, the plaza, the church block and the bluff as the fixed things**, from §9's
   measurements, turned 9.6° from north. Draw the streets as survey lines, not roads.
2. **Put the huts near the church and leave the plaza and the north and east blocks empty.** A
   Refugio drawn as a busy grid town is the error to avoid.
3. **Draw the church roofed but decayed.** Not restored, and not a roofless shell.

**What will surprise anyone who knows Refugio today:** the town's official point is three-quarters
of a mile north of the mission. The grid everybody drives on is a Mexican plat of 1834, surveyed
by compass. The church Ward defended stood at the edge of a bluff two blocks from a plaza that had
nothing on it.

**The things most worth doing before drawing it**, in order of value:

1. **Read UTSA CAR Archaeological Survey Report 315** (Tennis et al. 2002), through a library or by
   asking UTSA's Center for Archaeological Research. It found the church and the cemetery, and it
   would move §4.3 and most of §9.4 from invented to measured.
2. **Get Bray's plat** (Refugio County Deed Records vol. B p. 183) **and the *Libro Becerro*** from
   the Refugio County Clerk. They give the 1834 street names and which lot went to whom. The
   merchants' and the gunsmith's lots are the likeliest places for their houses.
3. **Read Hobart Huson's *Refugio Pueblo Grant*** and **Father Oberste's *History of Refugio
   Mission*** (1942).
4. **Read Ehrenberg, Duval, Linn and Ayers in the original**, for anything Huson left out about
   houses and trades.
5. **Fix `HIST-TEX-010`'s Refugio point** (move it to the plaza, 28.29648, −97.27489) **and check
   whether Copano really lies south of 28°N** (it very probably does not).
