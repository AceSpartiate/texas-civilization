# Mina (Bastrop) in 1835 — what is known about the town plan

Research only. Nothing here is built, and no claim below has a `HIST-` or `FIC-` ID yet;
IDs are assigned when something is drawn. Read alongside `docs/town-research/san-felipe.md`
and `docs/town-research/victoria.md`, which this follows in shape, and `public/bexar-layout.js`
and `public/gonzales-art.js` — the two towns already drawn building by building, which set the
vocabulary the sketch in §7 uses. (The brief for this report named `public/gonzales-layout.js`;
**no such file exists** — Gonzales' drawn scenery lives in `public/gonzales-art.js`, in miles
rather than feet, and `public/bexar-layout.js` is the file whose terms §7 actually uses.)

Researched 2026-09-15. Every URL in §1 was opened and read on that date. The measurements in
§2.6, §3.3 and §3.4 were computed here on that date from the sources named.

**The one-line answer.** Mina sits between San Felipe and Victoria in how much is recoverable,
and it is recoverable by a **third** route. **Both plats are gone**: Arciniega's of 1832 was
never located, and Thomas Mays' of 1834 — the plan that actually governed the town in 1835 —
is **documented as lost**, last seen tattered on a city council table in 1890. But three
things survive that together fix this town harder than either of the others. First, a
**stated street width from the surveyor's own instrument**: Arciniega called for streets of
"twenty varas in width". Second, a **stated orientation**: the grid was laid out north/south
and east/west, and the modern streets measure within about a degree of true cardinal — so
**Mina's bearing is not unknown and not merely measured; it is stated and confirmed**. Third,
**a long list of original street names with their meanings**, from Bastrop's own community
historian working off the city's deed and council records. Measured off OpenStreetMap, the
surviving grid runs on a module of **388.7 ft**, which is **140 varas to within 0.05 %** — a
20-vara street plus a 120-vara block. The town stands on a level terrace about **52 ft** above
the Colorado's east bank, and the fall to the low south end where the original square was
platted is **19 ft**, measured, which is exactly the ground the 1836 flood drowned.

And there is one thing here that neither San Felipe nor Victoria has: **a fort with a block.**
A map printed in John Holland Jenkins' memoir places Mina's log stockade on the ground that
later became the courthouse square — the block bounded by Water, Pecan, Pine and Walnut —
and an independent list of what the town held in 1837 names the same stockade. That is the
single most valuable find in this research, because the stockade is why this town looks
different from every other settlement in the game.

What is missing is, again, the built town. **No source read counts the houses of Mina in
1835.** The population figure that exists is Almonte's, and it is the municipality, not the
town; the only town figure is "approximately 400" at the outbreak of the Revolution, with no
count of buildings behind it. And apart from the stockade, the store and one 1835 lot sale,
no building has a block.

### 1b. Sources looked for and not found

- **Arciniega's 1832 plat was not located.** No archive read holds it. It is quoted at one
  remove by Kesselus (S3), who evidently worked from the founding grant and the town records,
  but no image or transcription of the plan itself was found.
- **Mays' 1834 map — the plan of the town as it stood in 1835 — is documented as destroyed.**
  Kesselus records an 1890 city council report in which a councilman moved to put Mays' map
  under glass "because it was getting so tattered", and states flatly that the document is now
  gone (S3). **This is not a gap in searching. The governing document for the period this
  report covers no longer exists.** Anything a search might still turn up would be a copy or
  a derivative, most likely in the Bastrop County Clerk's plat records or the Bastrop County
  Historical Society Museum, neither of which was searched.
- **The Portal to Texas History could not be reached.** Every attempt returned its
  "Validating your request…" bot interstitial and never resolved — exactly as the Victoria
  research found. The Portal holds at least one Bastrop item this research wanted (a
  photograph of the Mina-Bastrop Pioneers Home) and is the likeliest home of a digitised
  Bastrop town map. **It is entirely unsearched here**, and it is the first thing to redo.
- **The Texas General Land Office map database could not be searched.** Its public search
  interface returned the full collection (41,251 records) for every query term attempted, the
  same failure the Victoria research hit. One Bastrop record was visible as a thumbnail

- **The National Register nomination for the Bastrop Commercial District** (listed 22 December

- **The 1885 Sanborn sheet of Bastrop was not read.** It is catalogued (S14) but every Library

- **The 1887 Koch bird's-eye view of Bastrop was not examined**, only its catalogue essay (S9).

- **Kesselus, *History of Bastrop County, Texas: Before Statehood* (1986) was not read.** It is
  the standing scholarship on exactly this town in exactly this decade, it is cited by both
  TSHA (S1) and Carson (S5), and it is the book behind the newspaper column that carries most

- **Bastrop Historical Society, *In the Shadow of the Lost Pines* (1955)** — cited by TSHA (S1)

## 2. Documented — the plan

### 2.1 Who laid it out, and when

- The place was a crossing before it was a town. **El Camino Real crossed the Colorado here**,
  and in **1805** (the marker's date; other sources say **1804**) the Spanish founded the fort
  **Puesta del Colorado** and a community with it, to protect commerce on the road
  (S6-Early History; S5; S9 says "about 1804"). **The 1804/1805 disagreement is left standing.**
- Settlement under Austin followed the **Little Colony** grant of 1827. The first Anglo
  settlers arrived about **1829** — Jenkins names **Martin Wells** as the first man to settle
  where the town stands, with Billy Barton, Leman and Jesse Barker, Josiah Wilbarger and
  Reuben Hornsby following (S4; S10 gives the same list and the same date).
- **The town was formally established on 8 June 1832.** Land commissioner **José Miguel de
  Arciniega** named it Bastrop and platted it "along conventional Mexican lines, with a square
  in the center and blocks set aside for public buildings" (S1, quoted). S2 adds that Arciniega
  signed a **four-league grant** for the town and laid it out **with Samuel May Williams**.
  The founding grant describes the site as the place on the Colorado where it crosses the road
  from Bexar to Nacogdoches (S7).
- **The town was surveyed again in 1834.** **Thomas H. Mays** "became Bastrop's first municipal
  surveyor and platted the city's new streets" in 1834 (S6-Mays, quoted). Kesselus says Mays
  "refined this plan to produce a better map that prevailed for nearly a century as a
  determinative guide" (S3, quoted). **So the plan a student would walk into in October 1835
  is Mays', not Arciniega's** — and Mays' map is lost (§1b).
- **The name.** Oliver Jones introduced the renaming bill on 3 April 1834; it became law on
  **24 April 1834**, creating both the town name **Mina** and the **Municipality of Mina**; by
  **18 August 1834** the ayuntamiento was sitting, with **Robert M. Coleman** as alcalde (S4).
  The name reverted to Bastrop on 18 December 1837 (S1, S6-Bastrop County).
- **One period resident denied the name was ever used.** Smithwick: "I have no knowledge of the
  town of Bastrop ever having been called Mina" (S10, quoted), and he calls the place simply
  the San Antonio crossing. **Do not resolve this.** The ayuntamiento of Mina demonstrably
  granted town lots under that name in 1834 and 1835 (S4, S6-Sayer), so the official name is
  not in doubt; what Smithwick records is that the people there went on saying Bastrop.
  **A student in October 1835 would plausibly hear both names**, and that is worth showing.

### 2.2 The rule it was laid out under — and the one measurement the surveyor states

This town's sources give something neither San Felipe's nor Victoria's do: **a street width in
the surveyor's own terms.**

Kesselus, working from Arciniega's own words, records that Arciniega stated the survey of the
town proper — its streets, squares, blocks and lots — accorded exactly with the plan he had
drawn, and that **he called for streets of "twenty varas in width"** (S3, quoted). Kesselus
converts that to "a quarter of an inch less than fifty-five feet" and notes that **55 ft
remains Bastrop's right-of-way width today.**

**A conversion note that matters.** Kesselus' arithmetic implies a vara of 32.99 in
(20 × 32.99 in = 54.98 ft). This project's standing conversion — used in
`docs/town-research/san-felipe.md` and `victoria.md` — is the statutory Texas vara of
**33⅓ in = 2.7778 ft**, which makes 20 varas **55.56 ft**. **The two differ by 7 inches in a
street and by 4 ft in a 140-vara module.** Both are given in §7. This is a real, small,
unresolved discrepancy and should not be smoothed over.

The scheme Arciniega reserved, as Kesselus describes it (S3):

- **a central square — the Square of the Constitution**, named for the Mexican constitution of
  1824, in the **south end of town**;
- **adjacent blocks for municipal buildings and a jail**;
- **one block for a school and another for public education**;
- **a twelve-acre cemetery**, confirmed independently: "Included in the initial community plat
  was a twelve-acre cemetery overlooking the colony" (S6-Fairview, quoted);
- and, per the search-level summary of Kesselus' work, **a slaughterhouse**.

And the street names follow the reservations, which is the strongest internal evidence that
Kesselus has the plan right: **Austin Street at the head of the square**, **Government Street**
(now Emile) for the city buildings, **College Street** for the schools (S3).

**The square was never built on.** TSHA: "subsequent developments prevented the square from
ever being developed" (S1, quoted). §2.7 says what those developments were.

### 2.3 The grid's orientation — stated, not inferred

Kesselus states it outright: Arciniega used a plan "based on a survey that laid out a typical
Mexican municipality with **streets running north/south and east/west**" (S3). Measurement
agrees (§2.6): every downtown street falls within about a degree of true cardinal.

**This is the one thing Mina has that San Felipe does not have at all and Victoria has only by
measurement.** San Felipe's bearing is unknown; Victoria's was measured off the modern streets
at N 20.7° E with nobody stating it; **Mina's is stated in words by a named historian working
from the founding documents, and then independently confirmed on the ground.**

### 2.4 The streets, by name

Kesselus sorts the modern street names into those that plausibly come from Arciniega's plan
and those that cannot. **This is a historian's judgement, not a transcription of the plat**,
and he says so — he is openly "puzzled" about several. Reproduced with his hedges intact:

**Attributed to Arciniega's scheme, with a stated reason:**

| Street | Runs | Why it is named that (S3) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Austin** | east/west | for the founder, at the head of the square |
| **Government** (now **Emile**) | east/west | for the city buildings reserved on it |
| **College** | east/west | for the school and public-education blocks |
| **Water** | north/south | extended south it meets the San Antonio Road's river crossing |
| **Hill** | north/south | it ran out toward the eastern ridge, on the line of the Old San Antonio Road |
| **Spring** | east/west | it ran to the river at the most prominent spring, reserved for public access |
| **Mill** | east/west | where an early horse-powered gristmill stood |
| **South** | east/west | the southern end of the plan |
| **Farm** | east/west | the dividing line between building lots and farm lots (§2.5) |
| **Jefferson** | north/south | "almost certainly" for the author of the US Declaration, a model for Mexican liberators |
| **Pecan**, **Haysel** | north/south | a preference for trees |
| **Walnut**, **Pine**, **Chestnut** | east/west | the same preference for trees |

**Explicitly doubted or excluded by Kesselus:**

- **Main** — "might have" been named as the westernmost street along the top of the bluff, but
  Kesselus notes that building lots were originally described as east or west of **Water**, and
  only later as east or west of **Main**, which suggests **Water was the westernmost street in
  Arciniega's scheme and Main was added afterward** (S3). See §2.7.
- **Fayette, Marion (now MLK), Chambers** — Kesselus is "puzzled"; their position east of the
  core suggests they were unnamed by Arciniega and named after independence.
- **Washington, Jackson, Madison, Houston, Pitt** — post-independence, and Kesselus notes no
  Mexican official would have honoured Sam Houston.
- **Church** — laid out in **1849** by John D. Hogan when he subdivided Farm Lots 1 and 2 west
  of Main; he reserved 27½ ft on each side of a line so the street matched Arciniega's width.
  **Church was the first new street** after the original plan, with the possible exception of
  Main. **Buttonwood** was Hogan's "cross street"; **Wilson** is the northward extension of an
  older **Diagonal Street** (S3).

**So the defensible 1835 street list is: Water, Pecan, Jefferson, Hill, Haysel running
north/south; and Farm, Spring, Chestnut, Pine, Walnut, Austin, Government, College, South and
Mill running east/west — with Main uncertain and everything west of Main (Church, Wilson,
Buttonwood) certainly later.**

### 2.5 Lots — and the one number that does not reconcile

Kesselus states the plan's two-tier lot scheme plainly (S3):

- **South of Farm Street**, on the high bluff, the town proper, divided into **three-acre
  "building lots"**.
- **North of Farm Street**, **twelve-acre "farm lots"**, and **in the farm-lot section streets
  were not designated**.
- Each early settler received **a small lot for a home or business and a large lot for
  agriculture**.

Two independent confirmations of the farm lot:

- Hogan bought **Farm Lots No. 1 and No. 2 West of Main** in 1849, "twenty-four acres", bounded
  by present Farm, Cedar, Main and Wilson (S3) — twelve acres each.
- Amos Alexander's estate held "a 12-acre farm lot and a building lot in Bastrop" (S4).
- And the Bastrop Methodist Church bought "farm lot 1" for its 1851 building (S6-Methodist).

**But the building lot does not reconcile.** Jenkins' editor, working from the Bastrop deed
records, states that on **16 September 1834 the Ayuntamiento of the Municipality of Mina
granted James Northcross a four-acre building lot in Bastrop for four dollars**, and that he
**already had a house built on that lot** (S4). Kesselus says three acres. Both are working
from town records. **Three acres and four acres are both on the record and this document does
not resolve it** — but see §6, I-3, where the measured block offers a way they might both be
loose readings of the same figure.

Two further facts about lots, both from S4:

- **S. V. R. Eggleston received two free building lots from the town commissioners.** Free lots
  "were given to every 'artist' who might settle in the town" — that is, to any tradesman.
  **The town was paying craftsmen in land to come.**
- Northcross' 1834 grant shows that **houses stood on the ground before the lots were formally
  granted**, which is the same pattern the San Felipe waysides record of settlers building in
  the streets and squares before the ayuntamiento moved them.

### 2.6 The grid on the ground, measured

Every street centreline in downtown Bastrop was pulled from OpenStreetMap (S16) and projected
into a local plane with its origin at Main × Chestnut. Results:

- **Bearing.** The north/south streets measure between **176.4° and 181.9°** (Main 176.4,
  Water 178.9, Pecan 178.8, Hill 179.2, Jefferson 1.9, Haysel 179.3, Fayette 178.5); the

  27.2 acres**. Close, not exact — consistent with twelve-acre farm lots, and a reminder that
  the modern street lines are not the 1832 lot lines.

### 2.7 What happened to the plan in 1836 — and why it matters for a 1835 drawing

This is the most important single caution in this report, and it cuts the other way from the
usual one.

Kesselus (S3): Arciniega's original design **centred on a square in the southern portion of
town**, and that design **was abandoned when the flood of 1836 raised Gills Branch enough to
inundate the area**, forcing the town's centre of gravity onto the highest ground — a street
that had not been part of the original scheme and became **Main**, running "atop an ancient
levee left by centuries of river overflowing".

**So the Bastrop that exists today is the post-1836 town, and the Mina of October 1835 was
centred somewhere else.** A drawn Mina that puts its life along Main Street is drawing the
town the flood made, not the town the students would walk into. In 1835 the civic ground was
the south end — Austin, Government and College Streets around the Square of the Constitution —
and the measured elevations (§3.3) show that ground sitting **19 ft lower** than the Main
Street levee, which is exactly why one flood was enough to move a town.

**How confident is this?** Kesselus is one source, writing in a newspaper column, hedging
("might have", "may indicate", "adding to the theory"). TSHA independently says the square was
never developed (S1). The measured spacing break south of Government Street (§2.6) is
consistent with the south end never being built out. **Three weak-to-moderate arrows pointing
the same way, and no source read contradicts it.**

## 3. Documented — the river, the road and the site

### 3.1 The crossing — why the town is there

- **The Old San Antonio Road / El Camino Real crossed the Colorado here, and that is the whole
  reason for the place.** The founding grant itself describes the site as the place on the
  Colorado where it crosses the road from Bexar to Nacogdoches (S7). The 1968 marker says the
  same: because the Camino Real crossed the river here, this was a strategic spot, and the
  1804/1805 Spanish fort was put here to protect commerce on the road (S6-Early History).
  Smithwick calls it simply the San Antonio crossing (S10).
- **In 1835 it was a ford, not a ferry.** Carson is explicit: "Some time after Bastrop's
  founding, ferry service was opened", naming **Samuel T. Stone**, who settled in Bastrop in
  **1839**, and dating the more direct ferry at Pine and Chestnut Streets to **the 1840s and
  1850s** (S5). Jenkins' editor records a sentry posted at "the river ford" in March 1836 (S4).
  Smithwick's company **sank all the boats** at Bastrop in 1836 (S10), so boats existed — but
  **no ferry and no ferryman is documented for 1835, and no keeper of the ford is named
  anywhere.** This is a real answer to the brief's question 3, and it is a negative one.
- **Where the crossing was.** Two statements, and they broadly agree:
  - **Zively's 1916 survey of the King's Highway** placed the crossing **100 varas — 277 feet —
    north of the mouth of Gills Branch**, and set the survey post for OSR Marker #65 there,
    which Carson reckons "some 200 to 300 feet south of the intersection of present-day Water
    and Jasper streets" (S5).
  - **Kesselus** says Water Street, continued south, would intersect the San Antonio Road's
    crossing (S3).
  Both put it on Water Street's line, a little south of Jasper. **Gills Branch has moved
  southward since the early 1800s** (S5), so its 1830s mouth was north of the modern one.
- **Why there and not straight across.** Kesselus, reported by Carson (S5), gives two reasons,
  and they are the best piece of terrain reasoning found for any of the three towns:
  1. **a rock shoal downstream** caused sand and gravel to fill the river bed, giving a firm,
     shallow bottom to ford;
  2. **the banks of Gills Branch gave a gentler slope** up from and down to the water than
     scaling the Colorado's steep, high east bank directly.
  The King's Highway detoured **over a quarter of a mile** each way to use it.
- **Austin saw the same ground in 1821.** His journal for Tuesday 7 August 1821, quoted in S4:
  "near the river heavy pine timber"; the banks very high and generally clear of overflow;
  bottom and banks gravelly; water clear and well tasted; the current brisk; and where the road
  crosses, about five miles of bottom, mostly high prairie clear of overflow, timbered with
  pecan, ash, oak and cedar. **Austin's "banks very high" and "gravelly" bottom are the same two
  facts Kesselus reasons from, fourteen years earlier and from the man who chose the site.**

### 3.2 The road through the town — and a live dispute about which street

**The sources disagree, publicly and in print, about which street the Camino Real ran on.**

**Zively's route (S5):** the road entered Bastrop at what is now Hoffman Road × Highway 95,
followed the **west bank of Gills Branch** south-west to **Hill Street**, ran down Hill from
Spring to Pine, turned south-west to **Water and Government (Emile)**, then down **Water Street
to Jasper at the river**, and crossed 277 ft north of the Gills Branch mouth. Zively's survey
post for OSR Marker #64 is at Hill × Chestnut and the granite marker is still there. **Kesselus
independently says Hill Street "ran from town out toward the eastern ridge… following the line
of the Old San Antonio Road into the forest" (S3)** — two sources on Hill Street.

**Local tradition (S5):** the road ran east–west along **Chestnut Street**. Chestnut carries the
El Camino Real street signs, the DAR granite marker at Chestnut × Hill, and **52 bronze
"El Camino Real de los Tejas" medallions laid into its sidewalks in 2010.**

**Carson's objection to Zively, which is worth recording in full because it is specific:**
on his sketch of Bastrop, **Zively swapped the labels of Chestnut and Hill Streets** — the
north–south street is labelled "Chestnut Street" and the main east–west street is labelled
"Hill or Bridge Street". Carson argues this may have skewed the whole survey ninety degrees.
He adds a physical argument: the **Mina-Bastrop Pioneers Home, built 1836 or 1837** and known
as "The Old Stagecoach Inn" for its role on the Old San Antonio Road, has a **Hill Street
address but fronts Chestnut Street** — which was only discovered when the house was restored
in 2010 (S5).

**Do not resolve this.** Both routes converge on Water Street and the Gills Branch ford at the
river end; they differ in how the road reached town from the east. §7 draws both.

The other road out of Mina is documented too: **the Gotier Trace**, originated in the 1820s,
joined San Felipe to Bastrop across Austin, Washington, Fayette, Lee and Bastrop counties
(S6-Gotier). It was "only a marked route which travelers could follow — dusty in droughts,
boggy in rains" (S6-Gotier, quoted). **That is the road a family dealt to Mina would have come
in on from San Felipe**, and it is not the Camino Real.

### 3.4 Where the river actually runs

Computed here from the OSM channel (S16). **The town is on the east bank** — stated by S9
("the east bank of the Colorado River on a bluff that is generally level") and by S5 ("the
Colorado River's steep, high east bank"), and confirmed by the geometry.

The Colorado comes down from the north-west, runs **south past the west side of the town**,
then **swings east across the ground south of the town**, reaches its easternmost point about
level with the College/Jasper end of the grid, and turns away south-west. **The town sits in
the crook of that bend, with water on two sides.** Gills Branch drains the hills east of town,
loops around the south end and empties into the Colorado at the bottom of that bend.

## 4. Documented and strongly supported — the buildings

### 4.1 The stockade — the one building in Mina with a block

**This town had a fort in the middle of it in 1835, and no other settlement in this game does.**

- Fort Tours states it plainly: Bastrop was the uppermost white settlement of any size on the
  Colorado in 1835; the residents had banded together against Waco, Tawakoni, Kichai and
  Comanche raids; and consequently **"a strong log stockade or fort was erected in the center
  of the little town"** (S8, quoted), as a refuge for the townspeople in the event of a serious
  attack.
- TSHA corroborates its existence and its date-bracket from the other end: when the town
  incorporated in December 1837, the community comprised **"a courthouse, a hotel, a stockade,
  a gunsmith shop, a general store, and a number of residences"** (S1; S7 repeats it).
- TSHA also describes the town's function in exactly these terms: Bastrop was "the place where
  settlers rallied for retaliation and **forted up for protection** when Indian depredations
  occurred in the vicinity" (S1).
- **And Jenkins' 1837 map places it.** The key to the map "Bastrop, Texas (showing location of
  homes in 1837)", printed at page 15 of the 1958 edition, includes the entry
  **"STOCKADE (Site of Present Courthouse)"** (S4).

**Where that is.** The Bastrop County Courthouse of 1883 stands at **803 Pine Street**
(S6-Courthouse; S15 confirms the address and the 1883–84 date), and the 1968 marker says the
first courthouse was built in 1853 and "present one in 1883 on the same spot" (S6-Early
History). Converted into the grid frame, the courthouse marker falls at **x +173 m, y −170 m**
from Main × Chestnut — **inside the block bounded by Water, Pecan, Pine and Walnut Streets**.
S9 independently describes the courthouse as standing "in the center of a small square" one
block east of the Main Street business district.

**So the stockade's block is recoverable, and it is the Water–Pecan–Pine–Walnut block.**
Caveats, stated plainly: Jenkins' map is a 1958 reconstruction of 1837 by the author's
great-great-grandson, drawn from Bastrop deed and probate records and from a local guide who
walked him round the sites (S4's acknowledgements name both); it is **1837, not 1835**; and
the stockade's **size, plan, height, gates and whether it had blockhouses at the corners are
nowhere stated.** The only comparable structure any source read describes is Fort Coleman on
Walnut Creek, built **June 1836** — "a cluster of log cabins enclosed by a heavy stockade with
two block houses at opposite corners" (S4's footnote, citing Smithwick) — **which is a
different fort, built the following year, and must not be drawn as if it were this one.**

### 4.2 The store, and the rest of the built town

Jenkins is this town's Smithwick, and his single most useful sentence is about the store:

> "the only store in Bastrop was a little old elm log house"

nevertheless did a thriving business, and that butter, chickens, eggs — all home produce —
were taken in trade. **The date is "our young days", not stated exactly**, and Jenkins' index
puts Gazley's store at pages 213–214 among the "Recollections at Random", so it is
reminiscence, not a dated entry. Treat it as **the 1830s**, probably early.

That is a real and drawable fact: this town had no church.

### 4.3 What they were built of — and the pines

This is the brief's question 4, and it has a sharper answer than expected.

- **The Lost Pines were there, and were being used, and were not being sawn.** The stand is
  "the westernmost stand of the eastern pine forest and the only timber available in what was
  then western Texas" (S1, quoted). Austin noted "heavy pine timber" near the river in **1821**
  (S4, quoting his journal). Zebulon Pike recorded the trees in **1807** (S6-Lost Pines).
- **But the first mill came after the period.** The **Bastrop Steam Mill Company** began
  lumbering in the Lost Pines in **1838**; Higgins Mill, run by Jacob Higgins and Abner Cook,
  followed in **1840** (S11). The lumber trade took off only when Austin became the capital in
  **1839**, after which "ox teams were carting Bastrop lumber to San Antonio, along the western
  frontier, and into Mexico" (S1, quoted). **In October 1835 there was no sawmill at Mina.**
- **So the pine in 1835 was split, not sawn.** Jenkins describes his own family's cabin, built
  in Bastrop County about 1830: **cedar logs cut with axes and dragged up with horses; the
  roofs of split pine boards, riven about a mile and a half away and carried up on horseback;
  built without wagons, nails, or any kind of machinery** (S4). The editor's foreword puts it
  the same way: the boards for roof and siding were hand-hewn from that island of pines, and
  placed without nails.
- **The one building material stated for a town building is elm** — Gazley's store, "a little
  old elm log house" (S4).
- **Brick came later.** The Sayer's Office marker describes the site as occupied **by 1855** by
  a structure of local brick and "Lost Pines" wood (S6-Sayer). **Nothing read describes brick,
  stone or adobe construction in Mina in the 1830s.**

**The honest summary for a drawn Mina: log walls — cedar and elm and oak from the bottoms —
with roofs of riven pine boards weighted or pegged, no nails, no sawn siding, no brick, one
two-storey building by 1837 and probably none in 1835, and a log stockade in the middle.**
This is the most log-built and least masonry town of the three researched, and the pine roof
is the thing that makes it look different from San Felipe.

### 4.5 What happened in 1835–36

Relevant because a student may walk into Mina in October 1835:

- **Mina was first in Texas to organise.** "In May 1835, Mina citizens became the first to
  organize a committee of safety and correspondence for the general diffusion of information"
  (S1, quoted). TSHA dates the appointment to **8 May 1835**, with Gonzales and Viesca
  following a few days later (S11). **The town whose families this game deals to Mina was the
  first town in Texas to set up the machinery of the Revolution.**
- **Mina had a delegate at the Consultation.** Judge **R. M. Williamson** — "Three-legged
  Willie" — "retreated to Mina, which district he represented in the Consultation which
  established the provisional government" (S10).
- **Mina raised a company.** The **Mina Volunteers** under **Jesse Billingsley**, with
  **Edward Burleson** commanding at higher level, fought at San Jacinto; Thomas Mays the
  surveyor was wounded in the leg there (S6-Mays; S4). Markers in Fairview Cemetery commemorate
  **Campbell Taylor**, "A Mina Volunteer", and **Jesse Halderman**, who "Served in the Volunteer
  Army of Texas, 1835" (S6 index). **Robert Love Reding of Bastrop took part in the capture of
  Goliad on 9 October 1835 and signed the Goliad Declaration** (S6 index) — which is the direct
  answer to why this town's volunteers matter in October 1835.
- **The town was abandoned in the Runaway Scrape and the sources disagree about how badly it
  was hurt.** TSHA: residents "returned to find it completely destroyed by the Mexican army and
  Indians" (S1, quoted). **Jenkins, who came back with his mother about 1 May 1836, says the
  opposite in specifics: "two houses in the town had been burned and the country was sacked of
  everything except a few hogs"** (S4). Smithwick records the town depopulated but standing,
  with his company of twenty-two men the only people in it, and the boats sunk (S10).
  **Do not resolve this.** Two houses burned and complete destruction are both in print, one
  from a compiler and one from a boy who walked back into it.

## 5. Unknown — what a layout would need and no source read supplies

Everything in this section would have to be invented and registered as fiction.

1. **Both plats.** Arciniega's 1832 plan was not located; **Mays' 1834 plan, which is the plan
   of 1835, is documented as destroyed** (§1b). Every statement in §2 about what the plat
   contained is second-hand, and most of it is from one newspaper column.
2. **Where the Square of the Constitution was, block by block.** Kesselus puts it in the south
   end with Austin Street at its head and the municipal, jail, school and education blocks
   around it (S3). **He does not say which column of the grid, and nobody else places it at
   all.** §7 puts it between Water and Pecan for reasons stated there; **that column is
   invented.**
3. **The size and shape of the square, and whether it was one block or several.** No dimension
   anywhere. Same for the jail block, the school block and the education block.
4. **The extent of the platted town.** Five streets by seven is what the surviving module
   covers (§2.6), but **no source says how many blocks Arciniega or Mays laid out.** Whether
   Main Street existed in 1835 at all is doubted by the one historian who addresses it (§2.4).
5. **The building lot's size.** Three acres (S3) against four acres (S4), both from town
   records, unreconciled (§2.5).
6. **The positions of sixteen of the seventeen households on Jenkins' 1837 map.** The names
   were recovered, the numbers and the drawing were not. **This is the single most recoverable
   unknown in this report** — it needs one look at page 15 of the 1958 edition.
7. **Any dimension of any building.** Not one. No length, width, height or roof pitch anywhere
   in Mina. **Every footprint would be invented.**
8. **The stockade's plan.** That it was log, strong, and in the centre of town is documented;
   its size, shape, gates, height, whether it enclosed cabins, and whether it had blockhouses
   are all unknown (§4.1). **The temptation to borrow Fort Coleman's plan must be resisted** —
   that is a different fort, built in June 1836.
9. **The number of buildings or houses in the town in 1835.** Nothing. §4.4 is the whole of it.
10. **Where the store, the ayuntamiento, the ford keeper or any 1835 trade stood.** Gazley's
    elm-log store is the best-described building in the town and **it has no location at all.**
11. **Whether there was a ferry in 1835.** The record says the ferry came later (§3.1), but
    Smithwick's sunken boats in 1836 mean *something* floated there. **No ferryman, ford keeper
    or landing is named for 1835.**
12. **Which street the Camino Real ran on inside the town** — Hill (Zively, Kesselus) or
    Chestnut (local tradition, the 1836–37 inn's frontage). Unresolved in print (§3.2).
13. **Where the 1804 Spanish fort stood.** "Its location is unknown" (S5). There may be a
    ruined or robbed-out Spanish fort somewhere near the crossing in 1835 and **nobody knows
    where, or whether anything of it was still visible.**
14. **Any archaeology.** Nothing read describes a single excavated feature anywhere in Mina.
15. **Whether the pines around the town had been cut into in 1835, and how far out.** Jenkins'
    family split boards "about a mile and a half distant" from their cabin south of town about
    1830; the commercial cut began in 1838. **The state of the woods immediately around Mina in
    1835 is not described.**
16. **The town's own water.** Spring Street is named for "the most prominent spring… designated
    for public access" running to the river (S3). **Its position on the street and whether it
    was the town's water supply are not stated.**
17. **Whether the town was fenced or open outside the stockade.** No enclosure is recorded.

## 6. Inferred — things that follow but that nobody states

Each is an inference of this document and must be labelled as one if it is used.

- **I-1. The block module is 140 varas: a 120-vara block and a 20-vara street.** The street
  width is documented (S3). The module is measured at 388.7 ft across thirteen clean gaps
  (§2.6), and 140 varas is 388.9 ft at the statutory vara. **Nobody states the block size.**
  This is the load-bearing inference of the document: if it is wrong, every dimension in §7 is
  wrong, though the street order and the bearing are not.
- **I-2. The measured grid is Arciniega's and Mays' grid, not a later relaying.** The module
  holds without exception between Government Street and Farm Street and breaks outside both —
  and those two lines are exactly where the documents say the plan changes character (the
  farm-lot section north of Farm with no designated streets, and the abandoned square end south
  of Government). **Nothing read says the grid was never re-laid**, but a later relaying would
  not reproduce those two boundaries.
- **I-3. A "building lot" may be a block plus its bounding half-streets.** A 120-vara block is
  2.55 acres; a 140-vara square — block plus half a street on each side — is **3.47 acres**,
  which sits between Kesselus' three acres and Jenkins' four. **That is arithmetic of this
  document's, not a claim by anybody**, and it is offered only because it would reconcile two
  sources that otherwise contradict.
- **I-4. The civic centre of Mina in 1835 was the south end, not Main Street.** Follows from
  §2.7 and §3.3: the square and the municipal blocks were platted there, and the ground there
  measures 19 ft below the levee that Main Street later took. **A drawn 1835 Mina centred on
  Main Street would be drawing the town the 1836 flood made.**
- **I-5. Water Street, not Main, was the town's western street in 1835.** Kesselus' own
  reasoning from the lot descriptions (§2.4), which he offers as a theory and not a finding,
  plus the fact that Water Street is the one the Camino Real came down to the crossing.
- **I-6. Most of the grid was empty in 1835.** A town of ~400 people (S1) on a grid whose
  measured core alone holds 24 to 30 blocks; seventeen households on the 1837 map; and a
  nineteenth-century teacher's verdict on the town, quoted by S9: "The town was very
  extensively laid out, but like a half starved cat" — it was not filled out yet. **That
  remark is decades after the period and is used here only as a direction of travel.**
  **A Mina drawn as a filled grid would be wrong**, the same warning San Felipe's and
  Victoria's research both end on.
- **I-7. The built cluster in 1835 sat between the stockade and the crossing.** The stockade's
  block (Water–Pecan × Pine–Walnut) is documented; the crossing is south down Water Street;
  the one 1835 lot sale is at Main × Chestnut, one block north of the stockade. **Three points
  in a line down the Water/Main columns, and nobody draws the line.**
- **I-8. The houses had riven pine-board roofs on log walls.** Documented for Jenkins' own
  cabin in the county about 1830 (S4) and for the region's practice; **not stated of the town's
  buildings.** The absence of a sawmill until 1838 (S11) is what makes it near-certain.
- **I-9. The eastern ridge on the Hill Street line is "the hill" Kesselus names**, and it is
  where the pines began. Measured at 424 ft against the town's 369 ft (§3.3); Kesselus says
  Hill Street "ran from town out toward the eastern ridge… into the forest" (S3).

## 8. Verdict

**Can Mina be drawn honestly? Yes — and its frame is the best documented of the three.**

The bearing is **stated in words and confirmed by measurement**, which is better than
Victoria's (measured only) and incomparably better than San Felipe's (unknown). The street
width comes from the **surveyor's own instruction**, which no other town in this research has.
The module measures to 140 varas within 0.05 %, and it breaks exactly where the documents say
the plan changes. Nine or ten original street names survive with reasons attached. The two
places where the plan meets the ground — the flooded south end and the level Main Street levee
— are confirmed by an independent elevation survey. And the **stockade has a block**, which
makes Mina the only one of the three towns with a documented civic structure on documented
ground.

**But the building record is the thinnest of the three, and the most important document is
destroyed.** San Felipe has Smithwick's walk down the road and twenty named businesses on
numbered lots. Victoria has four buildings at four crossings. **Mina has one store with no
location, one lot sale with a corner, one stockade with a block, and a map from 1837 whose
positions this research could not read.** Mays' 1834 plat — the plan of the town as it stood in
1835 — is gone, and no substitute exists.

**What that means for the game.** A student who walks into Mina in October 1835 can be walked
up a true north–south street 55 feet wide, on a grid whose angle and spacing are real, past a
log stockade standing on the block it really stood on, down to a gravel ford where the King's
Highway crossed the Colorado — and told truthfully that the walls are log and the roofs are
split pine boards because there is no mill yet, that there is no church, that the square the
surveyor drew is empty low ground that will flood next spring, and that this town was the first
in Texas to appoint a committee of safety. **What they cannot be shown is which house belongs
to whom.** That is the same trade the other two reports strike, and here the arrangement is
firmer and the furniture is thinner.

**The four things most worth doing before drawing it**, in order of value:

1. **Look at page 15 of Jenkins' *Recollections of Early Texas* (1958).** One printed map with
   seventeen numbered households and the stockade, in a book this research has already read as
   text. It would turn §4.2's unplaced names into positions and make Mina the best-documented
   built town of the three. **This is the cheapest high-value thing left undone.**
2. **Read Kesselus, *History of Bastrop County, Texas: Before Statehood* (1986).** It is the
   book behind the newspaper column that carries most of §2, it is cited by TSHA and by Carson,
   and it is where the square's block, the lot sizes and the 1835 town would be settled if they
   can be settled at all.
3. **Get into the Portal to Texas History and the GLO map database**, neither of which this
   research could search (§1b). And ask the **Bastrop County Historical Society Museum** — 6,000
   archival documents, 230 maps, founded specifically to preserve the records of Austin's
   Little Colony — whether any copy or tracing of the Mays map survives.
4. **Read the 1885 Sanborn sheet and look at the 1887 Koch bird's-eye view.** Both are fifty
   years late, but the Sanborn carries a scale bar that would check the 140-vara module
   directly, and Koch drew the grid, the courthouse square and Gills Branch from the air.
