# Velasco in 1835 — the fort, the bar, and what there was of a town

Research only. Nothing here is built, and no claim below has a `HIST-` or `FIC-` ID yet; IDs are
assigned when something is drawn. Read alongside `docs/town-research/brazoria.md` — **Velasco is
Brazoria's sea gate, thirty miles down the same river, in the same jurisdiction, served by the same

`san-felipe.md`, `victoria.md`, `mina.md`, `liberty.md`, `columbia.md` and `washington.md` supply

## The one-line answer

**Velasco in October 1835 was not a town. It was a bar, a pilot, a customs post, a tavern and a
handful of houses on a sand flat, with the ruin of a circular Mexican fort standing beside them and
an eighteen-pounder being mounted on it in the last week of the month. The grid called "The City of
Velasco" is a New York lithograph of March 1837 and it was never built. And for the first time in
ten reports there is a dated scale drawing of the place, made three months after the game's month,
showing exactly six buildings.**

Six things, and they agree:

1. **A picture, in February 1836.** Eduard Harkort, chief engineer of the Texas army, drew the mouth
   of the Brazos in his journal with Velasco on the north bank as **two fenced enclosures holding
   about six buildings**, and the fort a short distance east of them as **a ring inside a ring with
   a dot in the middle** — the concentric palisade with its central bastion, exactly as four
   witnesses described it (S18, §3.1, §3.2). Measured here against Harkort's own 900-foot scale, the
   whole built town covers about **400 × 330 ft** and the fort is about **87 ft across**.
2. **Four eyewitnesses describe the fort and they do not contradict each other.** Circular; log and
   sand; two rows of posts six feet apart filled with sand, earth and shell; an interior banquette;
   a raised central mound carrying a long gun on a pivot; a ditch outside (S5, §3.1).
3. **The bar is the best-documented thing about the place.** Six feet of water ordinarily (Holley,
   1836), seven to eight feet by the town's own promoters (1837), eight feet at mean low water when
   the Coast Survey measured it (1858); a sand bank about twenty yards wide, four hundred yards off
   the beach, with three fathoms just outside it; **two pilots, two flagstaffs, a pair of range
   marks with black balls on white ground, and a fire lit on the beach at night for vessels caught
   off the bar in bad weather** (S9, S13, S16, S17; §4).
4. **The place had about ten legal voters.** James F. Perry, writing from Peach Point on 7 December
   1834, says the Velasco precinct could not muster more than seven to ten, and that fifty or sixty
   votes were taken there anyway because two or three vessels were lying in the river (S15, §5.6).
   In the game's month the same complaint recurs in print (S13, §5.6).
5. **There is a dated, located, populated scene here in the game's month.** The **New Orleans
   Greys landed at Velasco**, were welcomed by the inhabitants, and **organised and elected their
   officers on the spot** (S14, §5.7) before going up the river.
6. **The trap is the worst in the series and the repository is already inside it.** There are three
   Velascos. `HISTORY.md`'s `HIST-TEX-010` and `docs/COLONIES.md` §3a give Velasco the coordinate
   **28.96191, −95.36050**. That is **New Velasco, platted 1891, now part of Freeport** — measured
   here at **3.91 miles on bearing 291°** from where the 1835 town stood. See the corrections below.

### Corrections to the brief, and three to the repository

- **"Velasco had a fort" is right, but the fort of October 1835 is not the fort of the battle, and
  it is not the fort of the plan people draw.** There were at least four Forts Velasco at or near
  this spot (S6). The **1832 Mexican work** — circular, log and sand — was abandoned on 29 June 1832
  and was a ruin by 1833. The **Texan fort**, the one with the dimensioned plan in the Nacogdoches
  Archives, was built **after February 1836** (S6, S19). **What existed in October 1835 is the
  ambiguous middle case**: Thomas F. McKinney wrote on 24 October 1835 that a long eighteen-pounder
  had that evening been mounted "on our fort at Velasco", and nothing but the 1832 work is known to
  have been standing to mount it on (S6, §3.5). **Draw the old circle with a new gun in it.**
- **"a Mexican fort was besieged and taken" — yes, but not in a town.** The brief implies a town
  with a fort in it. At the time of the battle the fort had **a customs house and one office beside
  it and every other building outside it had been burned by the garrison** (S5-Smith, S7). The
  town's name and its street grid come **after** the fort, not before.
- **"the site of the battle of Velasco in June 1832, where a Mexican fort was besieged and taken"**
  — the dates need care. The Texians moved on the night of **25 June**, fought through the night of
  25–26 June, the fort hoisted a white flag on the morning of **26 June**, and the capitulation was
  signed at the **camp at the mouth of the river on 29 June 1832** (S10, S12). TSHA's own battle
  entry says 26 June and the armistice 29 June; the 1936 markers say 26 June; one participant's
  broadside says 27 June (S8-Memorial). **26 June for the surrender, 29 June for the treaty.**
- **"TSHA's 1834 settlement list gives it 100 people" — the figure is real and it is soft.** TSHA's
  *Velasco, TX* entry attributes the hundred to what survived a **cholera epidemic**, and its
  *Brazoria County* entry uses it as a settlement size. Against it stand Perry's seven-to-ten legal
  voters in December 1834 (S15) and an April-1833 traveller who counted one public house, two
  stores and four or five dwellings (S7). **100 is plausible as a jurisdiction-flavoured figure for
  Velasco-and-Quintana-and-the-vicinity and is far too many for the buildings anyone drew.** §5.6.
- **"the *Texas Republican* … covers the river mouth" — true, and better than the brief expects.**
  The paper carries the **pilotage rules for the Brazos bar**, a **second set of rules signed by a
  second pilot at the mouth**, the **Velasco Hotel**, **Robert Clokey's tavern**, **Brown & Hoskins'
  house**, the **Velasco poll**, the **customs quarrel**, and the **Montezuma off the mouth**. Its
  Velasco material is itemised in §1c. **Thirty-four issues of it were already read and cached by
  the Brazoria researcher; this report re-read the Velasco parts rather than re-fetching them.**
- **"the *Telegraph and Texas Register*" — worth reading, and it was.** Four issues (10, 17, 31
  October and 7 November 1835) were read here. **It carries the only account found of the New
  Orleans Greys landing at Velasco and organising there, and it carries the General Council's mail
  route, which runs to Velasco by way of Quintana** (S14, §1c). **It also contradicts the
  *Texas Republican* about the date, which is a correction to the Brazoria report — see below.**
- **"any Velasco or Brazos-mouth imprint" — none was found and none is likely.** No press is
  recorded at Velasco or Quintana in 1835. The only Brazos-mouth imprint traced is the 1837 New
  York lithograph of the plat (S17).

**Three corrections to the repository.** None is made here; this file changes nothing else.

1. **`HIST-TEX-010` and `docs/COLONIES.md` §3a put Velasco in the wrong place by four miles.** The
   GNIS feature "Velasco" (1381075) at **28.96191, −95.36050** is the town platted in **1891**, four
   miles up the river, which incorporated with Freeport in 1957 (S1, S7). The 1835 town stood at
   about **28.9419, −95.3001**, in what is now **Surfside Beach**, at the old mouth. The offset is
   **3.91 miles on bearing 291°**, computed here. `HIST-TEX-010` already carries the general caveat
   that "where an 1835 settlement stood a little apart from its present town… the offset is noted
   there and not yet measured." **For Velasco it is now measured, and it is not a little apart.**
   Two Texas Historical Commission markers in Freeport make the same point on the ground: one is
   headed *Four Miles Southeast to the Original Town of Velasco* (S8).
2. **The Brazoria report's date for the New Orleans Greys is probably wrong, and its own source is
   internally inconsistent.** `brazoria.md` §4.2 puts the Greys at Mrs Long's on **20 or 21 October
   1835**, from the *Texas Republican* of 31 October, which says they were towed up to Brazoria
   "on Tuesday the 21st inst." **21 October 1835 was a Wednesday** (computed here). The *Telegraph
   and Texas Register* of 7 November 1835 says the company **arrived at Velasco on Sunday the 25th**,
   and 25 October 1835 **was** a Sunday (S14). Both papers describe the same company — Morris's,
   from New Orleans in the schooner *Columbus*. **The Brazoria dinner cannot precede the landing.
   Either the Republican's compositor dropped four days, or the Telegraph is a week out. Flagged,
   not resolved; the Brazoria report should carry the doubt.**
3. **`sim/texas.mjs` line 69 is right about Velasco and wrong about nothing** —
   `{ id:'velasco', name:'Velasco', on:'the Brazos near its mouth', x:166, y:56, weight:'port' }`.
   The brief is correct that Velasco is on the game's map as a port. **But the `weight:'port'`
   ordering that the Brazoria research flagged is worth restating from this side: Velasco at 100 is
   marked `'port'` and Brazoria at 500 is marked `'minor'`, and by every measure except the sea
   Brazoria is the larger place.** Velasco's claim is not size. It is that **nothing reached
   Brazoria, Columbia or San Felipe by sea without crossing this bar**.

## 2. Documented — where the town was, and what has happened to the ground

### 2.1 Three Velascos, and the repository is on the wrong one

**This is the first thing to settle and it is worse than Brazoria's two towns, because all three
share a name and two of them are on different rivers.**

| | **Old Velasco** | **New Velasco** | **Freeport** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Dates | settled by 1830; **1831–1875**, gone by 1886 | **platted 1891**; incorporated with Freeport 1957 | 1912 |
| Where | the **old** mouth of the Brazos, east bank | **four miles up the old channel** | on the old channel, west of New Velasco |
| Today | **Surfside Beach**, 28.9419, −95.3001 | part of **Freeport**, 28.96191, −95.36050 | Freeport |
| Water beside it | the **Freeport Harbor Channel**, dredged, between two jetties | the same channel, upstream | the same channel |
| What the Brazos does there | **nothing. The river has not run past Old Velasco since 1929** | as above | as above |

**Measured here: Old Velasco to the GNIS "Velasco" coordinate is 3.91 miles on bearing 291°.** Two
Texas markers in Freeport say the same thing, one of them in its own headline (S8). `HIST-TEX-010`
and `docs/COLONIES.md` §3a carry the 1891 coordinate. **Anything drawn from it is four miles and one
river-diversion wrong.**

**And there is a fourth trap underneath.** The Brazos itself was moved. In **1929** the Corps of
Engineers cut a new mouth for the river **about six and a half miles west** of the jetties, and the
old channel at Velasco and Quintana became the **entrance to Freeport Harbor** (Brazoria research;
confirmed here by OpenStreetMap, which labels the water at Surfside **"Freeport Harbor Channel"**
and the abandoned course a mile and a half west **"Old Brazos River"**, S20). **The modern mouth of
the Brazos is 7.0 miles south-west of Old Velasco, measured here.** Draw the river at Velasco and
you are drawing a ship channel.

### 2.2 The ground, measured — and there is no ground

to the grid:

**Four things follow.**

1. **Velasco is the flattest and lowest town in this series.** Matagorda, the previous record, sits
   at 8 to 10 ft; Brazoria at 23 to 27 ft; Columbia at 36 ft. **Velasco's whole townsite is between
   four and seven feet above the sea, and the variation across half a mile is three feet.**
2. **The only relief on the site is the fore-dune**, a ridge about 7.5 ft high two hundred feet in
   from the surf, and the hachured bank along the river which the 1858 chart also draws (S16).
   **Everything else is a sand flat.** There is no bluff, no cut bank, no rise to put a building on.
3. **The town stood between the dune and the river bank, on the strip between them.** That is what
   the 1858 chart shows and it is why the plat's "Front Street" ran along the river (S7, S16).
4. **Nothing here is defensible ground.** The fort's advantage was not height; it was that it stood
   where a vessel had to pass within musket shot to cross the bar. That is the whole military
   argument for the place, and the 1832 attackers dug their own parapet because there was no ground
   to use (S12).

## 3. Documented — the fort

**This is the brief's first question and it is the best-answered thing in the report.**

### 3.1 What the 1832 fort looked like, from four witnesses

Four men who were there or whose fathers were there describe it, and **they agree on every
structural point.** One short quotation from each.

- **William J. Russell**, second in command of the attack, writing in the Texas Almanac for 1872
  (S12): the plan and structure were well understood, **"of circular form, built of logs and sand"**
  — with strong sharpened stakes set close together all round the embankment; in the centre a
  bastion standing considerably above the outer wall, carrying a **long nine-pounder worked on a
  pivot**, ringed by a **wooden parapet about two feet high**, which protected the gun crew but
  prevented the gun from being depressed onto anything close under the wall.
- **Edwin Waller**, in his 1874 sketch (S5): a fort of circular form with a raised platform of earth
  in the centre, the artillery **"placed en barbette, so as to fire over the outer wall"**, and the
  outer wall surrounded by a fosse or ditch, with something perhaps intended for chevaux-de-frise or
  abattis.
- **John H. Brown**, whose father Henry S. Brown fought there, in 1892 (S5): the fort stood about a
  hundred and fifty yards from both the river and the Gulf shore, **"which formed a right angle"** —
  the outer wall made of parallel rows of posts six feet apart filled between with sand, earth and
  shells; inside, an embankment on which musketeers could stand and fire over without exposing more
  than their heads; in the centre an elevation of the same material enclosed by higher posts.
- **Henry Smith**, who was wounded in the head there, writing in 1836 (S8-Memorial): **"The fort was
  a complete circle enclosing but a small area"** — so full that it was completely manned; the
  nine-pounder planted on a central elevation perhaps ten feet above the musketry; the fort seeming
  to ignite instantaneously and flame like a volcano when fired on.

**Composite, and every element is in at least two witnesses:**

- a **circle**, small, enclosing little ground;
- **two concentric palisades of sharpened logs about six feet apart**, the space between filled with
  **sand, earth and shell**;
- a **banquette inside** the wall, high enough for a man to fire over it;
- a **central mound** of the same material, about **ten feet** above the interior, revetted with
  higher posts, carrying a gun **en barbette on a pivot** behind a low wooden parapet;
- a **fosse or ditch outside**, and possibly an abattis;
- and **a second, smaller gun mounted on the wall itself**, facing the landward approach (S12), the
  piece that did most of the killing.

**The one disagreement is the armament and the official return settles it.** Russell and Smith both
call the pivot gun a **long brass nine-pounder**. The return of arms taken in the fort on 26 June
1832, signed by John Austin, lists **one brass cannon, eight pounder; one small iron swivel; two
brass blunderbusses; thirty-five stand of arms in bad order** (S10). **Take the return as the truth
about the metal and the witnesses as the truth about where it was mounted.** Whether the wall gun
was the swivel or a separate small piece is not established.

### 3.2 The size, measured off Harkort's drawing

**This is the only documentary evidence for the fort's dimensions and this report measured it.**

Eduard Harkort, chief engineer of the Texas army, was at Velasco from about 8 February 1836 and
copied a scale map of the mouth of the Brazos into his journal (S6, S18). It draws **Velasco's
buildings, Quintana's buildings, the river, East Bayou and — as a ring inside a ring with a dot in
the centre — the fort.** Harkort drew his own scale bar, graduated to 900 feet English.

**Measured here on the published image:**

| Thing | Measurement | How |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Harkort's scale | **4.16 ft per pixel** | his 900-ft bar spans 216.5 px |
| **The fort circle, outside diameter** | **about 87 ft** | 21 px across |
| **The whole built town of Velasco** | **about 400 × 330 ft** | the drawn cluster of enclosures and buildings |
| Buildings drawn inside it | **about six**, in **two fenced enclosures** | counted |

**S6's own reading of the same drawing is "just less than 100-feet diameter", with about 500 feet
from the fort's seaward wall to the beach and about 200 feet from its riverside wall to the river.**
Those numbers were taken from the original photographs and should outrank the 87 ft measured here
from a published copy. **Call it 85 to 100 feet across and say which.**

**That is a very small work**, and it explains Henry Smith's "complete circle enclosing but a small
area so that it was full and completely manned". A hundred and fifty men inside an 87-foot circle
with a ten-foot mound in the middle of it have about seven square feet each.

**It also disagrees with John H. Brown.** Brown's hundred and fifty yards from both river and shore
is 450 ft each way; Harkort's drawing gives about 500 ft to the beach and 200 ft to the river.
**Brown wrote sixty years after, from his father; Harkort drew it with a scale in his hand.
Prefer Harkort and record the disagreement.**

**And it disagrees with the other candidate plan.** S6 notes an undated two-sided diagram of a
**circular fort of 60-vara radius — about 330 feet in diameter** — found in Stephen F. Austin's map
collection and suggested for Velasco in 1982. **S6's own conclusion is that it is a Mexican document
of about 1822 sent to Austin for other reasons and cannot be the design for this fort.** At 330 ft
it is four times the drawn work. **Do not draw the 60-vara circle.**

### 3.3 What was inside the fort, and what came out of it

- **Buildings.** Henry Smith says the garrison **burned every building outside the fort except
  two — a customs house and a small office** — at the beginning of the battle (S8-Memorial, S7).
  S7's timeline records a customs house, a pilot's house, a barracks, and the Mexican commander's
  quarters and office standing in 1831. **The fort itself is nowhere described as containing
  quarters**; it appears to have been a gun platform with a garrison in it.
- **The garrison.** About 100 soldiers arrived with Ugartechea in spring 1832 (S6); Russell and
  Smith both say about 150 were in it at the battle; TSHA gives 91 to 200 (S2).
- **What was taken.** The return above (S10). **Article 8 of the capitulation left the eight-pounder
  and the swivel in the fort with all the public stores, supernumerary guns and ammunition** — so
  the guns did not leave with the garrison. Article 3 names the collector, **Don Francisco Duclor**.
  Article 7 names the schooner **Brazoria** as the transport, with the *Elizabeth* to be substituted
  if she arrived. Article 5 has the troops landed **outside the bar of the Brazos Santiago**.
- **What happened to it.** The Mexican army abandoned it on 29 June 1832 (S5, S7). By April 1833 a
  traveller records the fort abandoned (S7). A May-to-September 1833 account lists, among what was
  standing, **the ruins of an old Spanish fort** (S7). In 1838 Mary Austin Holley walked out from
  the Archer House with Gen. Thomas Jefferson Green and **looked at the old fort, "the work of the
  Mexicans"** (S6, quoted). **It was still visible as a ruin six years after the battle and within
  walking distance of the hotel.**

### 3.4 Where it stood — two hypotheses, and the geophysics

**Neither the town's own tradition nor the archaeology is settled, and a layout must choose.**

| | **Hypothesis A: Monument Square** | **Hypothesis B: the west side of Block 13** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Claim | the fort stood on the open, unnumbered block the 1837 plat calls Monument Square | the fort stood on the river side of Block 13, at the foot of Fort Street; Monument Square is where the Texian dead were buried |
| For it | the block is unnumbered and reserved; it is at about the right place; **the 1980 CAR report and the 1996 Prewitt report both adopt it** (S7) | **Mrs Ellen Shannon, who lived on Lots 8–9 of Block 13, told Adele Looscan in 1898 that her ground was the fort site**, and **A. G. Follett agreed** (S6); the street beside it is called **Fort Street**; an 1845 deed sells the Archer House lots **"not including the two lots near what was called the 'Old Fort'"** (S6); **the 2020–21 geophysical survey found almost the entirety of the 1832 fort where Mrs Shannon said it was** (S6, S23) |
| Against it | **S6 says there is no direct evidence**; the 1975 identification of a brick foundation east of Monument Square as the fort was wrong (S7) | relies on two nineteenth-century recollections and on a report this project could not read (§1b) |

**The name is the argument against A.** The Battle of Velasco Memorial's own text says Monument
Square is probably named for **a monument that was planned in late 1832 by the survivors** — the
*Arkansas Advocate* of 6 February 1833 printed the San Felipe meeting's proceedings under the
headline "Monument, to be erected at the mouth of the Brazos River" — and **archaeology has found no
evidence it was ever built** (S8-Memorial). **A square named for a planned monument to the dead is
more likely to be over the graves than over the fort.**

**Prefer B, and mark it as preferred, not settled.** S6 prefers B; the geophysics supports B; the
street name supports B; the 1845 deed supports B. **But the report that contains the geophysical
plan was not read here (§1b), and the two witnesses were eighty and sixty-six years old when
Looscan interviewed them.** §8 places the fort on B and says so.

**What both hypotheses agree on:** the fort stood **immediately south-west of Monument Square, a few
hundred feet from the river bank, with the Gulf a few hundred feet to the south-east**, and the
Texian attack came in **from the north**, from the landward side, where the Texians took most of
their casualties under a gun mounted on the wall (S6, S12).

### 3.5 What was standing in October 1835 — the answer to the brief's first question

**Nothing in the record describes a fort at Velasco between 1833 and February 1836 except by
implication, and the implications are strong.**

| Date | Evidence | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1833–1835** | The fort is "abandoned"; its "ruins" are among the things standing | **A ruin, not a garrison** (S7) |
| **1836 (book)** | Holley: **a Mexican garrison was formerly stationed at Velasco; at present it is a rendezvous of the patriot troops** (S9) | **No Mexican garrison in the game's month.** The word is "formerly" |
| **17 Oct 1835** | The Consultation writes that **the cannon should be mounted and Velasco put in a state of defence as speedily as possible**, and that every precaution should be used to prevent a landing (S13, metapth80275) | **On 17 October the guns were not yet mounted** |
| **24 Oct 1835** | **Thomas F. McKinney: "we have this evening completed the mounting on our fort at Velasco a most superior long 18 pounder besides some other smaller pieces"** (S6, quoted) | **By the evening of 24 October there is a fort at Velasco with an 18-pounder on it.** S6 notes that **no fortification other than the 1832 work is known to have existed at Velasco at that date** |
| **29 Oct 1835** | McKinney again: the Mexican cruiser off the place fired one shot which fell short, and **four were fired at her from the shore** (S6) | **The guns were in action from the beach or the work within five days of being mounted** |
| **31 Oct 1835** | The express from Velasco at midnight; the *Montezuma* off the mouth; **the steamboat and schooners at Velasco preparing to pursue her** (S13, metapth80277) | **The defence of the river mouth in the game's month is naval and improvised, not a garrison in a fort** |
| **Feb 1836** | Harkort draws a circular fort (S18) | **A circular work is still there, and it is the only one drawn** |
| **after Feb 1836** | The Brazoria Committee of Safety calls for aid in erecting a **new** fort at Velasco; Bell's volunteers and Harkort build it March–July 1836 (S6, S19) | **The dimensioned polygon is 1836** |

**So, for October 1835, draw: the 1832 circular work, three and a half years derelict — its palisade
robbed for timber, its ditch silted, its central mound still there — with a long eighteen-pounder
and some smaller pieces newly mounted on it in the last week of the month, and men working on it.**
That is a specific and unusual thing to draw and it is supported from four directions.

**It is an inference that the 18-pounder went on the old work** (S6's inference, adopted here as
`I-3`). **Nobody says so.** The alternative is a fresh sand battery on the beach, which is what the
Republic's 1836 battery in plat Block 61 eventually was.

### 3.6 The 1836 fort, dimensioned — for reference, and not for October 1835

The Nacogdoches Archives plan (S19), read here, gives the Texan work. **It is later than the game's
month and must be labelled as such**, but it is the only dimensioned fort plan this town has and it
will be wanted if the arc ever runs past March 1836:

- an irregular **five-sided earthwork** with a rampart and ditch drawn as a double line;
- **North Line 168 feet; South Line 162 feet; North East Line 112 feet; East Line 112 feet**;
- an **embankment** with a stated base and depth;
- a **sally port** in the west;
- **"Battery 31 by 31"**, a square work inside the enceinte with a gun and an embrasure drawn;
- a **diamond-plan battery of about 18 by 18** in the north-east, with a gun arc;
- **"Guard Tent 12 by 18"** and a **magazine**;
- a **"Barrack Room" about 50 by 18** with a small attached room, in the south-west;
- a **scale of feet** and a **section through the parapet**.

Francis J. Haskins — the bar pilot — was asking James Morgan to settle his account **for building
the fort at Velasco** on 13 July 1836 (S6), which dates its completion. Francis C. Sheridan of the
British diplomatic service saw the battery still in place in 1840, with **an old brass 18-pounder
and three other small guns**, and a liberty pole crowned with a small beer barrel (S8-Battery).

## 4. Documented — the bar, the river mouth and the pilots

**This is the brief's third question and Velasco answers it better than any town in the series
answers anything.**

### 4.1 The bar itself

| Source | Date | Depth over the bar | Other |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Holley** (S9) | 1836 | **six feet ordinarily** | a narrow sand bank **about twenty yards wide**, **about four hundred yards from the beach**; **three fathoms of blue mud immediately outside**; blue clay under the sand and between the bar and the beach; the harbour within perfectly safe |
| **The Mesier plat's own promotional text** (S17) | Mar 1837 | **"from 7 to 8 feet water upon the bar"** | capable of great improvement with small expense; good anchorage within one mile of the city |
| **U.S. Coast Survey** (S16) | 1855–58 | **eight feet at mean low water on the range**; soundings of 5 to 9 ft drawn on the bar | twelve to thirteen feet inside; anchorage outside in four to six fathoms, soft bottom, red clay |
| **J. Brown, pilot** (S13, metapth80267) | May 1835 | not stated | anchor in **4½ fathoms in rough weather, 3½ in fine**; a **strong current setting westward** which a poor sailer cannot beat |

**Six to eight feet is the number.** Compare Matagorda's Pass Cavallo at ten to twelve. **A
sea-going schooner drawing seven feet crosses the Brazos bar on a good day; a ship does not.**

### 4.2 The pilots and how a vessel was worked in — the best single scene this town has

**Two pilots operated at the mouth in 1835 and both published their rules in the Brazoria paper.**

**F. J. Haskins, Branch Pilot, appointed by the Ayuntamiento of the jurisdiction of Columbia**,
dated Velasco 9 May 1835 and running in the paper all year (S13):

- vessels approaching the bar are **boarded if it is prudent**;
- if not, signals: **the Mexican flag at the top of the staff at high water**, and, when crossing is
  safe, **two white flags with a red ball in each, set in a range**;
- **the Mexican flag at half mast** means it is not possible to board and not prudent to cross, and
  then **no signals at all** are shown;
- he has provided **two substantial boats and a full crew**.

**J. Brown, Pilot, dated Mouth of Rio Brazos 23 May 1835** — a longer and more technical notice
"to ship-masters" (S13, metapth80267):

- **a second flagstaff has been erected at the mouth**, so that a master seeing both flags in range
  might be deceived; therefore new signals;
- at high water, **a white flag with a Mexican eagle in black in the middle** at the flagstaff; at
  half tide or low water, **the same at half mast**;
- if she is not boarded and there is plenty of water, **the flag is hauled down and two smaller
  flags set up at a distance from each other — a black ball on a white ground — and these are the
  ranges for crossing the bar without a pilot**;
- running down, **do not get to the westward of the bar**: there is generally a strong current
  setting that way and a vessel that does not sail well will find it almost impossible to come in;
- vessels with good ground tackle may anchor off the bar — **4½ fathoms in rough weather, 3½ in
  fine**;
- **when vessels are off the bar in bad weather, "a fire will be made on the Beach every night"**;
- he will continue to conduct the pilotage at the hazard of his life.

**Jeremiah Brown and Isaac Hoskins kept the tavern; Brown and Haskins were the pilots; "Brown &
Hoskins" is the house strangers lodged at (§5.4). The same four or five men are the pilotage, the
inn, the customs and the poll.** That is the whole civil establishment of Velasco.

**Haskins resigned in January 1836** because the General Council cut the pilotage rates and he could
not keep a sufficient boat and crew (S13, metapth80280). **By December 1835 he was also signing as
Post Master, Velasco** (S13), which is three years and a revolution before the U.S. post office TSHA
dates to 1846 — **a correction worth carrying: there was a post office of sorts at Velasco in the
game's winter.**

### 4.3 How cargo and people actually moved

- **Towing.** Holley: **three steamboats were in operation on the Brazos and vessels are towed over
  the bar** (S9). The Brazoria paper names one of them: the steamboat ***Laura***, McKinney's, which
  went out from the bar to help take the *Correo Mexicano* on 1 September 1835 (S6) and which
  W. H. Wharton engaged in January 1836 to carry volunteers west from the Velasco rendezvous (S13).
  **The *Lady Madison* "was towed in this morning" at Quintana on 11 October 1835** (S15).
  **A schooner under tow by a steamboat over a bar is the defining image of this place.**
- **Lightering.** The Florida lumbermen who lodged at Brown & Hoskins' in September 1835 were there
  until they **got their lumber lightered from the brig *Tremont***, which had herself been seized
  by the *Correo* off the bar (S13, metapth80276; S6). **Cargo too deep for the bar was lightered
  across it.**
- **Up-river.** Brazoria, thirty miles up by the river and fifteen by land, was the first real port;
  Bolivar, sixty miles up, was the head of tide water and the limit for anything that could cross
  the bar (S9). **Velasco is the gate, not the destination.**
- **Across the river.** Velasco and Quintana faced each other, and people crossed constantly —
  W. H. Wharton crossed from Velasco to Quintana on the morning of the first election and stayed an
  hour (S13, metapth80276). **No bridge and no ferry is named; a boat is implied.**
- **The mail.** Holley's route No. 4 ran San Felipe – Fort Bend – Orozimbo – Columbia – Brazoria –
  Velasco, 98 miles weekly, arriving Tuesday at 6 p.m. (S9). **The General Council's Texas Mail,
  advertised from 31 October 1835, runs San Felipe – Orozimbo – Columbia – Brazoria – Quintana –
  Velasco, weekly** (S14). **The 1835 route comes in through Quintana, which means the road crossed
  the river below Brazoria.** `brazoria.md` §7.3 uses Holley's version; both are real and they
  belong to different years.
- **Warehousing.** **McKinney and Williams' warehouse was at Quintana, not Velasco — sixty feet
  square** (S15, S9). In January 1832 the Mexican collector set up his office at **Brazoria** rather
  than Velasco, S6 says, **because Velasco lacked wharves and warehouses.** By October 1835 that may
  have changed and nothing read says it had. **The named warehouse on the Velasco plat, C. P. Green
  & Co.'s on Block 14 Lot 10, is ca. 1836** (S7).

**Read down that column.** A land association existed from 1832–33 and intended to plat. Edward, in
1836, calls the place a **surveyed town location** rather than a town. The first recorded lot sale is
1836. The lithograph is 1837. And in 1838 the association's own builders could not find the line of
their own Second Street. **The honest reading is that a survey of some kind existed on the ground
before 1837 — Edward says so — and that it was neither recorded nor built nor accurately staked.**

**So the town plan of Velasco as it stood in 1835 is: a surveyed intention, a landing, and a row of
houses along the river bank.** §5.2 describes the intention; §5.3 measures what is on the ground
today; **§8 draws the row of houses, because that is what Harkort saw.**

### 5.2 The 1837 plat, read off the photograph

Read here from the Beinecke photograph published by S6 (S17). **Everything in this section is 1837
and is being used as the best evidence of what the 1832–33 survey intended, not as a description of
1835.**

**The sheet.** Titled *THE CITY OF VELASCO*, dated **March 22nd 1837**, lithographed by
**P. A. Mesier & Co., 28 Wall St., N.Y.**, about 62 × 42 cm, **scale 1333⅓ feet to the inch**.

**The promotional text** (read in full; one short quotation): the site is at the mouth of the Brazos
on the Gulf, within 36 hours of steam navigation of New Orleans; there is no situation on the whole
Gulf coast believed to be more healthy; within reach of steamboat navigation on this river there are
twelve millions of acres of cotton and sugar lands; **"Velasco, must soon become the second export
City upon the continent"**; there is at present from seven to eight feet water on the bar, capable of
great improvement with small expense, and good anchorage within one mile of the city sufficient for
the whole commerce of the world. **It is a prospectus, and it should be read as one.**

**The plan.** A rectilinear grid of about **thirteen columns by seven rows** of blocks on the east
bank, with the **Brazos along the south and south-west** and the **Gulf of Mexico along the east**;
a further block of unnumbered, larger river-front parcels along the west edge; and **the Town of
Quintana platted opposite** on the other bank. Ships are drawn in the river; **Eagle Island, the
residence of the Hon. Wm. H. Wharton**, is marked upstream.

**Streets.** Two families, and the naming convention is the useful part:

- **Parallel to the water, numbered: First, Second, Third, Fourth, Fifth, Sixth** and beyond.
  First Street is nearest the water. **Front Street** runs along the river itself (S7).
- **Across, named: Custom Street, Fort Street, Gulf Street, Green Street, Canal Street, Main Street,
  Congress Street** and others not legible at this resolution. **Salt Street** is named by S7.

**Public ground, all unnumbered and all hatched on the sheet:**

| Ground | Where | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Monument Square** | between **Custom Street and Fort Street**, bounded by **First and Second Streets**, near the Gulf–river corner | **the fort/graves square**, §3.4 |
| **Bonham Promenade** | a full block-sized strip at the **extreme Gulf edge**, east of Block 13 | a sea-front esplanade |
| **Church Square** | on the western edge of the grid, toward the river | **no church was ever built on it** (§5.5) |
| **College Square** | on the same western street, five rows north | **no college** |
| **Block 61, marked "Battery"** | **the extreme corner of the Brazos and the Gulf**, south of Block 13 | **the Republic's 1836 artillery battery, drawn on the plat** |

**Blocks and lots.** Blocks are numbered; **S6 reads 8 or 10 lots to a block, and the blocks
photographed here are unmistakably 10 — two columns of five, numbered 6-7-8-9-10 up one side and
5-4-3-2-1 down the other.** **S7, working from the Earls 1996 reconstruction, says twelve lots to a
block except along Front and Gulf Streets.** **These two readings disagree and this report cannot
settle it**; the 1847 plat S7 used and the 1837 plat S6 photographed may not be the same subdivision.

**The module, measured — and honestly not calibrated.** Block-to-block pitch on the photograph is
**38.2 to 38.4 px**, consistently, in both directions; **the grid is square**. Within a pitch, the
block occupies about **33 px and the street about 5 px** — a **block:street ratio of about 6.6 : 1**.
**The absolute scale could not be recovered**: the sheet's own "1333⅓ feet to the inch" cannot be
applied to a photograph of unknown crop, and the scale bar's division value is not legible at this
resolution. **All that can be said is the ratio.** If the pitch is the 370 ft measured on the modern
ground (§5.3), the block is about **320 ft** with **50-ft streets**, and a ten-lot block gives lots
of about **160 × 64 ft**. **That chain has one unproven link in it and §7 `I-6` marks it.**

### 5.3 The grid on the ground today, measured — and whether it is the plat's

**What 370 ft is, and the honest answer is that nobody knows.**

- **133⅓ varas is 370.37 ft.** The measurement is 0.1 % off it. **That is a startlingly exact fit

  not in Mexico in varas, and the number behaves like it.**

**Reading down: TSHA's 100 does not survive as a count of people living at Velasco, and it does
survive as a count of something.**

- **Perry's seven-to-ten legal voters in December 1834 is the hardest number in the table**, it comes
  from a man who lived six miles away and had every reason to know, and it is written in a private
  letter rather than for publication. **Seven to ten free adult males.**
- **Forty votes in October 1835** is the other hard number, and the same letter explains how it gets
  that high: **ships' crews and passengers vote at Velasco.** The whole 1835 election dispute in the
  Brazoria paper is about exactly that (§1c).
- **Six buildings in February 1836** is the third, and it is a measurement.
- **Against all three, "100" would require sixteen people per building.** Even with families,
  enslaved people, boarders and a ship's crew ashore, that is too many.

**So: Velasco in October 1835 was a place of perhaps six to ten buildings and forty to seventy
people, with a floating population of sailors that could double it overnight, and a hinterland of
plantations that voted there.** TSHA's 100 is best read as **Velasco-with-Quintana-and-the-vicinity**,
which is how the 1834 county list treats Brazoria and Bolivar too.

**Against Brazoria, which the series invites:** Brazoria had fifty families, four stores, a hotel, a
printing press, four doctors and a bank. **Velasco had a bar, two pilots, a tavern, a customs house
and the ruin of a fort.** It is not a smaller version of Brazoria. **It is a different kind of
object: a gate with a few houses at it.**

### 5.7 What happened here in 1835

- **1 September 1835 — the San Felipe incident, off this bar.** The schooner *San Felipe*, carrying
  **Stephen F. Austin home from eighteen months in Mexico** and Lorenzo de Zavala, was beating in
  for Velasco with two six-pounders in her waist and cotton bales on her deck. The Mexican gunboat
  *Correo Mexicano* under Thomas M. Thompson lay off the bar; she had that same day taken the
  American brig ***Tremont*** and her cargo of lumber. The *San Felipe* slipped in over the bar.
  **Thomas F. McKinney, watching from the shore, put armed volunteers aboard his steamer *Laura*,
  swapped the passengers out, and went back over the bar after the *Correo*, taking her after a
  cannon duel and an overnight chase.** Austin's nephew records him walking the beach late into the
  night waiting for news (S6). **This is the beach at Velasco, on 1 September 1835, and it is the
  event that turned Austin for war three weeks before the dinner at Brazoria.**
- **18 August 1835.** The **Committee of Safety and Correspondence of the jurisdiction met in the
  town of Velasco**, Branch T. Archer in the chair, fifteen members present, including W. H. Jack,
  W. D. C. Hall, Henry Smith, Isaac T. Tinsley, John Hodge, Silas Dinsmore, W. H. Bynum and William
  T. Austin (S13, metapth80269). **A documented meeting, in this town, in a room nobody names.**
- **20 September 1835.** The committee resolves that **four volunteer companies be raised in the
  jurisdiction — one at Columbia, one at Velasco, one at Brazoria, and one east of the Brazos** —
  and that **W. H. Bynum open the polls at Velasco** (S13, metapth80272).
- **25 or 26 September and 5 October 1835 — the Velasco poll, and the scandal.** Notice reached
  Velasco after the polls opened; W. H. Wharton crossed to Quintana and told nobody; four or five
  men getting ready at Quintana for the campaign left without voting; two sick strangers at Brown &
  Hoskins' were pressed to vote; **forty votes were given on 5 October** and a further certificate
  of about eighteen appeared later (S13, metapth80276). **The best-documented single day in
  Velasco's 1835.**
- **11 October 1835.** **The schooner *Lady Madison* is towed in with seven guns, ammunition and
  seventy-five muskets**, ordered by the committee; the *San Felipe* is hourly expected and may
  already be off the bar (S15, S13). **The arms for the siege of Béxar come in over this bar.**
- **17 October 1835.** The Consultation advises that **the cannon be mounted and Velasco put in a
  state of defence as speedily as possible** (S13, metapth80275).
- **24 October 1835.** **McKinney completes the mounting of a long eighteen-pounder on the fort**
  (S6). §3.5.
- **25 October 1835.** **The New Orleans Greys land at Velasco**, are welcomed by the inhabitants
  with demonstrations of grateful acknowledgment, **organise and elect their officers on the spot**
  — Robert L. Morris captain, Wm. G. Cooke first lieutenant, Charles B. Bannister second, Albert M.
  Levy surgeon, Mandred Wood commissary and quartermaster (S14). They then go up to Brazoria, are
  fed at Mrs Long's, spend nearly two days getting horses, and march for San Antonio (S13). **See
  the date conflict in the Corrections.**
- **29 October 1835.** The Mexican cruiser fires a shot at Velasco that falls short; **four are
  fired back at her from the shore** (S6).
- **late October 1835.** An express rides from Velasco to Brazoria at midnight: **the *Montezuma* is
  off the mouth under the Mexican flag**; citizens mount and ride for the coast; **the steamboat and
  the schooners at Velasco prepare to go out after her** (S13, metapth80277).
- **December 1835 – January 1836.** Haskins signs as postmaster; then resigns the pilotage because
  the Council cut his rates; **Wharton orders the volunteers to rendezvous at Velasco on the 8th,
  with the steamboat *Laura* engaged to carry them west** (S13).

## 7. Inferred — things that follow but that nobody states

Each is an inference of this document and must be labelled as one if it is used.

- **`I-1`. The October-1835 town is essentially the February-1836 town.** Harkort drew six buildings
  in two enclosures three and a half months after the game's month, and nothing in the record adds
  or removes a building in between. **The load-bearing inference of the whole report.** Against it:
  the town was a rendezvous and a landing all winter, and volunteers and army stores were coming
  through it; something may have been thrown up. **Nobody says so.**
- **`I-2`. There was no street grid on the ground in October 1835** (§5.1). From Edward's "surveyed
  off", the 1836 first lot sale, the 1837 plat, and the 1838 failure to find Second Street.
  **Nobody states that the ground was unstaked.**
- **`I-3`. McKinney's eighteen-pounder of 24 October 1835 was mounted on the 1832 work.** S6's
  inference, adopted here: no other fortification is known to have existed at that date, and
  Harkort draws the circle four months later. **The alternative — a fresh sand battery on the beach
  — is equally consistent with the words "our fort".**
- **`I-4`. The 1832 fort stood on the ground the plat later called the west side of Block 13, at the
  foot of Fort Street**, and Monument Square is the burial ground (§3.4). Preferred, not settled.
- **`I-5`. The modern Surfside grid descends from the 1837 plat** (§5.3). If this is wrong, §8's
  frame is a 1950s beach subdivision and every coordinate in it is decorative.
- **`I-6`. The plat block is about 320 ft with 50-ft streets, and a lot about 160 × 64 ft.** From
  `I-5`, the measured 370-ft module and the measured 6.6 : 1 ratio. **Three measurements and one
  unproven link.**
- **`I-7`. The buildings stood on the strip between the fore-dune and the river bank**, with their
  fronts to the water. From the 1858 chart's arrangement, the plat's Front Street, Holley's pilot's
  house fronting the stream, and the fact that there is nowhere else on the site that is not
  identical sand. **Nobody describes a Velasco street front.**
- **`I-8`. The buildings were frame, not log, and the timber came in by sea.** The strongest join in
  the report and still a join: **there is no timber at the mouth of the Brazos** — the coast is
  prairie and dune; the brig *Tremont* was taken off this bar **with a cargo of lumber** in
  September 1835 and her lumber was lightered in (S13, S6); Matagorda's research documents
  **imported frame houses landed duty-free under the law of 6 April 1830** on this same coast; and
  Brazoria's research shows sawn plank coming up the Brazos from Harrisburg. **The one contrary
  datum is the 1831 inn, "a mere log house" (S6), and the fort itself, built of logs — which had to
  come from somewhere.** Draw frame for the houses and note that the logs were imported too.
- **`I-9`. There were fences.** Harkort draws two clear enclosures around the buildings, and the
  1858 chart draws fence lines. **Nobody in text mentions a Velasco fence.**
- **`I-10`. The place had no trees.** Coastal dune and salt flat, four to seven feet above the sea,
  and neither the 1858 chart nor Harkort draws one. **Velasco is the only town in this series with
  nothing growing over head height**, and that is the single biggest difference in how it should
  look. **Nobody states it.**
- **`I-11`. Quintana was the better-built side of the river in 1835.** McKinney and Williams' firm,
  their sixty-foot warehouse, their proprietor's house and their steamboat were all there, and
  every letter of theirs in 1835 is datelined Quintana. **Holley calls Quintana a town in embryo,
  which is a lower rating than she gives Velasco**, and the inference runs against her.

1. **It is small and it is a circle.** Ninety feet across with a thirty-foot mound in the middle.

2. **The gun is up in the air and it cannot shoot at its own feet.** Russell's whole plan of attack

50 × 18 barrack room, a guard tent 12 × 18 and a magazine (S19). **Do not mix the two.**

**And then put ships in it.** The buildings are the smallest part of what a student should see. **In
the game's month this place is a beach with vessels off it**: a schooner standing in for the bar
under a black-ball range; a steamboat going out to tow her; the *Lady Madison* discharging seven

**Six rules for drawing this water, all documented:**

1. **The bar is the whole point of the place and it is four hundred yards off the beach.** Draw the
   line of broken water.
2. **Six to eight feet on it.** A schooner crosses; a ship does not; a steamboat tows.
3. **The signals are the town's most distinctive furniture** — two flagstaffs, a flag with a black
   eagle, two white flags with red balls, two black balls on white ground set in a range. **Nothing
   else in this series has signals.**
4. **There is no lighthouse.** There is **a fire on the beach at night**.
5. **Quintana is across the water and it is part of the picture.** The warehouse is there, the
   merchants are there, the mail road comes through it, and men cross back and forth.
6. **Draw the water as the river and say the modern channel is not it.** `I-11` and §2.3.

### 8.5 What must be drawn *around* the place

- **Nothing over head height.** No timber, no oak, no pecan, no cane. `I-10`. **This is the
  inverse of Brazoria, where the trees come into the streets.**
- **Dune, beach grass, salt flat and shell.** The soil under the sand is **blue clay** (S9).
- **The Gulf on one side and the river on the other, meeting at a right angle** (S5-Brown). **The
  town is on a point.**
- **Coastal prairie inland**, running back toward the plantations of the lower Brazos.
- **Eagle Island, W. H. Wharton's plantation, six miles up** — drawn on the 1837 plat and named in
  the 1835 paper.
- **Peach Point and Brazoria upriver**; **Matagorda down the coast to the west**.
- **The mail road to Quintana and Brazoria**, weekly (S14).
- **`HISTORY.md`'s representation limits apply here as everywhere on the lower Brazos.** This is a
  customs port in a slaveholding district; the law of 6 April 1830 that built the fort was in part a
  law against the traffic, and the 1835 paper's Velasco columns sit beside runaway advertisements.

### 8.6 Art gaps this sketch creates

Checked against `public/assets/frontier-v1/atlas.json`, which holds **767 frames**, and against the

**Already covered — do not raise a request for these:** `wharf`, `skiff`, `ferry-raft`,

**Existing requests this town raises the priority of, with the reason it supplies:**

- **A sea-going schooner. Matagorda raised this as its highest-value request; Brazoria asked for a
  third state, under tow. Velasco is the reason all three towns need it.** Every vessel in this
  report has to be drawn crossing, lying off, being towed over or firing across **a bar**. **This is
  now a three-town request and it should be at the top of the list.**
- **A cargo lighter.** Matagorda's request. **Velasco supplies the documented instance**: the brig
  *Tremont*'s lumber lightered in over the bar in September 1835 (S13).
- **Open sea water and a horizon.** Matagorda asked for open bay water. **Velasco needs the Gulf** —
  which is a different thing again: **a bar with broken water on it and open ocean beyond.**
- **Salt-works pans.** Matagorda's request, from two salt factories documented by 1832. **Velasco is
  "celebrated for its salt works" in Holley's own sentence** (S9). **Second town, raise it.**
- **Salt marsh and shell bank.** Matagorda's request. Velasco's whole subsoil is sand over blue clay
  with shell in it — the fort's own wall was filled with shell.
- **A frame / clapboard building.** San Felipe raised it, Matagorda re-priced it, Columbia supplied
  the adjective, Brazoria made it four towns. **Velasco makes it five and the argument is the
  strongest yet: there is no timber within miles of this place** (`I-8`).
- **A two-storey building.** Columbia, Washington and Brazoria. **Velasco's is the one the Coast
  Survey pilots steered on** — a two-storey house with a balcony used as a leading mark (S16) — and
  the one the Republic of Texas met in. **Fifth town.**
- **A steamboat on the lower Brazos.** Brazoria raised it with date evidence and amended
  Washington's "do not request". **Velasco doubles the evidence: Holley says three steamboats were
  in operation on the Brazos and that vessels are towed over the bar** (S9), and the *Laura* fought
  an action off this bar on 1 September 1835 (S6). **Raise it again with the towing composition.**

**Genuinely new, and the first is the most valuable thing in this report:**

1. **A circular palisade earthwork with a central bastion.** Two concentric rings of sharpened
   posts about ninety feet across, the space between filled with sand and shell, a banquette inside,
   a ten-foot mound in the middle revetted with higher posts, a low wooden parapet round the gun,
   and a ditch outside. **Nothing in the atlas is a circular work.** Goliad asked for **rounded
   stone bastions**; this is a different object — **timber, sand, and the whole fort is the
   bastion**. **It is also the second fortification in the series and the only one with four
   independent eyewitness descriptions of its section.** Wanted in **two states**: the 1832 work
   entire, and **the same work derelict with a new gun going up on it**, which is the October-1835
   state. Stand-in: `palisade` set in a ring with `rocks` and `cannon-bronze-e` on a mound, marked
   `stand-in:` naming the request.
2. **A signal flagstaff with day-marks.** A tall staff with a halyard; **a white flag with a black
   eagle**; **two white flags each with a red ball**; and, separately, **two posts each carrying a
   black ball on a white ground**, meant to be seen in line. **No other town in this series has
   navigational furniture at all**, and this is cheap: one staff, four small flags, two ball marks.
3. **A fore-dune and a surf beach.** A low ridge of sand with beach grass on it, and the surf
   beyond. `reeds` is a freshwater marsh plant and will not serve. **Raise it as a ground/terrain
   request alongside Matagorda's open-water one.**
4. **A vessel crossing a bar.** Probably a composition note rather than a sprite: **a schooner in
   broken water with a steamboat ahead of her on a towline**, seen from the beach. **This is the
   single image that explains why Velasco existed.**
5. **A gun being moved and mounted.** Men, rope, rollers, a sheer-legs, a carriage. Documented for
   the evening of 24 October 1835 and for the *Lady Madison*'s seven guns on 11 October.

**Record the gap, do not raise the request yet:**

- **A ship's boat and a pilot boarding.** `skiff` will serve, badly.
- **A sixty-foot-square warehouse** for Quintana — the only building dimension this report has.
  Probably a scale note on the existing `storehouse`.

## 9. Verdict

**Can Velasco be drawn honestly? Yes — and it is the one place in this series where the honest
drawing is nearly empty.**

**What is solid.** **The fort is the best-documented structure in the ten towns after the Alamo
itself.** Four eyewitnesses describe its plan, its wall section, its banquette, its central mound,
its gun mount and its ditch, and they agree. **An 1836 scale drawing by a professional engineer
shows it, and its diameter can be measured off that drawing: about ninety feet.** The 1832 return of
arms taken inside it survives and is printed. The battle account gives the range from the schooner
to the bastion gun — a hundred and sixty-nine yards — and the range of the attackers' parapet —
thirty-one yards. **The bar is documented three times across twenty-two years and the three
readings agree within two feet.** The pilots' own printed rules survive with their signals, their
anchoring depths, their current and their beach fire. **The ground is measured and it is flat:
four to seven feet above the sea, with one seven-and-a-half-foot dune.** And **the same engineer's
drawing tells us how big the town was: about four hundred feet by three hundred and thirty, holding
six buildings in two fenced enclosures.**

**What would have to be invented.** **Every building position without exception.** Whether a survey
existed on the ground in 1835 at all. The plat's dimensions, its lot count, its street widths.
Which of two squares the fort stood on. The 1835 bank of the river and the 1835 line of the beach —
both of which have been dredged, jettied, eroded and, in 1929, abandoned by the river. What the fort
looked like after three and a half years of being robbed for timber. Where the custom house, the
pilot's house, the tavern and the salt works stood. **And any dimension of any building at Velasco —
the only one in the record is Quintana's warehouse, sixty feet square.**

**Is there enough to draw the town honestly? Yes, on one condition: draw what Harkort drew.** A
sand flat between a dune and a river bank; two fenced enclosures with six buildings in them, one of
them two storeys; a flagstaff; a derelict circular earthwork a few hundred feet away with men
working on it and an eighteen-pounder going up; the bar four hundred yards off the beach with
broken water on it; a schooner standing in and a steamboat going out to meet her; and Quintana's
sixty-foot warehouse across the water. **That is the whole place and every element of it is
documented. The temptation to be resisted is the 1837 lithograph, which is a beautiful sheet
advertising a city that was never built.**

**What will surprise anyone who knows Velasco from a marker:** in October 1835 it was **not a town**.
It had no street, no church, no store that anybody names, no school, no wharf of its own, no
municipal existence, and about ten men who could legally vote. **What it had was the only door into
the Brazos** — a bar with six feet of water on it, two pilots, a set of signal flags, a customs
house, a tavern kept by one of the pilots, and the ruins of a fort that a hundred and fifty Mexican
soldiers had held for eleven hours three years before. **Everything Texas fought with in the autumn
of 1835 that came by sea came in over that bar, and Austin himself came in over it on 1 September
and walked the beach that night waiting to hear whether the *Laura* had taken the *Correo*.**

**The seven things most worth doing before drawing it**, in order of value:

1. **Read Earls et al. 1996** (Prewitt RoI 94) — the comprehensive Old Velasco report, and
   specifically **its Figure 15**, the reconstruction of blocks, lots and streets that every later
   report cites. **It answers unknowns 3, 4, 5 and 7 in §6 from one figure.**
2. **Get the 2021 geophysical report past the Cloudflare challenge** (§1b). It contains both the
   archival research that located the 1832 fort and the survey that confirmed it. **It settles
   §3.4.**
3. **Get a high-resolution scan of the Mesier plat** from the Beinecke (call 796 V541 1837) or
   TSLAC Map #7573. **One sheet settles the module, the lot size, the street widths, the full block
   numbering and the extent of the public squares.**
4. **Get high-resolution images of Harkort's drawings** from the Stiftung Westfälisches
   Wirtschaftsarchiv, Dortmund (N 7/47 Nr. 2), with permission. **They are the only picture of the
   place within four months of the game's month, and this report measured them off a web JPEG.**
5. **Read Looscan 1898** — three pages, and they are the primary evidence for hypothesis B.
6. **Read the eight Béxar Archives dispatches of 1832** that S6 lists with transcriptions. They are
   Ugartechea and Bradburn building the fort, in their own words, and nobody in this project has
   read a Mexican source about it.
7. **Fix `HIST-TEX-010` and `docs/COLONIES.md` §3a.** Velasco's coordinate in the repository is
   New Velasco, **3.91 miles on bearing 291° from where the 1835 town stood** (§2.1). The 1835
   coordinate is about **28.9419, −95.3001**, in Surfside Beach. **This report does not change the
   files; it records the measurement and the reason.**
