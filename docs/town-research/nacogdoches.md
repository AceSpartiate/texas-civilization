# Nacogdoches in 1835: the Stone House, a crooked plaza on a ridge, and a town of stores

Research only. Nothing here is built, and no claim below has a `HIST-` or `FIC-` ID yet. IDs are
assigned when something is drawn. Read alongside `docs/town-research/washington.md` (the powder
wagon sent from Nacogdoches in October 1835 stopped there, and the road east runs through it),
`liberty.md` (the Liberty–Nacogdoches road) and `anahuac.md` (a precinct of the same Department of
Nacogdoches). The layout vocabulary in §9 is that of `public/bexar-layout.js` (feet, a local plane,
+y south, `river`, `roads`, `building(id, sprite, x, y, heightFeet)`), with the Stone House in the
`room`/`wall` terms of `public/alamo-layout.js`. `public/gonzales-art.js` (`GONZALES_BUILDINGS`)
was read and exists as the brief says; there is no `public/gonzales-layout.js`.

Nacogdoches is **not a start**. `docs/COLONIES.md` keeps it on the map as a place, because it was
not an empresario colony. This report is for drawing it as a place, and for knowing which trades
it really had.

Researched 2026-09-16. Every URL in §1 was opened and read on that date, except where §1b says a
source was blocked. Every measurement in §2 and §9 was computed here on that date from the sources
named.

Labels used throughout: **DOCUMENTED** (a source read here says it), **INFERRED** (follows from
documented things; numbered `I-n` in §8), **INVENTED** (nothing supports it; needed only to draw).

## The one-line answer

**In October 1835 Nacogdoches was an old Spanish town of perhaps eight hundred to a thousand people
on a low ridge between two creeks, La Nana to the east and Banita to the west, which join about a
mile south of it. El Camino Real ran along the crest of the ridge as the main street. South of that
street lay a small, crooked plaza, the Plaza Principal. Across the street from the plaza's north-east
corner stood Antonio Gil Ybarbo's two-storey stone house, which people then called the Stone House.
Nobody called it the Old Stone Fort until the 1870s. By 1835 it was owned by the town's two Mexican
judicial officers and was used as a courthouse and meeting place. West of the plaza stood the Red
House, the former Mexican commander's headquarters, which was empty of soldiers since the battle of
August 1832. At the west end of the main street stood the ruin of the old church. The town was a
mix of old Spanish houses built of upright logs and mud and new American frame houses painted
white. It had two public houses and "a number of stores" trading with farmers and with Indians who
brought in deer skins. In the game's month it was the headquarters of the war in east Texas: its
committee raised money for guns, sent riders west, lit up the town when news came that La Bahía had
been taken, and sent a wagon of powder and lead to Washington.**

Seven things, and they mostly agree:

1. **The Stone House is the one building with a documented size and a documented site.** It was
   70 ft along the Camino Real, 23 ft along Fredonia Street and 20 ft tall (TSHA, S2). It was built
   of local iron-bearing stone with adobe inner walls (S2, S17). It stood on the north side of Main
   Street at Fredonia, where a 1936 marker still stands. **Measured here:** that marker is 45 ft
   from the centre of the Main × North Fredonia intersection (§2.1). The building was demolished in
   1902. The replica on the university campus stands **6,196 ft (1.17 mi) north-north-east** of
   the site.
2. **The plaza survives in outline, but it is built over.** It is an irregular four-sided block south
   of Main Street, bounded by Main, Fredonia, Pilar and Pecan streets (S6). It "still retains its
   original form" but was "undeveloped until 1917", when the federal building was put in the middle
   of it (S6, S17). **Measured here on OpenStreetMap:** 246, 185, 268 and 225 ft between street
   centrelines, 1.19 acres, and turned about 30° from north (§3.2). It is about a third the size of
   the 120-vara square that the state's 1827 rules asked for. The town refused to rebuild itself to
   those rules (S6).
3. **The street pattern is the eighteenth-century one**, according to the National Register
   nomination (S6). Main Street follows the crest of the ridge at a bearing of 116°. North Street was
   *La Calle del Norte*, the old trail north. Pilar Street was *Calle de Nuestra Señora del Pilar*.
   **No town plat of 1779 or 1835 was found.** §3 builds the plan from the modern streets, the
   markers and the written descriptions.
4. **The ground is measured.** The plaza stands at 303–304 ft above sea level. Banita Creek's bed is
   260–265 ft, about 700–800 ft south-west of the plaza. La Nana's bed is 255–259 ft, about
   2,000 ft east. The two creeks meet at 249 ft, **5,210 ft (0.99 mi) south of the Stone House**
   (USGS 3DEP and OSM, §2.2). "A low hill" (Terán, 1828) is right. "A romantic dell, surrounded by
   woody bluffs" (Edward, 1836) is a flourish.
5. **Two eyewitnesses describe the town within a year of the game, and they agree.** Amos Parker,
   in late 1834, saw a "grotesque" mix of old Spanish log houses with "the logs standing
   perpendicular" and mud between them, and "elegant, framed houses, well finished and painted
   white". He also saw "an ancient Roman Catholic church ... now falling to decay", "two public
   houses" and "a number of stores, which carry on a brisk trade with the country people and
   Indians" (S7). Benjamin Lundy was there in July 1834. He saw a thief whipped and drummed out of
   town, and a ball given by the Mexican residents (S8).
6. **The game's month is documented in letters and newspapers, day by day** (§5.3). On 10 October
   George Antonio Nixon wrote that a meeting at his house had subscribed $2,100 for guns, and that
   35 or 40 San Augustine men and "about as maney more from this Place" were leaving that day. On
   20 October Frost Thorn, chairman of the committee, reported that the news of La Bahía had caused
   "a general illumination" of the town, and that a wagon of powder and lead would leave for
   Washington the next day (S12, S13).
7. **The trades are better documented than for any other town in the series** (§6). A census of
   1835 (a fragment) names two blacksmiths, three carpenters, a doctor, a tailor, two musicians and
   a violinist. TSHA biographies name at least eight merchants or merchant firms active in 1834–35,
   and a tavern is named in the Austin Papers. **But almost no business has a known location.** Only
   the Stone House can be placed with confidence.

### Corrections to the brief

- **"refounded 1779 by Antonio Gil Ybarbo": right.** TSHA says he "rebuilt the town" in 1779 on the
  abandoned mission site (S1, S3). The National Register nomination says he laid out a new town
  site "at a slight distance from the original mission" (S6). The 1936 mission-site marker stands
  on North Street, 2,490 ft north of the Stone House (§1a).
- **"the Old Stone Fort (Casa de Piedra)": in 1835 it was the Stone House.** TSHA calls it
  "La Casa Piedra", the Spanish name. It was "the Stone House" in 1830s English (TSHA's own battle
  entry, S4). The 1962 marker says it became "commonly known as 'the Old Stone Fort' by the late
  1870s", when a saloon there advertised under that name (S17). **The game should label it "the
  Stone House".**
- **When it was built is disputed.** The 1936 marker and a 1936 photo caption say 1779. TSHA's
  county entry says Ybarbo "probably began" it in 1779. The 1962 marker says "circa 1788-91"
  (S2, S3, S17, S18). Either way it was about fifty years old in 1835.
- **"on the plaza": across the street from it.** The plaza lay south of Main Street, between Pecan
  and Fredonia. The Stone House stood on the north side of Main at Fredonia, "at the corner of the
  plaza principal" (S6), facing the plaza's north-east corner across the street. Two sources put it
  on the west side of North Fredonia: the 1936 marker's coordinates, and TSHA's statement that
  Roberts's later store stood "across Fredonia Street east of the Old Stone Fort" (§2.1).
- **"demolished in 1902": right, with one dissent.** TSHA and both markers say 1902. The National
  Register nomination says 1901 (S6).
- **"a replica stands on the university campus, not the original site": right, and it is built of
  the original stones.** The Perkins brothers gave the stones to the Cum Concilio Club. In 1907 the
  club used them for a building on school property on Washington Square. In 1936 the state moved
  them to the campus of what was then Stephen F. Austin State Teachers College for the replica (S2,
  S17). **Measured:** the replica is 1.17 mi north-north-east of the site.
- **"Almonte counted about 3,500 in the municipality (HIST-TEX-011), the largest place in east
  Texas": right for the municipality, in 1834.** The other municipalities in the department were
  San Augustine 2,500, Johnsburg 2,000 and Liberty 1,000 (S16). **The town was much smaller, but no
  count is certain:**
  - Pavie counted "forty buildings" in 1830.
  - Edward (published 1836) says "from eight hundred to one thousand actual citizens, although it
    generally presents twice that number" because of its trade.
  - A 1835 figure of 996 permanent residents plus 140 enslaved people appears in the National
    Register nomination, citing a secondary source. It does not say whether that is the town or the
    whole municipality.
  - Holley (1836) puts the Mexican population "at Nacogdoches and in the vicinity" at about 500.
  - Newell (1838) says "about five hundred inhabitants".

  See §5.5.
- **"The Battle of Nacogdoches (August 1832) drove out the Mexican garrison under Piedras": right,
  with the detail.** The battle was fought on 2 August 1832. Piedras held the Stone House, a church
  and his headquarters, the Red House. The Texans took the Stone House, "Sim's Tavern, Thorne's
  store, and Robert's Store". Piedras left that night, and his own men handed him over on 3 August
  at John Durst's house on the Angelina. He lost 47 killed (S4, S17). **No Mexican soldier was
  stationed in the town again**, so in October 1835 there was no garrison.
- **"on El Camino Real (the Old San Antonio Road), between two creeks (La Nana and Banita)": right.**
  Main Street is the Camino Real (S6, S17). The creeks are spelled many ways: La Nana, Lanana,
  Bayou La Nana, "the Nana" (Edward), "La Nina" (Newell); and Banita, Bonita, "Bonito". **They join
  about a mile south of the town, not beside it** (measured, §2.2). Newell's "just above the
  junction" and Lundy's "unite a little below Nacogdoches" are loose.
- **"sent volunteers west and elected delegates to the Consultation": right.**
  - The Nacogdoches Independent Volunteers enlisted under Rusk on 4 October (S5, Roberts).
  - Nixon counted about 35–40 men from the town leaving on 10 October (S12).
  - Baker and Johnson, back at San Felipe on 22 October, said "about eighty men immediately
    prepared, and started to join the army at San Antonio" (S13).
  - The delegates present at the first meeting at San Felipe on 16 October were Wm. Whitaker, Samuel
    Houston, Daniel Parker, James Robinson and N. Robbins (S13, 17 October). The list of 7 November
    adds Hoffman and T. J. Rusk (S13).
- **"Sam Houston lived there": he claimed it as his residence. In October 1835 he owned no house there
  and was mostly away.**
  - He "domiciled in Nacogdoches in February 1833" (his 1849 affidavit, quoted on the 2009 marker).
  - He boarded with the Sternes and had "his first law office in Texas" in the Stone House in 1833
    (S17).
  - The house on Pilar at Pecan that is marked as his was built in 1836 by John Forbes and bought by
    Houston in 1839 (S17).
  - In the game's month he wrote from San Augustine on 5 October (S14). He issued orders from "Head
    Quarters, Texas Department of Nacogdoches" on 8 October (S13), and was at San Felipe by
    16 October (S13).
- **"Adolphus Sterne's house (1830) still stands": right.** It is the Sterne-Hoya House, 211 South
  Lanana Street, "about 1830", listed on the National Register in 1976 and thought to be the oldest
  building in the town (S1, S6). **TSHA says Sterne built it "near the confluence of La Nana Bayou
  and Bonita Creek". Measured, that is wrong:** the house is 587 ft from La Nana and 4,300 ft north
  of the confluence (§2.2). **The brief omits a second survivor.** The Durst-Taylor House on North
  Street was "built around 1835" by Joseph Durst, according to its museum's marker. The 1994 state
  marker says it is "more probable" that it dates from Durst's ownership (1827–36) than before
  (S17). **It may or may not have been standing in October 1835.**
- **Not in the brief, and it matters for drawing:** in 1835 the town had **no church in use** and
  **no garrison**. Parker saw the church as "a hideous mass of ruins" in late 1834 (S7). Sacred Heart
  marks a break in church life from 1834 until 1847, and the nomination says services were held in
  private homes until 1847 (S6, S17).

### Notes for the repository

Nothing is changed here.

1. **`HIST-TEX-010`'s Nacogdoches coordinate (31.60351, −94.65549) is good.** It lies **368 ft
   west-north-west of the Stone House site**, 280 ft from the middle of the plaza, on the ridge at
   301.5 ft (§2.1). Unlike Columbia, Goliad, Velasco and Harrisburg, **the present-day point is on
   the 1835 town centre.** No change is needed for the map or for a town drawing.
2. **`docs/town-research/washington.md` §5.3 gives the Nacogdoches wagon's lead as "150 lb". Two of
   three readings say 1,500 lb.** Thorn's committee letter of 20 October, as copied by Royall in the
   Austin Papers, says "150 lb lead and 250 lb Duponts E E E. powder". Nixon's own letter of the same
   day says "250 Pounds of Bast Powder and 1500 Pounds of Lead" (S12, pp. 194–195; Thorn's letter p. 196). The Telegraph's
   printing of Thorn's letter on 31 October reads "l,500 lbs, of lead" in the OCR (S13). The page
   image was not checked. **Flagged, not resolved; 1,500 is the better-supported figure.**
3. **`docs/COLONIES.md` §5.4's table says "about seventy to a hundred rode Oct 10".** Nixon's letter
   says 35 or 40 San Augustine men and "about as maney more from this Place" left on the 10th, and
   that "we may Count on a hundread more from San Auguteen" (S12). **So seventy to eighty rode, and a
   hundred more were expected.** Sublett's 70–80 mounted men (San Augustine) left on 19 October
   (S12, S13). The wording is loose, not wrong.
4. **The Washington–Nacogdoches road in `scripts/build-colonies-map.mjs` is a documented route in
   the game's month.** `FIC-GONZ-027` rightly calls its course invented. On 30 October 1835 the
   General Council ordered a weekly mail "From San Felipe de Austin, by Whiteside's, in Cole's
   settlement, Washington, and Nacogdoches, to San Augustin". The Postmaster General advertised for
   bids on 31 October (S13, 7 November, pp. 6 and 8). Holley's later list of routes gives other
   stages: "No. 2. From Robbins, by Albridges, Masters, and Williams, to Nacogdoches, 110 miles" and
   "No. 3. From Nacogdoches, by Steddams, San Augustine, and Robinson's, to Gaines's, on the Sabine
   river, 63 miles" (S9). This matches the later ordinance that `harrisburg.md` read in the
   12 December issue.
5. **`HIST-TEX-003` says "about fifty miles west of the Sabine".** TSHA says fifty (S1). Holley and
   Edward, both published in 1836, say sixty (S9, S10). Noted, not wrong.
6. **Outside the repository, and not to be copied into it:** TSHA's *Rusk* entry says his Nacogdoches
   volunteers "hastened to Gonzales, where his men joined Stephen F. Austin's army in preventing the
   Mexicans from seizing their cannon". That cannot be right. The fight at Gonzales was on
   2 October, and the first Nacogdoches men left on 10 October (S5, S12). TSHA's *Houston* entry has
   the Consultation "deliberat[ing] in Columbia in October". It met at San Felipe (S13). The
   Adolphus Sterne marker gives his death as 1859; TSHA says 27 March 1852 (S5, S17).

- **No plat of the town from 1779, 1827 or the 1830s was found.** The nomination (S6) describes the

  SFA** (the Blake Collection, Partin's thesis) are the places to ask.

  gallery and its lean-to on its lot. It is the best way to settle §2.1's corner.
- **W. F. Gray, *From Virginia to Texas, 1835*** (1909) was not read. HathiTrust answered with a

- **Carolyn Reeves Ericson, *Nacogdoches — Gateway to Texas: A Biographical Directory, 1773–1849***

- **No archaeology of the Stone House site or the plaza was found.** The site has been dug for a

## 2. Documented: where the town was, and what has happened to the ground

### 2.1 The site, and the coordinate check

The town stood on **"an elevated triangular site created by the confluence of the Lanana and Banita
Creeks"**, with Main Street "a meandering east-west route that traces the crest of the elevated site
above the Banita Creek" (S6). The crossroads was **El Camino Real and La Calle del Norte** (S2, S17).
Today that is **Main Street at Fredonia**, with North Street running north from the church plaza at
the west end of downtown.

**The Stone House.** The 1936 "Old Stone House" marker stands on the north side of East Main at
Fredonia, **45 ft west-north-west of the Main × North Fredonia centreline intersection**. The
building ran 70 ft along the road and 23 ft along Fredonia (S2). **Its long side faced Main Street
and its gable end faced Fredonia.** That matches the 1885 photograph, which shows a gable end with a
stair and a long side with a two-storey gallery (S18).

**The dog-leg.** North Fredonia and South Fredonia do not meet Main at the same point. South
Fredonia joins Main **56 ft west and 13 ft north** of North Fredonia (S19). The Stone House, on the
north-west corner of North Fredonia, therefore faces the plaza's north-east corner across Main Street
almost squarely. The offset may be one of the "dog-leg streets" S6 attributes to the failed 1827
straightening (`I-2`).

**What follows.**

1. **The town sits on a narrow ridge about 300–305 ft high, running north-north-west to
   south-south-east, with Main Street along it.** The ground falls **40–45 ft to Banita Creek within
   700–800 ft** of the plaza, and about the same to La Nana within 2,000 ft. The Sterne house stands
   on the eastern slope, 10 ft below the plaza.
2. **The ridge narrows southward to the confluence**, 55 ft lower, a mile south. That is S6's
   "triangular site" and Newell's "angular plain".
3. **Main Street crosses Banita at about (−1,180, −730) and La Nana at about (+2,040, +800)**
   (nearest OSM points, S19). The 1835 town lay between those two crossings, **about 3,300 ft apart
   along the road.**
4. **Terán's "low hill surrounded by forest on all sides" (1828) is the right picture.** Lundy's
   "handsome mill streams" and the "much waters" that cut a channel down a street in 1833 (S6) are
   the creeks and the slopes.

## 3. Documented: the town plan

### 3.1 Was there a plan in 1835?

**Yes, a Spanish one that the town would not change.** Ybarbo laid the town out "around the
traditional Plaza Principal" (S17) and "was careful to observe the path of the Camino Real and other
existing travel routes" (S6). He also designated a secondary plaza at present West Main and North
Streets for the church and cemetery (S6).

**In 1827 the government ordered the town remade** to the state's colonization rules (S6, from the
Blake Collection):
- a plaza 120 varas square, "exclusive of the streets", named the Plaza of the Constitution;
- streets running to the cardinal points, straight, and 20 varas wide;
- the vacant part of the church plaza reserved for a town hall, jail and school.

"The citizens of Nacogdoches, however, complained loudly when the streets were straightened and the
blocks were formed into squares. The new streets crossed some lots and left others without openings
to a street" (Partin, quoted in S6). **The effort partly failed, and the irregular plaza and
"dog-leg streets" survived** (S6).

**In 1833** "Royal Street between the small square (probably the Church Plaza) and the creek" had "a
canal" washed down its middle "that prevented carts and wagons (from) being able to pass" (S6, from
the ayuntamiento minutes of 25 May 1833). **The streets of 1835 were dirt, rutted and gullied.**

### 3.4 Street widths and blocks

**No width for any 1835 street was found.** The 1827 order to widen streets to 20 varas (55.6 ft)
implies they were narrower, and the order partly failed (S6). **Blocks are irregular, and lots are
"trapezoidal"** (S6). No lot dimension from 1835 was found.

## 5. Documented: the town in 1835

### 5.1 How it looked

**Parker, late November or early December 1834 (S7):**

> "In by-gone days, the Spaniards built a town of log houses; generally having the logs standing
> perpendicular at the sides and ends, and the space between them filled with mud; with chimneys
> made of the same materials. These look old and woe-begone. In modern times, the Americans have
> erected a number of elegant, framed houses, well finished and painted white; and these are
> scattered along among these ancient hovels."

He also saw a ruined church (quoted in §5.2). **That is the whole visual rule for the town: old
upright-log-and-mud Spanish houses, new white frame American houses scattered among them, and a
ruined church.**

Supporting descriptions:
- **Terán, 1828** (S6): the houses "of lumber, well-built and facing the street"; "a low hill
  surrounded by forest on all sides".
- **Pavie, 1830** (S6): "the forty buildings which make up this village are in large part stores
  where English is spoken; the rest are more or less dirty huts with no other floor than the ground,
  on which the Spaniards stretch out skins and multicolored rugs".
- **McReynolds**, secondary (S6): early houses of "small trees laced together with vines and coated
  with an insulating layer of mud. Roofs were shingled, and stick-and mud fireplaces".
- **The Sterne house**, c. 1830, surviving: "a wooden frame structure clad in clapboards with a
  side-gabled roof and a deep gallery on the front ... typical of the structures that would have
  been built in Nacogdoches during the period", with Creole influence from Louisiana (S6).
- **The Red House**: a frame building "covered with adobe made from the red clay common in the
  region" (S6), with "an exterior of local red clay" (S17).

silversmith, cooper, brickmaker, mason, butcher, baker, apothecary or drug store**, a **mill in town**,
and a **customs house in use**. Holley's "custom house establishment" is in a paragraph describing the
garrison era (S9). **Not found is not the same as absent.** S15 is a fragment, and Ericson's directory
(§1b) was not seen.

One near-miss is worth recording. **A thief convicted of stealing "a pair of silver sleeve buttons, in
Nacogdoches"** was whipped in July 1834 (S8). Silver goods were sold in the town; there is no evidence
anyone made them there.

### 6.4 What the game can use

- **Honest trades for invented keepers:** general store (several), tavern / public house (two),
  blacksmith and wagonmaker, carpenter, tailor, doctor, musician, land agent/surveyor, lawyer, the
  skin-buying Indian trade, and wagon freight to Natchitoches.
- **Not honest for 1835 Nacogdoches without new evidence:** gunsmith, saddler, tanner, cobbler,
  miller in town, printer before November.
- **The documented economy runs on credit and barter.** "Not one in ten sales are made for cash"
  (Almonte, S16). The stores bought deer skins, cotton and cattle, and sold goods from New Orleans
  by way of Natchitoches (S5, S7, S16).

## 8. Inferred: things that follow but that nobody states

Each must be labelled as inference if used.

- **`I-1`. The Stone House stood on the north-west corner of Main × North Fredonia**, its 70-ft side
  on Main. From the 1936 marker's coordinates (west of the Fredonia centreline, north of Main), S5's
  Roberts store "across Fredonia Street east of the Old Stone Fort", S2's dimensions along each
  street, and S6's "corner of the plaza principal".
- **`I-2`. The North/South Fredonia offset is original or dates from 1827**, one of S6's dog-legs.
  Position only.
- **`I-3`. The 1833 "Royal Street" is the Camino Real, Main Street**, since *camino real* is the royal
  road and it ran from the church plaza toward Banita Creek.
- **`I-4`. Fredonia Street was not called that in 1835.** The name commemorates the 1826 Fredonian
  rebellion against the government; a Mexican ayuntamiento would not have used it. Nobody says what
  it was called.
- **`I-5`. The Stone House had its gallery, shingled roof and exterior stair in 1835.** From the 1885
  photograph and the 1962 marker's description of the original design.
- **`I-6`. The ruined church stood on the church plaza at the west end of Main Street.** S6 puts the
  church and cemetery there. Parker saw the church "before me" as he "entered the principal street"
  from the east, which fits a church at the far end of Main.
- **`I-7`. The town held roughly 500–1,000 people and 80–150 buildings.** §5.5.
- **`I-8`. The stores clustered on Main Street near the plaza and the Stone House**, where the 1832
  battle captured "the Stone Fort, Sim's Tavern, Thorne's store, and Robert's Store" in one sweep,
  and where the "wood-frame business houses and shops along the main street" stood until the 1880s
  (S4, S6).
- **`I-9`. The Mexican residents' palisade houses lay mostly around the church plaza and on the
  slopes, and the new frame houses along Main and on the east side (Sterne) and north (Durst).**
  Weak. It comes from the marker sites of the Anglo homes. No source maps who lived where.
- **`I-10`. The creek channels near the railroad and industrial land have been straightened.**
  Unchecked.
- **`I-11`. On the night of about 19–20 October 1835 candles or lamps were set in windows across the
  town**; that is what "a general illumination" meant in 1835 usage. The illumination itself is
  documented (S12).

### 9.6 What the express road looks like here

Riders from San Felipe come **in from the west on the Camino Real**, over Banita Creek and up the
slope to the church plaza. They ride along the ridge past the plaza to the Stone House, where the
committee met, and **go on east over La Nana toward San Augustine** (S12, S13). **This is where the
news from Gonzales turns into guns, money and men for the whole east.** On 20 October a wagon goes
back west along the same road toward Washington (S12).

## 11. Verdict

**Can Nacogdoches be drawn honestly? Yes: its centre better than most towns in the series, its
buildings less well.**

**What is solid.**
- The town stood on a **measured ridge** at 300–305 ft between La Nana and Banita creeks, which **meet
  a mile south**.
- **Its main street was the Camino Real**, and the **surviving street pattern is the Spanish one**.
- **The plaza still exists in outline** (1.19 acres between centrelines, skewed about 30°) and was
  **open ground in 1835**.
- **The Stone House stood on Main at Fredonia**, facing the plaza's corner. It was **70 × 23 ft, two
  storeys, 20 ft, of ironstone rubble**, and an 1885 photograph shows it. **In 1835 it was a
  courthouse and meeting place**, owned by the town's Mexican judge and attorney.
- **The Red House stood west of the plaza on Pilar**, and **the old church was a ruin**.
- **Houses of two kinds**: old Spanish upright-log-and-mud houses and new white frame houses.
- **Two public houses and a number of stores**, with **eighteen businesses taxed in 1834**.
- **Named merchants** (Thorn, Sterne, Roberts, Logan & Raguet, Rueg, Hoffman, probably Douglass), and
  **named craftsmen** from the 1835 census: two blacksmiths, three carpenters, a tailor, a doctor and
  three musicians.
- **Two houses survive**: Sterne's (c. 1830) and Durst's (c. 1835).
- **In the game's month the town can be followed nearly day by day**: money for guns at Nixon's
  house, men riding west on 10 and 19 October, the illumination for La Bahía, and a wagon of powder
  and lead for Washington.
- **`HIST-TEX-010`'s point is on the town centre.**

**What would have to be invented.**
- Where every store, tavern, workshop and ordinary house stood.
- How many buildings there were.
- Street widths.
- The Red House's and the church's exact positions and sizes.
- The Stone House's exact corner (±100 ft) and whether its gallery is as old as 1835.
- Lot lines.
- The 1835 creek channels near the railroad.

**Is there enough to draw the town honestly? Yes, on three conditions.**
1. **Draw the Stone House, the plaza and the Camino Real as the fixed things**, from the
   measurements in §9, and put everything else loosely along Main Street and down the slopes, marked
   invented.
2. **Mix the two kinds of house as Parker saw them**, with the church a ruin at the west end.
3. **Call the building "the Stone House"**, not the Old Stone Fort. Do not give the town a garrison, a
   working church or a courthouse square.

The temptations to resist are the 1936 replica's tidy campus setting and the brick downtown of the
1890s.

**What will surprise anyone who knows Nacogdoches as "the oldest town in Texas":** in October 1835 its
famous stone building was a courthouse, not a fort; its church was a ruin; and its busiest trade was
deer skins, bought by weight from Indian hunters and baled for the wagon road to Natchitoches.

**The things most worth doing before drawing it**, in order of value:

1. **Get the 1885 Sanborn sheet** (UT Perry-Castañeda or the Library of Congress, both bot-guarded
   today). It fixes the Stone House's footprint, gallery, lean-to and corner, and probably shows the
   Red House's lot.
2. **Ask the East Texas Research Center (SFA) for the Blake Collection translation of the 1827–28
   re-planning (vol. LXVII)** and any sketch plan with it, and for Partin's *History of
   Nacogdoches*.
3. **Read the full 1835 census in the Nacogdoches Archives** (Texas State Library), and Ericson's
   *Nacogdoches — Gateway to Texas*, for the merchants' and craftsmen's occupations.
4. **Read W. F. Gray's diary** for Nacogdoches in early 1836.
5. **Check the 31 October 1835 *Telegraph* page image** (metapth47881, p. 6) for 150 or 1,500 lb of
   lead.
