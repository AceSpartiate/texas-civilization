# San Felipe de Austin in 1835 — what is known about the town plan

Research only. Nothing here is built, and no claim below has a `HIST-` or `FIC-` ID yet;
IDs are assigned when something is drawn. Read alongside `public/bexar-layout.js` and
`public/gonzales-art.js`, which are the two towns already drawn building by building and
which set the vocabulary the sketch in §7 uses.

Researched 2026-09-15. Every URL in §1 was actually opened and read on that date.

**The one-line answer.** The 1824 plat survives and can be read, so the *paper* town —
its streets by name, its plazas and its 582 numbered lots — is documented. The *built*
town of 1835 is documented as a ribbon of buildings about half a mile long on both sides
of one road, with a named order of buildings along it from a resident's memoir and a list
of about twenty named businesses tied to numbered lots by the state historic site. What is
missing is the join between the two: **no source read gives the position of any numbered
lot on the plat**, except by inference from the plat's own numbering (§3.1). That single
gap is what decides how much of a drawn San Felipe would be invention.

**What these panels are.** They are interpretive signage written by the Texas Historical
Commission for its own state historic site, standing on the ground they describe. That is
better than a compilation website and worse than a published excavation report or a deed:
they are unfootnoted, and they occasionally summarise a source rather than quote it. They
are treated here as **strongly supported** rather than **documented**, except where a panel
quotes a document or a memoir, where the quoted matter is treated on the strength of the
thing quoted.

### 1b. Sources looked for and not found

- **No published excavation report** for San Felipe de Austin was reachable. S6 establishes
  that excavation happened and where it concentrated; it gives no plan, no feature, no

- **The original Ingram plat in the General Land Office Spanish Collection** was not read.

- **The July 1, 1824 town deed**, said in search summaries to dedicate the streets, squares,
  ferry landing and timberlands, was **not read**. Do not cite it from this document.
- **The Telegraph and Texas Register printing office** has no located building. See §5.
- **A visitor's description from 1835 itself** was not found. S2 is 1828, remembered in 1900.
  William Fairfax Gray reached San Felipe on 16 February 1836 and his diary is published, but
  the relevant pages were not read here; that is the most likely place to find a description
  of the town as it stood months before the burning, and it should be read before anyone draws it.

## 2. Documented — the plat

Everything in this section is read directly off S1, the 1824 plat, unless another source is
named. The photostat is white-on-black and the hand is small; readings are marked
`[legible]` or `[probable]`.

### 2.1 The document

- Title **"Plan de la Villa de Austin"**, dated **1824**, in Spanish, scale **1 inch = 500
  varas**, sheet 37 × 26 cm, catalogued as a plat of San Felipe de Austin showing "streets by
  name, plazas, and lots and blocks by number" (S1 catalogue record, quoted). `[legible]`
- The sheet is surrounded on three sides by long manuscript notes in Spanish, written
  sideways to the map. They were **not transcribed**: at photostat resolution the running
  hand is not reliably readable. They are the single most valuable unread thing about this
  town, because a plat's marginal notes are where street widths, lot sizes, reservations and
  bearings are normally written.
- A compass ornament near the centre-right is labelled **"Norte de la Aguja"** (north of the
  needle) with an angle reading **10° 32′ Este** `[probable]`. That is a magnetic-declination
  or needle-bearing note, which means the grid's true bearing is recoverable from the
  original — but it was **not recovered here**.

### 2.2 Streets

Nine streets run parallel to the river. In order, starting at the river and going back from it:

1. **Calle del Rio** `[legible]` — nearest the Brazos, drawn in two segments, one either side
   of the creek
2. **Calle Comercio** `[legible]`
3. **Calle Primera** `[legible]`
4. **Calle Segunda** `[legible]`
5. **Calle Tercera** `[legible]`
6. **Calle Quarta** `[legible]`
7. **Calle Quinta** `[legible]`
8. **Calle Sexta** `[legible]`
9. **Calle Septima** `[legible]`

The cross streets are named, and most of the names were not readable. One was:
**Calle del Colegio** `[legible]`, running at the eastern edge of the dense lot grid, with an
annotation beside it reading **"…y esta 25 varas ancha"** — *and this [street] is 25 varas
wide* `[probable]`. At 33⅓ inches to the vara that is **69.4 feet**. This is the only street
width recovered, and it may apply only to that street.

A THC panel independently names two more streets: **Commerce and Mier y Terán Streets**,
whose junction is put "1/2 Mile East" of the wayside, in the Tejano neighbourhood
(S4, Spanish Town). Another panel says the town "patriotically named its streets to honor
other Mexican officials and leaders" and shows portraits of Manuel de Mier y Terán, Guadalupe
Victoria, Nicolás Bravo and Vicente Guerrero (S4, Founding the Town) — which is why those four
names are the likely candidates for the unread cross streets, but that is an inference (§4).
The modern street the San Felipe Methodist church stands on is **Vicaro Guerrero**
(S4, Methodist Church), which looks like a survival of one of them.

### 2.3 Plazas

Three plazas sit in a single column on one central axis, and their order relative to the
river is legible. From the river inland:

1. **Plaza de Comercio** `[legible]` — the block nearest the river, between Calle del Rio and
   Calle Comercio
2. **Plaza de la Constitución** `[probable — "Plaza de la Const…"]` — the middle of the three
3. **Plaza de Armas** `[legible]` — the furthest back from the river

The panels corroborate the first two as living place names: the 1847 Josey store was "Built
by John Crutcher in 1847 on the Plaza de Commercio" (S4, Josey, quoted), and in 1837 the town
council built its town hall / school / church "on land known as 'Constitution Square'"
(S4, Methodist Church, quoted).

**The count of squares does not agree between sources.** The plat shows three named plazas in
the central column; a THC panel says the plan "included five public squares and 582 lots"
(S4, Founding the Town, quoted); TSHA says the grid was "dominated by four large plazas"
(S3, quoted). Three, four and five are all on the record. Do not resolve this — state it.
Two further reserved blocks on the plat may be part of the higher counts: a block on the west
edge labelled **"Solar del Hospicio"** `[legible]` (the hospice or almshouse lot), and a
**"Solar de Escuela"** (school lot) named in one of the marginal notes `[probable]`.
A panel adds that the town hoped lot sales would fund "a school, church and hospital"
(S4, Founding the Town).

### 2.4 Lots and blocks

- **582 lots** in the plan (S4, Founding the Town). The plat's numbers run into the 580s,
  which is consistent.
- The dense lot grid occupies the **western** part of the plat. Each block carries two rows of
  lots, about four to a row — **eight lots to a block** `[probable]`.
- **East** of the dense grid, across the creek, the plat shows large blocks numbered **1 to 25
  in a separate series**, with their sides dimensioned in varas: **265 varas**, **270 varas**
  and **277 varas** are all legible on different blocks `[legible]`. These are outer or
  suburban blocks, not town lots. 265 varas = 736 ft; 270 = 750 ft; 277 = 769 ft.
- **No dimension for a town lot or a town block was read.** This is a real gap: see §5.

### 2.5 The water

- The Brazos is labelled **"Rio de los Brazos"** `[legible]` and is drawn running across the
  bottom of the sheet, roughly left to right, then bending away. The town is drawn on the far
  side of it from the bottom edge.
- A creek, catalogued as **"Arroyo Dulca"** [*sic*, for Arroyo Dulce] (S1 catalogue record),
  and labelled **"Arroyo Dulce"** on the sheet `[probable]`, cuts diagonally through the
  plat between the dense western grid and the eastern numbered blocks and runs into the
  Brazos. **Calle del Rio is interrupted by it**, which is the plat's own admission that the
  creek, not the grid, governs that end of the town.
- Smithwick calls the same creek, or a creek in the same place, **"Palmito"** (S2, p. 56,
  quoted below). THC calls it **"Sweet Creek (now Bullinger's Creek)"** (S9). *Arroyo Dulce*
  translates as *Sweet Creek*, so S1 and S9 agree and S2 does not. Three names, two of which
  agree; state all three.

### 2.6 What the lot numbers tell us about where the lots are

The plat's lot numbers are legible in a vertical slice, and they are not random. Reading a
column of blocks from the river inland:

- the tier of lots between **Calle del Rio and Calle Comercio** — the tier nearest the
  Brazos — carries numbers in the **520s to 580s**
- the next tier back carries numbers in the **1 to 90** range
- numbers then climb tier by tier going inland — roughly 100s, 150s–190s, 200s–250s,
  260s–310s, 330s–390s, 400s–440s — reaching about **470–510 in the tier furthest from the
  river**

So the numbering appears to start one tier back from the river, run inland to about 510, and
then finish with the riverfront tier, 520–582. `[probable — this is a reading of a photostat
and must be checked against the original before anything is built on it.]`

**Two independent facts corroborate it.** The panel for lots 537 & 538 says those lots were
"Ideally Situated Close to Commerce Square and the Brazos River" (S4, quoted) — which is
where this reading puts them. And Austin "donated lot 580 to McFarland" the ferryman in
return for the ferry (S4, Rio Brazos, quoted) — a ferryman's lot belongs on the river.

This is the single most useful thing recovered here, because it turns the panels' lot numbers
into rough positions. See §4.

## 3. Documented — the river, the road and the site

- The town stood on the **west bank of the Brazos** at the road crossing (S3, and S2, "the
  town proper, which lay along the west bank of the Brazos"). **Wikipedia says "the south
  side of the Brazos River"** (S5). Both can be true of the same place — the Brazos bends
  sharply here and the plat draws the reach running west-to-east — but the two sources use
  different words and the disagreement is left standing.
- The site was chosen for a **high, easily defensible bluff overlooking broad, fertile
  bottomlands**, with **fresh water independent of the Brazos** (S9, quoted on S4 panels).
  Austin in 1821 noted "the tall bluff, the rolling countryside, abundant timber" and fine
  water (S4, Welcome, quoting Austin). Bastrop wrote on 20 October 1823 that the spot "is
  high, free from inundations, and accessible most of the year by middle-sized vessels"
  (S4, Founding the Town, quoting Bastrop).
- **The ferry came first and the town was laid out around it.** A ferry run by the settler
  **John McFarland** carried travellers over the river; "Austin platted his town around the
  ferry crossing and donated lot 580 to McFarland in return" (S4, Rio Brazos, quoted).
- **Which road the ferry carried is disputed.** S4 (Rio Brazos) says the **Atascosito Road**.
  S3 says San Felipe stood "at the Old San Antonio Road crossing". The project's own
  `HIST-TEX-008` already records the Atascosito Road running "to the Brazos near San Felipe de
  Austin" and records that the surveyed Old San Antonio Road route is a road of about
  1750–1800 rather than an 1835 route. **Prefer Atascosito; state the disagreement.**
- A **steamboat reached the town**: the *Cayuga* "docked at Commerce Square in 1835"
  (S4, Rio Brazos, quoted). **Commerce Square therefore fronted the landing**, which is
  independent confirmation of the plat's placing it in the tier nearest the river.
- The **colonial well** was on **lot 15**, dug by the colony in 1824 (S4, Welcome; THC marker
  at the well). The site's replica well still stands.
- **Labors** — farm tracts of **177 acres** each — spread outward from the town, many along
  waterways, and most settlers lived on them, not in town (S4, Bottomland Farms).
- The modern state historic site covers about **90 acres** (S5) or "nearly 100 acres" (S7),
  and is said to include "much of the footprint" of Austin's headquarters (S5).

## 4. Documented and strongly supported — the buildings

### 4.1 The shape of the built town: a ribbon, not a grid

This is the most important single finding for anyone drawing the place, and two independent
sources say it.

> "the town was strung along either side of the road something like half a mile"
> — Noah Smithwick, 1828 (S2, p. 57)

TSHA says the same from the other direction: the town "began to sprawl westward from the
Brazos for more than a half mile along both sides of the Atascosito Road" (S3, quoted).

**The 582-lot grid was a paper plan the town never filled.** A panel records that settlers
"built their 'shanties and cabins' in the middle of what were intended to be squares and
streets" and that the ayuntamiento had to make them move to designated lots (S4, Founding the
Town, quoted). Smithwick adds that the fronts were not even a line: "every fellow built to
suit himself, only taking care to give himself plenty of room" (S2, p. 57).

**A San Felipe drawn as a filled grid would be wrong.** It is a half-mile ribbon of buildings
along one road running back from the ferry, with a cluster — "this cluster of two dozen
buildings was 'downtown'" (S4, Business District, quoted) — near Commerce Square, and
outlying houses and a Tejano neighbourhood beyond.

### 4.2 Smithwick's order of buildings, 1828

Smithwick set up as the village blacksmith in 1828 and gives the order of the buildings along
the road. This is the only building-by-building sequence found. It is 1828, written down
about 1900, and the town changed before 1835 (§4.4).

Coming in from the west end, going down toward the river:

*West end, outside the town proper*
- **Stephen F. Austin's headquarters**, "something like half a mile back from the river on the
  west bank of a little creek — Palmito — that ran into the Brazos just above the main
  village". A **double log cabin with a wide "passage" through the centre, a porch with a dirt
  floor on the front with windows opening upon it, and a chimney at each end** (S2, p. 56).
- **Joshua Parker's farm**, just above Austin's house.
- **The Ingram brothers' store** (Seth and Ira Ingram) in the same vicinity, with the lawyer
  **Hosea N. League** living near by, and the **League & Burnet law office** "in the immediate
  vicinity". Seth Ingram "laid off the town of San Felipe" (S2, pp. 56–57).
- **William "Buck" Pettus**, in "a suburban villa in the 'west end'".

*The town proper — left-hand side going down*
1. **Smithwick's own bachelor cabin** — the first house on the left
2. **The village smithy**, near it on the same side
3. **The Peyton tavern**, Jonathan C. Peyton and his wife — "the regulation double log cabin"
4. **Cooper & Chieves' saloon and billiard hall**, next below Peyton's — **"the only frame
   building in the place"**

*The town proper — right-hand side going down*
1. **Dinsmore's store** — the first house on the right entering from above
2. **Walter C. White's store**, next to it
3. **The office of the "Cotton Plant"**, the first newspaper in the colonies, and near it the
   **residence of Godwin B. Cotten** — these two "filled the space between White's store and
   the Whiteside Hotel"
4. **The Whiteside Hotel** — differing from its neighbours "only in point of elevation, it
   being a story and a half in height; through the center ran the regulation 'passage,' and at
   either end rose a huge stick and mud chimney"

*Behind the front*
- **The alcalde's office**, "a large double log house standing back some distance from the
  main thoroughfare almost immediately in the rear of the Whiteside Hotel, which building it
  much resembled". Hewn-log walls, **roofed in and abandoned at that stage** — windowless and
  floorless, and it was where the ayuntamiento sat (S2, p. 57; the same passage is quoted on
  S4, Governing the Town). Smithwick's impression was that it had been meant for a hotel.

### 4.4 What they were built of

- **1828, all of it:** "The buildings all being of unhewn logs with clapboard roofs, presented
  few distinguishing features" (S2, p. 55). The single exception Smithwick names is the
  Cooper & Chieves saloon, "the only frame building in the place".
- The THC panel on building (S4, Building the Town) sets out four materials, in this order of
  refinement: **round-log cabins** from bottomland timber, quick and sturdy but crude, which
  became outbuildings as better houses went up; **hewn log**, needing a trained carpenter with
  a broad axe; **sawn lumber** from a pit saw, for floors, siding and framing; and **brick**,
  costly, made locally or brought from Brazoria, used for cellars, foundations and chimneys —
  and the panel says plainly that though some settlers dreamed of building entirely in brick,
  **"none were ever completed."**
- The panel quotes a great-nephew of David McCormick on a house built in 1833: walls of hewed
  logs, floors and window and door frames and rafters whip-sawed, and **"the roof was of…
  split shingles"** (S4, Building the Town, quoted).
- **Two storeys is the exception.** The Whiteside Hotel was "a story and a half" and that is
  the thing that made it stand out (S2, p. 57). Nothing read describes a two-storey building
  in the town before 1836. The 1837 town hall on Constitution Square was **one storey**; its
  second floor was added in 1847 (S4, Methodist Church).
- **Chimneys:** stick-and-mud, one at each end of the better houses (S2, on both Austin's
  house and the Whiteside Hotel).
- **The double cabin with a central passage is the period plan here**, named three times by
  Smithwick — Austin's house, Peyton's tavern and the alcalde's office. This agrees with the
  project's existing `HIST-TEX-017`.

### 4.5 How big the place was

| 1836 | "some 500 residents called the town of San Felipe home" | S4, Founding the Town |
| any | "this cluster of two dozen buildings was 'downtown'" | S4, Business District |

**500, 600 and 2,500 are all in print for the mid-1830s and they are not the same measurement.**
2,500 is the municipality. 500 and 600 are the town, from two sources, and they disagree.

### 4.6 The burning

The Texian army in retreat ordered the town destroyed. Houston gave the order to Captain
Moseley Baker, Baker to Lieutenant Paschal P. Borden; Baker fired his own office and bedroom,
Borden carried a chunk of fire from the fireplace of Huff's store to the Butler hotel "just
across the street", and Huff fired his own store with his own hands. Huff remembered "the
evening and night of the 29th of March 1836" (S4, Burning of the Town, quoting Huff's 1850
recollection). **The date differs between sources:** the Welcome panel and Wikipedia say the
town was burned on **29 March 1836**; TSHA says **30 March**; the Burning panel says that by
the **morning of 30 March** it was a smouldering ruin. These reconcile as an evening fire
that burned through the night, but the printed dates differ and should be given as they stand.

## 5. Unknown — what a layout would need and no source read supplies

This section matters as much as §2–4, because everything in it would have to be invented and
registered as fiction.

1. **The bearing of the grid.** Not recovered. The plat carries a magnetic-north ornament with
   an angle, and its margins carry bearing notes, but neither was resolved. **Every drawn
   orientation is invented until the original plat is read.**
2. **Any town-lot or town-block dimension.** The only varas read are the 265/270/277-vara
   sides of the *outer* blocks east of the creek, and a probable 25-vara width for Calle del
   Colegio. **Lot frontage, lot depth, block size and the width of every other street are
   unknown.** Without them the grid's absolute scale is invented.
3. **The size and shape of the plazas.** The plat shows them as blocks in the central column;
   no dimension was read. Whether the plazas were one block or several is unknown.
4. **How many public squares there were** — three on the plat, four per TSHA, five per THC (§2.3).
5. **The position of any numbered lot, stated by a source.** §2.6 is an inference from the
   plat's own numbering, not a statement by anybody. **Which lot is which block, and which end
   of a tier a number starts from, is unknown.**
6. **Where the ferry landing was, in plan.** That the ferry crossed here and that the town was
   platted around it is documented; the landing's position relative to Commerce Square, the
   bluff and Calle del Rio is not.
7. **Where the Telegraph and Texas Register was printed.** The Baker & Borden press ran at San
   Felipe from 10 October 1835 to 24 March 1836 (S8) — exactly the window the game covers —
   and **no source read places its office on the ground**. Lot 50 is documented as the *earlier*
   Cotten press (1830–32) and later Travis's law office; it is not evidence for the Telegraph.
8. **Where Peyton's tavern, the Farmers Hotel and the land office stood relative to each
   other.** §4.3 and §4.2 are two different framings that do not join: the panels put the land
   office and Austin's first home on lot 14 in the business district west of Commerce Square,
   while Smithwick puts Austin's headquarters half a mile back from the river at the creek.
   **These are not obviously the same statement** — an empresario's house and a land office can
   move — and no source read reconciles them. Peyton's has no lot number in anything read.
9. **Any recorded dimension of any building.** Not one. No length, no width, no ceiling
   height, no roof pitch. "A story and a half", "double log cabin with a wide passage",
   "stick and mud chimney" is the whole of it. **Every building footprint would be invented.**
10. **What the archaeology found.** S6 establishes that THC crews under Jeff Durst have dug
    lot 566 since 2008, and one panel records that **lead type was excavated on lot 50**,
    which is how the printing office was confirmed there (S4, Cotten Print Shop). Beyond that
    single confirmed building position, **no excavated feature, foundation, chimney base,
    posthole, or plan is in any source read.** The eight-building 2021 "Villa de Austin"
    reconstruction represents **town lot 566 circa 1836** (S7), and its building sizes were not
    found stated anywhere; do not copy them without finding out what they are based on.
11. **Number of buildings in 1835.** No count. §4.5 gives 40–50 cabins in 1828 and "two dozen"
    downtown buildings at an unstated date, and population figures that disagree.
12. **The Spanish Town neighbourhood's plan.** Documented as a cluster of Tejano families on
    the east edge of town at Commerce and Mier y Terán Streets (S4, Spanish Town); no house,
    no count, no arrangement.
13. **Where the bluff edge runs.** "A high, easily defensible bluff overlooking broad, fertile
    bottomlands" is the only description; no height, no line.

## 6. Inferred — things that follow but that nobody states

Each is an inference of this document and must be labelled as one if it is used.

- **I-1. Commerce Square fronted the landing.** The plat puts Plaza de Comercio in the tier
  nearest the river, the *Cayuga* "docked at Commerce Square" in 1835, and lot 580 on the
  riverfront went to the ferryman. Nobody says "the ferry landed at Commerce Square", but
  three facts point at it.
- **I-2. The 520–582 lots are the riverfront tier** (§2.6). Inference from the plat's own
  numbering, corroborated by lots 537/538 being "close to… the Brazos River" and by lot 580
  being the ferryman's. **This is the load-bearing inference of the whole document** — if it is
  wrong, the whole of the position column in §4.3 is wrong.
- **I-3. The named cross streets honoured Mier y Terán, Victoria, Bravo and Guerrero.** One
  cross street named Mier y Terán is documented; a panel says the streets honoured Mexican
  officials and shows those four portraits; the modern street by the Methodist church is
  "Vicaro Guerrero". Suggestive, not stated.
- **I-4. The built ribbon ran along the Atascosito Road, which is the "main thoroughfare"
  Smithwick names.** Smithwick never names the road; TSHA says the sprawl was along the
  Atascosito Road; the ferry carried the Atascosito Road. Almost certainly the same road.
- **I-5. Calle Comercio is that road where it passes through the town.** It is the plat's
  street whose name means commerce, it fronts the Plaza de Comercio, and the business district
  developed "west of Commerce Square" — which matches a road running west from the ferry.
  **Not stated anywhere.** It is equally possible the built road ignored the plat.
- **I-6. Most of the 582 lots were never built on.** Forty or fifty cabins in 1828 and two
  dozen downtown buildings against 582 lots, plus a town described as a half-mile ribbon,
  makes this near-certain, but no source counts unbuilt lots.
- **I-7. The town's better buildings in 1835 were hewn-log dog-runs, and the round-log cabin
  had been demoted to outbuilding.** The panel says exactly that about the general
  progression; nobody says it about San Felipe in 1835 specifically.

place: the panel's own words are that farms "were where most settlers lived".

## 8. Verdict

**Can San Felipe be drawn honestly?** Yes — as a half-mile ribbon of log buildings along one
road running back from a ferry, with a named order of buildings along it, a named set of
streets and three named plazas behind it, and the explicit admission that the surveyed grid
was mostly empty paper. That is more than was known about Béxar's street plan when
`bexar-layout.js` was drawn from a panorama.

**But no coordinate in it can be real.** The grid's bearing, every lot and block dimension,
every plaza size, every building footprint and the ferry landing are all unknown (§5). A drawn
San Felipe is a reconstruction of *arrangement*, exactly as `BEXAR_LAYOUT`'s own note says of
Béxar, and it must carry the same warning.

**The three things most worth doing before drawing it**, in order of value:

1. **Read the original plat**, or a better scan than the TSLAC photostat, and transcribe its
   marginal notes. That would settle the bearing, the street widths, the lot and block sizes,
   the number of squares, and whether §2.6's lot-number reading is right. It is one document
   and it answers six of the thirteen unknowns.
2. **Read William Fairfax Gray's diary** for 16 February 1836 and after. It is the most likely
   surviving description of the town as it stood in the months this game covers, and this
   research did not read it.
3. **Get the THC excavation record for lot 566.** It is the one lot that has been dug, it is
   the lot the Farmer's Hotel stood on, and it is the basis of the reconstruction the state
   built in 2021. If any real building footprint exists for San Felipe, it is there.
