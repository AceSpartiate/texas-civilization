## The one-line answer

**Washington's plat is gone — burned or blown away in the Galveston storm of 1900 — the town it
described is gone too, and almost everything a layout needs has to be recovered from the state's
own reconstruction of the site, which is very recent, very well funded and finished ten months
ago.**

Four things follow from that, and they set the shape of this whole report:

1. **There is no plat and no grid to measure.** Every other town in this series left either a
   surviving plat (Matagorda, Liberty's Sanborn) or a surviving street grid on the ground (San
   Felipe, Mina, Columbia, Victoria). **Washington left neither.** The town was abandoned after the
   railroad passed it by in 1858, the last buildings burned in 1912, the townsite was ploughed into
   cultivated fields, and **the only known plat map of the town vanished in the 1900 storm** (S7,
   S8). OpenStreetMap over the townsite returns park service roads and foot trails and **not one
   historic street name** (S15). The trap this place has is not a moved town or a silted bay. **It
   is a missing document.**
2. **What replaces it is the Texas Historical Commission's own re-mapping**, done between 2019 and
   2025 from **tax records, deed records, historic photographs and geophysical survey**, then tested
   by **the first complete excavation of the townsite** (September 2023 – February 2024, more than
   10,000 artefacts) and built out as **seven replica structures on a landscaped layout of the
   original streets**, opened to the public **8 November 2025** (S7, S8, S9, S13). The project cost
   $57.4 million. Texas A&M's account of it is blunt about the method: *"We've essentially had to
   remap the town"* (S8, quoted). **This report treats that reconstruction as the best available
   evidence and says so every time it leans on it.** It is not a surveyed 1835 plan.
3. **Two street names, two block numbers and three lot numbers survive**, and that is the whole
   cadastral record found: **Main Street** and **Ferry Street**, crossing at the corner where
   Independence Hall stood (S5-Birth of a Republic); **Center Street**, the street the park's own
   townsite walk begins on (S6); **Block 2, Lot 7-B** (the blacksmith shop of an 1859 deed) and
   **Block 12, Lots 9 & 10** (William Rutledge's blacksmith shop, 1837–1855) (S6). **The plat
   therefore had numbered blocks running to at least 12, numbered lots running to at least 10, and
   lots subdivided into halves.** No dimension in varas or feet was found for any of them.
4. **The ground can be measured, and it is decisive.** Off USGS 3DEP the townsite is a flat shelf at
   **227–233 ft**, the Brazos water surface below it is **139 ft**, and the fall between them is
   **88 ft in about 750 ft of Ferry Street** — but it is *not* a single bluff. It is **a plateau, a
   36-ft upper bluff, a 200-ft-wide bench at 188–192 ft where the landing was, and then a 49-ft cut
   bank to the water** (§3.3). The 2023 interpretive panel's claim that the town was *built high on
   a bluff so it was unlikely to flood* is exactly right and is now quantified. **This is the fourth
   time in this series that 3DEP has settled something no document states.**

**And the central difficulty the brief names is real, and worse than the brief thinks — in both
directions.**

- **Worse, going forward.** Washington-on-the-Brazos is famous for a fortnight in March 1836, and
  **almost the entire famous fabric postdates October 1835**: the Convention, the Declaration,
  Independence Hall as a *finished* building, the town's status as seat of government, Hall and
  Lott's Tavern (opened 1836), Heath's carpentry shop as delegates' lodging (March 1836), Bailey,
  Gay and Hoxey's store (partnership formed 4 September 1836), Hatfield's Exchange (1839), Rucker's
  Drugstore (lot bought 1850), Sam Houston's presidential office (1842), Barrington (1844), the
  sawmills and the brickyard (1837), the first school (1837), and the whole steamboat trade. **A
  student walking into Washington on 1 October 1835 sees none of it.** Even the lots were not on
  sale: the town company's conveyance was executed in **January 1836** and the first auction of town
  lots was **8 January 1836** (S13, S14).
- **Better, looking at 1835 itself.** The brief treats Washington as a road node that "turned out"
  by 1 October. It was much more than that in the exact fortnight the game simulates, and the
  evidence is in a volume this repository already cites. **Washington was the place the Consultation
  of All Texas was first called to meet, on 15 October 1835** (S10); **it had been its own
  municipality since July 1835, with its own alcalde, two regidores, a síndico procurador and a
  sheriff** (S2); and in late October 1835 **it was the powder-and-lead depot on the road from
  Nacogdoches** (S10). §5 sets all of this out. **It is the single most useful thing this report
  found and the brief does not mention it.**

**What these markers are.** Two are **1969 Centennial-era State Historical Survey Committee markers**
and are treated the way the Liberty researcher treated the 1936 markers: useful, unfootnoted, and
wrong at least once (§2.2, §5.1). The rest are **modern interpretive panelling erected around 2023
as part of the reconstruction**, and they stand on the archaeology and the deed research that S6,
S7, S8 and S9 describe. **That makes them the best marker set in this series on building positions
and the weakest on anything before 1830.** They are treated as *strongly supported* rather than
*documented* except where S6 or S9 confirms them.

**The 1899 monument is the single most useful object on the site for a layout.** It is a granite
stone set by the schoolchildren of Washington County saying the Declaration was made *on this spot*,
and it stands **31 ft from the centroid of the 1970 Independence Hall replica** (§2.5). The replica
is therefore on the original site, as S6 states — and, uniquely in this series, **there is a
nineteenth-century marker corroborating a twentieth-century reconstruction's placement.**

### 1b. Sources looked for and not found

- **The Portal to Texas History could not be reached.** One attempt, as the brief directed: it
  served a "DAM texashistory.unt.edu — Validating your request" interstitial and never resolved.
  **That is eight reports in a row.** The Portal holds the digitised *Telegraph and Texas Register*
  and *Early Texas Newspapers*, and it holds at least two items that bear directly on this town — a
  narrative of Washington in the time of Jim Johnson, and a thesis on Noah T. Byars. **The lot-sale
  advertisement for the auction of 8 January 1836, if it was printed, is in that collection**, and
  it would very probably carry the number of lots, their dimensions and the street names. **It is
  the largest unopened door for Washington, exactly as it was for Matagorda and Columbia.**
- **The plat itself does not exist.** This is not a failure of searching. S7 and S8 both state that
  the only known plat map of Washington was lost in the Galveston storm of 1900, and S8 describes
  the re-mapping that had to replace it. **No amount of further work will produce this sheet.** What
  might still exist is a *copy* or a *tracing* in the Washington County deed records at Brenham, or
  a plat-book redraw made when lots changed hands in the 1840s and 1850s; the deed records were
  clearly worked through by the THC's researchers, since S6 cites an 1859 deed by block and lot.
  **Ask the Texas Historical Commission for the block-and-lot key behind the townsite reconstruction
  and this report's largest unknown closes in one email.**
- **`McFarland v. John W. Hall's Heirs`, 17 Tex. 676, was not read in full.** CourtListener answers
  with HTTP 202 behind Cloudflare and its API demands credentials; vLex is paywalled; *Texas
  Reports* volume 17 was not located on archive.org under the titles searched. **The opinion is
  public domain and it contains the date of the written agreement that preceded the January 1836
  conveyance** — which is very probably the 1835 date that S1, S7 and S13 all refer to loosely as
  "the Washington Town Company in 1835". One volume of *Texas Reports* closes §2.2.
- **The 1964–69 excavation reports were not read.** The Texas Archeological Research Laboratory,
  Texas Parks and Wildlife and UT's Texana Programs excavated here between 1964 and 1969, and
  **E. Mott Davis and James Corbin, *Archeological Investigations at Washington-on-Brazos State Park
  [Texas] in 1966*** (State Building Commission Report No. 5, Austin, 1967, 58 pp. with plates, plan
  and profile maps) is catalogued by antiquarian booksellers but is not in the Index of Texas
  Archaeology's open-access collection and was not found online. **It contains plans and profiles of
  townsite structures**, which is precisely what §6 lacks.
- **The 2023–24 excavation report was not found.** The fieldwork was done by Integrated
  Environmental Solutions of McKinney under the THC; only press accounts (S7, S8) were read. A final
  report, when it appears, will carry the building footprints and probably the block-and-lot key.
- **The Star of the Republic Museum's collections were not searched**, and the museum holds maps and
  documents of the Republic period (S7). **Nobody was contacted.**
- **The 34-ft three-dimensional map and the large-scale bronze townsite map at the Townsite Pavilion
  were not seen.** Both are the park's own best statement of the town plan, with a key identifying
  every building (S6, S9). **A photograph of either would supply more of §8 than every text source
  in §1 put together.**
- **Jack Vincent Donoghue, "Washington-on-the-Brazos" (M.A. thesis, University of Texas, 1935), was
  not read**, nor was any Washington County history. Both are the obvious next scholarly step.
- **William Fairfax Gray's diary was read only through S6's quotation.** The full text exists in
  *From Virginia to Texas, 1835* (1909) and at SMU's DeGolyer Library; the SMU page tried returned
  404 and archive.org did not surface the 1909 edition under the titles searched. **Gray is the best
  eyewitness to this town's fabric and he is quoted here at second hand.** He is February–March 1836

## 2. Documented — the plan, and why there is so little of it

### 2.1 What kind of place this was, in one paragraph

Washington is not a Mexican municipal grid like Victoria or Goliad, and not an empresario's capital
like San Felipe. **It is an Anglo speculation on a river crossing**, laid out by one man on land his
wife inherited from her father, at the point where the oldest road in the province met the last
reliable ferry on the Brazos. The reason it existed is the reason Bell's Landing existed and the
reason Matagorda existed: **somebody owned the ground where traffic had to stop.** Andrew Robinson
came with the Old Three Hundred in November 1821, was running a ferry at the La Bahía crossing by
1822, and had a hotel and a saloon there by 1830 (S1, S2, S5-Robinson). In 1831 he gave a quarter
league to his daughter Patsy and her husband **John W. Hall**; Hall bought the rest of the grant, and
in **December 1833** he surveyed and laid off a town on it (S1, S13).

### 2.2 When the town was laid out — four dates, and how to use them

This is the first question the brief asks and it does not have one clean answer. Four dates are in
play and each has a real source behind it.

| Date | What it is said to be | Source | Weight |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **December 1833** | Hall **surveyed and laid out** the town; Kenney built the first residence | S1, S13, S9 | **Strongest.** The Handbook and the park association agree, and it is the only date attached to an act of surveying |
| **1834** | "a townsite was laid out and named" | S5-Washington-on-the-Brazos (**1969 marker**) | **Weakest.** An unfootnoted Centennial-era marker, one year off the Handbook |
| **1835** | The **Washington Town Company** formed (Hall, Hoxey, Gay, Miller & Somervell) to promote lot sales; "Washington was platted in 1835" | S1, S7, S13 | **Strong for the company, loose for the platting.** S7's "platted in 1835" is a journalist's compression of the company's formation |
| **January 1836** | Hall **conveys** the undivided three-fourths of 640 acres; **first auction of town lots on 8 January 1836** | S14, S13 | **Strongest for the legal act.** A supreme-court statement of facts and a specific auction date |

**How this report resolves it.** The physical act of survey is **December 1833**; the commercial act
— the company, the printed plan, the numbered lots offered for sale — is **1835 into January 1836**.
**Both are true and they are two different things**, and that is exactly the distinction a layout
needs. **On 1 October 1835 the streets existed on the ground and the lots were not yet on sale.**
S14's phrase for the whole enterprise is the right one: the agreement was *"for the purpose of
laying off a town to be called Washington"* (quoted).

**A correction to the brief.** The brief says the plan was laid out "by John W. Hall and the
Washington Town Company". **It was laid out by Hall alone, two years before the Town Company
existed.** Hoxey, Gay, Miller and Somervell bought in to sell lots, not to lay out streets. Asa
Hoxey's contribution was the name — he came from Washington, Wilkes County, Georgia (S1).

### 2.3 The plat is lost, and this is the trap

**Method step 5 asks what trap this place has. It has this one.**

> The only known plat map of Washington-on-the-Brazos vanished during the Great Storm of 1900. (S7,
> S8, paraphrased)

Nothing read explains how a Washington County town plat came to be in Galveston in September 1900 —
presumably in the hands of an abstract company, a title attorney or a family. What matters is the
consequence: **the block-and-lot system that the deed records go on citing for another sixty years
has no surviving key.** The THC's researchers had to rebuild it from **tax rolls, deed records and
historic photographs**, then confirm it with geophysical survey and excavation (S7, S8).

Three further losses compound it:

1. **The town itself is gone.** The citizens refused the Houston and Central Texas Railroad in 1858;
   the county seat had already gone to Brenham in 1844; the place emptied over the following
   decades, and **the last buildings burned in 1912** (S5-Roads of Water, S7, S9). The townsite
   became cultivated fields.
2. **No street grid survives on the ground.** This is where Washington differs from every other town
   in the series. San Felipe, Mina, Victoria, Columbia, Liberty and Goliad all left streets that can
   be measured off OpenStreetMap; **Washington's bbox returns Park Road 12, FM 1155, a cemetery
   road, a scatter of park service drives and foot trails, and not a single historic street name**
   (S15). The modern hamlet of Washington sits about 420 ft south-west of Independence Hall and is
   a 1920s German settlement on top of the old town, not a continuation of it (S7).
3. **No Sanborn sheet exists.** Method step 2 recovered Columbia's and Liberty's plats from fire
   insurance maps. **Washington was already dying when the Sanborn Map Company began working Texas
   and was dead before it would have been worth a sheet.** None was found and none should be
   expected.

**The single practical consequence for this repository: if Washington is ever drawn, its street grid
is fiction, and it must be registered as fiction.** Not "reconstructed from a measured grid, with
invented buildings" as at Columbia. **Fiction, with two real street names in it.**

### 2.4 What of the cadastre does survive

Five facts, and they are all there is:

| Fact | Source | What it proves |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Main Street** and **Ferry Street** cross, and Independence Hall stood on that corner | S5-Birth of a Republic, quoted | Two street names; one named intersection; one building on it |
| **Ferry Street** is the La Bahía Road where it runs to the crossing | S5-Washington-on-the-Brazos, S8, S18 | **The town's principal street is a through road, not a market street** |
| **Center Street** is the street the park's townsite walk starts on | S6, S9 | A third street name — see the caution below |
| **Block 2, Lot 7-B** — the site of the replica blacksmith shop, an "old blacksmith shop" in an 1859 deed | S6 | Blocks and lots were numbered; **lots were subdivided into halves** |
| **Block 12, Lots 9 & 10** — William Rutledge's blacksmith shop, 1837–1855 | S6 | **At least twelve blocks; at least ten lots to a block** |

**The caution on Center Street.** S6 and S9 both put it at the head of the townsite walk, between
the Townsite Pavilion and the replica buildings. **Nothing read states that Center Street is a
historic name rather than a modern one**, and no 1830s document read uses it. It is carried here as
*strongly supported* and marked so in §8. Main and Ferry are on a marker that also states a
building's corner, which is a stronger footing.

**What is not known about the plat, and it is nearly everything else.** No lot dimension, no block
dimension, no street width, no vara figure, no count of blocks, no count of lots, no public square,
no reserved ground, no orientation statement, and no third street name that can be trusted. §6 lists
these plainly.

### 2.5 Ferry Street, measured — the one line of the plan that can be recovered

The park's reconstruction puts Ferry Street on the ground, the TPWD map labels it (S18), and
OpenStreetMap carries it as a foot path (way 1188475453). **It was measured here off the OSM
centreline.**

| Thing | Value | How |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Bearing** | **N 26.7° E** over the straight 991-ft run | least-squares end-to-end over four consecutive OSM vertices; individual segments run 23.8°, 25.8°, 27.7°, 27.8°, 25.2° — **a spread of 4°, i.e. a straight street with survey noise** |
| **Length, Main × Ferry to the head of the landing** | **1,296 ft** | Independence Hall replica centroid to the "Roads of Water Meet Roads of Dirt" panel at the top of the bench |
| **Length to the water** | **about 1,500 ft** | the 3DEP profile reaches 139 ft — the river surface — at 1,500 ft (§3.3) |
| **Independence Hall on the line** | **53 ft off the centreline** | i.e. the replica fronts the street, as a corner building should |
| **1899 monument to replica centroid** | **31 ft** | **the replica is on the original site** |

**Every interpretive panel and every marker of the townsite cluster falls within 70 ft of the Ferry

Rucker's, Hatfield's, Hall and Lott's, Heath's shop, the blacksmith shop, the Morris cabin — are
described by S9 as clustered around what had been Ferry Street. **The reconstructed town is a single
street running down to a ferry, and it is a quarter of a mile long.**

**What this does and does not establish.** It establishes the **alignment and length of one street**
as the state of Texas reconstructs it, to about ±4°, and it establishes that the famous building
stood at its head. **It does not establish a module, a block size, a lot size, or whether the rest
of the grid was square to Ferry Street.** Main Street's bearing is an inference (§7, `I-2`).

## 3. Documented — the river, the ferry, the bluff and the roads

### 3.1 Where the town is, and its relation to the confluence

`HIST-TEX-010` puts Washington at **30.32521, −96.15663**. Measured against the Independence Hall
replica at 30.325987, −96.156749, **that is 286 ft away.** `HIST-TEX-010` is right, and needs no
change — which is worth saying plainly after Columbia, where it was wrong by 1.95 miles.

**One figure in the Handbook does not survive measurement.** S1 places the town *"one mile
southwest of the junction"* of the Brazos and the Navasota (quoted). Off OSM the confluence is at
30.33056, −96.15447, and:

| From | To | Measured |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Independence Hall (Main × Ferry) | Brazos–Navasota confluence | **0.34 mi (1,816 ft), bearing NNE** |
| Head of the ferry landing | Brazos–Navasota confluence | **523 ft** |

**The town is a third of a mile from the junction, not a mile, and the crossing is practically at
it.** S9's phrasing is the accurate one: the town rose **just downstream of the confluence**. Two
caveats before this is treated as a correction to the Handbook: the Navasota's mouth may have
migrated in two centuries, and OSM's confluence node is a cartographic simplification of a braided
junction. **But the direction of the error is clear and it matters for the game**, because
`HIST-TEX-008` says the La Bahía road is *thought* to have crossed the Brazos at the mouth of the
Navasota. **Measured here, the crossing is 523 ft from the mouth of the Navasota. `HIST-TEX-008`'s
cautious "is thought to" can be strengthened.**

### 3.2 The ferry, the ford, and what the crossing was

- **Robinson's ferry began in 1822** and is the reason the place exists (S1, S2, S5-Robinson). The
  Handbook for the county dates it to 1822 and locates it near the Brazos–Navasota confluence — the
  same 523 ft measured above.
- **By 1830 Robinson had a ferry, a hotel and a saloon** at the crossing (S5-Robinson, 1969 marker —
  the weakest source class, but the only one that dates his buildings at all).
- **The crossing was a ford as well as a ferry.** S9 is explicit: wagons could ford the Brazos in dry
  seasons and take the ferry when the river rose. **That is the single most useful sentence about
  this crossing for a game that already has to decide whether a river can be crossed**, and it says
  the answer changed with the season at the same point.
- **The ferry ran at the foot of Ferry Street**, and the interpretive panel stands at the head of the
  landing (S6, S5-Roads of Water).
- **The La Bahía pecan** stands beside the landing, about 1,150 ft down Ferry Street and 105 ft off
  its west side. Genetic testing puts its closest relatives some 900 miles away in Mexico; it took
  root shortly after 1821 and is therefore **a tree that was already growing beside this crossing in
  October 1835** (S5-Rooted in History, S6). It was damaged by Hurricane Beryl in 2024.
- **Regular steamboat traffic is not 1835.** S6 and S5-Roads of Water make Washington the head of
  reliable riverboat navigation and describe a dozen steamboats at once — but that is the **1840s
  and 1850s**, and S5-Little Town on the River says plainly that in 1836 the town was *before*
  routine steamboat travel and goods in the stores were hard to come by. **Do not draw a steamboat
  at Washington in 1835.**

**Read that profile once and the whole site is explained.**

| Element | Elevation | Width / drop |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The town shelf** | **227–233 ft** | flat within 1 ft for the first 700 ft of Ferry Street |
| **The upper bluff** | 227 → 192 ft | **36 ft of drop in 350 ft**, 8–15 % |
| **The landing bench** | **188–192 ft** | **about 200 ft wide, and nearly level** |

A 7 × 7 grid of 3DEP points at 300-ft spacing over the townsite, in the same frame, gives the
plateau as a **nose of upland roughly 1,200 × 1,200 ft between 218 and 234 ft**, highest a little
south-east of the Main × Ferry corner (233 ft), and falling away to the north-east (the Brazos), the
north-west and the east. **It is a spur end, not a ridge and not a prairie.**

**Three things this settles.**

1. **The flood claim is true and now has a number.** S5-Little Town on the River and S13 both say the
   town was built high on a bluff and was unlikely to flood. **Eighty-eight feet above the water.**
   Compare Columbia's landing at thirty and Liberty's terrace lip. **Washington is the highest town
   above its river in this series.**
2. **The landing is a bench, not a beach and not a steps-in-the-bank affair like Bell's Landing.**
   There is a genuinely level 200-ft shelf fifty feet above the water where a ferry flat, a landing,
   wagons and stock could stand. **That is why the ferry worked here and it is a distinctive thing
   to draw.**
3. **Ferry Street is a graded descent, not a level street.** A wagon leaving Main Street is on the
   flat for 700 ft, then drops 36 ft in 350, then crosses 200 ft of bench, then goes down a 49-ft
   cut to the water. **Anything drawn on Ferry Street below about 750 ft from the corner is on a
   slope.**

*Caveat, as in every report in this series:* 3DEP is a modern surface. The cut bank is a **modern**
cut bank, the channel has moved within its bottom in two centuries, and the water surface sampled is
whatever stage the lidar caught. **The shelf, the 36-ft bluff and the bench are landforms and are
reliable; the last fifty feet to the water are not.**

### 3.4 The roads, and the distance checks

- **The La Bahía road is the town's main street.** S2 describes the Spanish road built through the
  county to link the presidio near Nacogdoches with La Bahía at Goliad; S5 says it ran where Ferry
  Street runs; S7 describes the replica buildings as lining it. `HIST-TEX-008` already carries the
  route. **The thing worth adding is that at Washington the road and the town are the same object.**
- **Holley's mail route No. 1** runs **San Felipe – Whitesides' in Cole's Settlement – Washington –
  Fantharp's – Sim's – Robbin's on the Trinity, 118 miles, weekly**, leaving San Felipe every Sunday
  at 7 a.m. (S11). It was established by the General Council over the winter of 1835–36, so it is
  just *after* the game's window — but **it is the documented shape of the road east from Washington
  and it matches `HIST-TEX-006`'s express traffic by way of Coles' Settlement exactly.**
- **Distance checks, computed from `HIST-TEX-010` coordinates:**

| Leg | Straight-line | Contemporary statement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Washington → San Felipe | **36.9 mi** | **Edward: "forty miles above San Felipe"** (S12, quoted) — good. **Holley: about fifty miles** (S11) — generous even for road miles |
| Washington → Gonzales | **96.2 mi** | — |
| Washington → Nacogdoches | **125.3 mi** | — |

  **Edward is the better source on this town's position and Holley the better one on its size.** Use
  each for what it is good at.
- **The country.** Washington County sits in the **post oak belt**, well timbered with elm, ash,
  hackberry, hickory and pecan (S2). The town had **plentiful spring water from nearby springs**
  (S1, S13, S5-Little Town on the River). **The springs are the second reason the site was chosen

### 4.2 Independence Hall in October 1835 — the case to get right

The brief asks specifically about "the unfinished building the Convention met in". Here is
everything that was found, in date order:

- **Byars came to Texas in 1835** and set up a gunsmith and blacksmith shop at Washington (S4).
- **Byars and Mercer appear as "merchants and partners" in the Washington County deed records in
  1835** (S6, reported at second hand from the THC's research).
- **The building was a two-storey frame.** William P. Zuber, who visited the Convention on 10 March
  1836: *"The house in which they sat was a two-story frame"* (S6, quoted); they used only the
  ground floor, and it had two doors.
- **The rental agreement was initiated in February 1836**; the lease ran three months from **1 March
  1836** at **$170**, taken by nine or eleven Washington merchants and professional men; Byars and
  Mercer covenanted to have the house in complete order and repair (S6, S17).
- **It was not in order.** By the delegates' arrival the roof and sides were on; **the windows and
  doors were not installed** (S17). The 2023 panel says the unfinished windows meant the delegates
  could not block the wind in freezing March weather (S5-Birth of a Republic).
- **Samuel Heath, a carpenter from Maine, probably built it** and most of the town's early buildings
  (S6), out of **post oak and cedar** (S9, quoted).
- **It was demolished in the late 1850s** and replaced by three-storey brick public houses; pieces
  were kept as relics, including a judge's gavel now in the Texas State Archives (S6).
- **The 1970 replica by Raiford Stripling stands on the original site** (S6), corroborated here by
  the 1899 monument 31 ft away (§1a, §2.5).

**What that gives October 1835, and what it does not.** It gives a building **begun by two men who
were demonstrably in the town in 1835**, on a corner whose street names are known, that was still
without windows and doors **sixteen months** after the earliest date Byars could have started it.
**It does not give a start date.** The honest statement is:

> **In October 1835 the frame at Main and Ferry was either not yet begun or was a bare frame.** A
> two-storey frame, roofed and weatherboarded but with open window and door openings, is what it
> looked like in March 1836; five months earlier it was less than that, and possibly nothing.

**That is a gift, not a problem.** A half-built two-storey frame on the corner of a town's two named
streets is a far more interesting thing to draw than a finished shrine, and **it is the truthful
October 1835 image.** §8.7 raises it as an art request.

### 4.4 What they were built of, and where the timber came from

**This is the best-answered question in the report.**

- **Post oak and cedar.** Samuel Heath, a carpenter who came from Maine, built most of the buildings
  in the original town *"of post oak and cedar"* (S9, quoted, from site manager Jonathan Failor).
  **Washington County is in the post oak belt** (S2), so the town was built from the timber standing
  on its own ground.
- **The first-generation houses were pole cabins.** Bethel Morris's 1835 dwelling is described
  exactly: **oak logs left in the round**, hewn only with **simple saddle notches to expedite its
  building**, and a **"mudcat" chimney built of sticks and clay** (S6). **That is the archetype of
  the 1835 Washington house and the park has rebuilt it.**
- **Sawn timber existed alongside logs.** S9 says of the replicas that some are made of logs and
  some of sawn wood, reflecting the originals. **But the sawmills came in 1837** — so a building of
  sawn boards in 1835 was built from lumber that had to be got in, which fits a two-storey frame
  being an unusual undertaking.
- **Brick is not 1835.** The brickyard is 1837 and the excavated handmade bricks are dated 1837
  (S9). The tavern's brick fireplace base and the intact brick floor found in 2023–24 belong to
  **late-1830s** structures (S7, S8). **No brick at Washington in October 1835.**
- **There was no stone.** Nothing read mentions any.

**In one line: in October 1835 Washington is a round-log, saddle-notched, stick-and-clay-chimneyed
town of post oak, with one unusual frame going up on the corner.**

### 4.5 How big the place was in 1835 — and three figures that cannot all be right

| Figure | Date | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **about 100 residents** | **1836** | S1, S5-Little Town on the River, S7 |
| **fewer than 200 residents** | "almost 200 years ago" | S9 |
| **200 people** | **1835** | S13 (*Washington as a major town*, park association) |
| **"already numbers fifty houses"** | printed **1836**, written after March 1836 | S11, quoted |
| **250 residents** | **1839** | S1 |
| **about 1,000** | **1852** | S7, S5-Boom Time |
| **about half the population enslaved** | Republic era | S9 |

**The problem is Holley.** Fifty houses and a hundred people cannot both be true of the same town in
the same year: that is two people to a house on a frontier where households of six and eight were
ordinary. Three explanations are available and this report cannot choose between them:

1. **Holley counted lots and frames, not households.** She was writing in 1836 about a town whose
   lots had just been auctioned; a speculative townsite in its first selling season has structures
   and stakes that nobody lives in. **This is the explanation this report finds most likely**, and
   it is exactly the picture S9 gives of a half-built town.
2. **Holley's fifty is wrong**, taken from a promoter. She was writing at a distance and her figure
   for Matagorda in the same passage (five hundred inhabitants) is also generous.
3. **The hundred is wrong**, because it is a modern round number repeated from marker to marker.

**What to use.** For October 1835, **neither figure applies directly**, because both describe the
town *after* the Convention brought people and the auction brought buyers. **Any 1835 number is an
invention**, and §7 `I-5` makes one and marks it.

### 4.6 Archaeology, and what is original

**The brief asks which standing structures are original and which are reconstruction. The answer is
blunt: none of the town is original.**

- **"None of the historic buildings — some made of logs, others of sawn wood — survived. The last of
  them burned in 1912."** (S9, paraphrased).
- **Independence Hall** is a **1970 replica by Raiford Stripling**, on the original site (S6).
- **All seven townsite structures** opened in November 2025 — Houston's office, Rucker's, Bailey Gay
  and Hoxey, Hatfield's Exchange, Hall and Lott's Tavern, Heath's carpentry shop, the blacksmith
  shop — **are replicas**, some "expressed" in skeletal frame form and some on low stilts to leave
  the archaeology visible (S6, S9).
- **The Morris cabin replica is deliberately off its original site**, because the archaeology under
  the true site was worth preserving (S9).
- **Barrington is original fabric — and it is not in the town.** Anson Jones's house was built in
  **1844** about four miles away, and **moved to Washington State Park for the Texas Centennial in
  1936** (S6). **The other structures at Barrington Living History Farm are replicas** (S9).
- **The La Bahía pecan is the only living original**, and it is the one thing on the site that was
  certainly there in October 1835 (§3.2).

**The excavations.**

| --- | --- | --- |
| **1964–69** | Texas Archeological Research Laboratory with TPWD and UT Texana Programs | Not read. **Davis and Corbin's 1966 report has plan and profile maps** (§1b) |

## 5. Documented — October 1835 at Washington, which the brief understates

**This section is the report's main contribution to the repository and it comes entirely from a
volume `HISTORY.md` already cites.**

### 5.1 Washington was the first designated seat of the Consultation, called for 15 October 1835

The Committee of San Augustine wrote to the Committee of Safety of the jurisdiction of Austin on
**22 September 1835**. Their delegates, seven in number, were to **meet in the Town of Washington on
the 15th day of October next**, vested with unlimited powers. And on the question of where the
consultation should sit, the committee was decided:

> *"that place being first designated, and generally understood, as being the place"* (S10, San
> Augustine committee to San Felipe committee, 22 September 1835, quoted)

Three more documents in the same volume confirm that this was a live question through the game's
whole window:

- **W. B. Travis to Austin, Mill Creek, 22 September 1835** — since leaving town he has been asked
  repeatedly where the convention is to be; it is uncertain whether Washington or San Felipe, both
  have been recommended, and there has been no decision. He asks Austin to have the place definitely
  designated.
- **The San Augustine committee, same day** — the objections to Washington are more easily got over
  than the prejudices that would arise from altering it to San Felipe.
- **R. R. Royall to Austin, San Felipe, 19 October 1835** — he encloses a resolution on holding the
  consultation at Washington, **one dissenting out of about twelve or fifteen**, and supposes they
  may meet there and perhaps adjourn to San Felipe.

**The Handbook's entry on the Consultation (S3) does not mention Washington at all.** It says the
meeting was originally set for 15 October and was postponed to 1 November because of the fighting,
and that thirty-one members assembled at San Felipe on 16 October. **S10 is a primary source and S3
is a secondary one, and on this point S10 wins.** The resolution is that **the 15 October meeting
was called for Washington; the war made a quorum impossible; the men who did assemble assembled at
San Felipe, where Austin and the committee already were; and the Consultation proper opened at San
Felipe on 1 November.**

**This also rescues the 1969 marker.** S5-Washington-on-the-Brazos says that in 1835 "the General
Council … met in the town". **That is wrong as written** — the Permanent Council and the General
Council sat at San Felipe. **But it is a garbled memory of something true**: Washington was the
called seat of the Consultation and was still being voted for as late as 19 October.

**Why this matters to this repository.** `HIST-TEX-006` and `HIST-TEX-014` already have Washington
"turning out" on 1 October 1835 and marching to Gonzales as fast as possible. **What they do not
have is that on that same day, delegates from as far away as San Augustine were riding toward
Washington for a meeting to be held there a fortnight later.** For a game about how news and people
moved between settlements in the first days of October 1835, **Washington is not a waypoint on the
eastern road. It is the place everybody was supposed to be going.**

### 5.2 Washington was its own municipality, with its own alcalde

**In July 1835** the voters of the new municipality of Washington elected their officers: *"Josa
Handley as alcalde, Jesse Grimes and Asa Mitchell as regidores"* (S2, quoted), with A. C. Reynolds
as síndico procurador and **John W. Hall — the town's founder — as sheriff**. The municipality had
been petitioned for after the legislature created the Brazos district in 1834, and the political
chief approved it (S2).

**A refinement to `docs/COLONIES.md`.** The file's `ceiling:` note says "Washington (then inside San
Felipe's jurisdiction)" has no 1834 figure and is not a start. **That is correct for 1834 and wrong
for the game's date.** In October 1835 Washington was a municipality in its own right with its own
ayuntamiento, and it had been for three months. The consequence for the dealing rule is unchanged —
Almonte counted in 1834, so there is still no Washington figure to deal by — but **the note should
say why**: not that Washington was a sub-district of San Felipe in 1835, but that **Almonte's census
predates the municipality by a year.**

### 5.3 Washington was the powder depot on the eastern road

- **F. Thorn, chairman, Nacogdoches, 20 October 1835**, to the committee at San Felipe: they will
  dispatch tomorrow **to Washington** a waggon containing **150 lb of lead and 250 lb of Dupont
  E E E powder**, and ask San Felipe to arrange payment of freight, which will run to eighty or a
  hundred dollars (S10).
- **R. R. Royall's endorsement, San Felipe, 28 October 1835**: the powder and lead is ordered to be
  **left at Washington for further orders** (S10).

**A waggon of powder and lead, sent 120 miles from Nacogdoches, halted at Washington while somebody
decided where the war needed it.** That is a concrete, dated, located event in the game's own
window, involving the game's own road east.

## 7. Inferred — things that follow but that nobody states

Each is marked, and each is this document's reasoning, not a source's.

- **`I-1` — the town shelf is the town.** The 3DEP grid gives a level nose of upland roughly
  1,200 × 1,200 ft at 218–234 ft, and Independence Hall stands on it near the north-east end. **A
  twelve-block town of ordinary Anglo blocks fits that shelf and very little else does.** Inference:
  **the platted town occupied the shelf and stopped where the ground fell away**, which is the
  ordinary reason an Anglo town plat is the size it is.
- **`I-2` — Main Street runs square to Ferry Street.** Ferry Street bears N 26.7° E (measured);
  Independence Hall stood on the corner of Main and Ferry (documented). **Nothing establishes that
  the crossing was a right angle.** Anglo town plats of the period normally are, and the shelf's
  long axis is roughly perpendicular to Ferry Street. **Inference: Main Street bears about
  S 63° E / N 63° W.** Mark it invented if drawn.
- **`I-3` — Main Street is the cross street and Ferry Street is the through road.** The La Bahía
  road is Ferry Street (documented); a road does not stop being a road when it enters a town. **The
  reconstructed buildings line Ferry Street**, and Hall and Lott's Tavern was the first building a
  traveller met after crossing (documented). Inference: **Ferry Street carried the trade and Main
  Street was the cross-axis**, which is the opposite of the usual arrangement and worth getting
  right.
- **`I-4` — the ordinary house was a round-log pole cabin with a stick-and-clay chimney.** One
  documented example (Morris, 1835) described as *archetypical* by the THC, plus a town built of
  post oak and cedar by one carpenter, plus no sawmill before 1837. Inference: **a dozen of them and
  one frame.**
- **`I-5` — a defensible invented population for October 1835.** Working backwards: about 100 people
  in 1836 after the Convention and the January auction (S1, S5, S7); a first house in December 1833;
  a municipality organised July 1835 with four elected officers. **Eight to fifteen households, forty
  to eighty people, and eight to fifteen buildings, in October 1835.** **That reasoning is this
  document's and the numbers are invented.** Use them only marked.
- **`I-6` — most of the platted lots were empty in October 1835.** The lots were not auctioned until
  January 1836 (documented). Inference: **whatever grid existed in October 1835 was a survey on the
  ground with buildings on a handful of lots and nothing on the rest** — stakes, blazes, a road and
  grass. **This is the image of the town, and the game already has the art for it** (§8.7).
- **`I-7` — the landing bench carried the ferry works.** A level 200-ft shelf 50 ft above the water
  at the foot of the street (measured) is where a ferry landing goes; nothing states it. Inference:
  **ferry flat, a wagon turn, stock pens and whatever Robinson's hotel and saloon amounted to, on
  the bench.**
- **`I-8` — the road down the bluff was graded or cut.** A wagon road cannot take an 11–15 % grade
  for 350 ft on raw ground for long without being dug into the slope. Inference: **a cut road down
  the bluff**, not a track.
- **`I-9` — Washington is the first Brazos crossing a rider from Nacogdoches meets, and the last
  before the road turns south-west.** `HIST-TEX-008` plus the measured 523 ft from the confluence.
  Inference: **this is a river-crossing town first and a town second**, and everything about its
  layout should say so.
- **`I-10` — the timber stood close.** The Morris cabin was built "using trees that were cleared
  from the land surrounding it" (S6, paraphrased) and the post oak belt is the county's own
  vegetation (S2). Inference: **post oak woods come up to the edge of the town shelf**, which is
  directly usable by `docs/WOODS_AND_BUILDING.md`.

not exist**; **one street, and the street is a road that is only passing through**; **a
ninety-foot bluff between the houses and the water, with a two-hundred-foot bench cut into it for
the ferry**; **a crossing that is a ford in August and a ferry in April**; **a grid of stakes with
nothing in it, because the lots do not go on sale for three months**; **a half-built two-storey
frame on the corner that nobody yet has any reason to remember**; **and a pecan tree by the landing
that is older than the town and is still alive.**

### 8.7 What must be drawn *around* the town

- **Post oak woods up to the edge of the shelf** (`I-10`, S2, S6). Washington County is post-oak
  belt, and the Morris cabin was built from trees cleared off its own lot.
- **Springs.** Two sources make the spring water a reason for the site (S1, S13). **Where they were
  is unknown** (§6.17), but they belong in the picture.
- **The Navasota coming in from the north**, 523 ft beyond the landing (measured).
- **The La Bahía road running away east toward Nacogdoches and west-south-west toward the Colorado
  crossing near La Grange and on to Goliad** — `HIST-TEX-008`'s route, and Ferry Street is its town
  section.
- **The road south to San Felipe, 36.9 miles** (measured), which is the road the express rode.
- **Cultivated ground.** "By 1835 Washington had become a supply point" serving emigrants and the
  surrounding area's increasing agriculture (S1). Fields, not plantations, in 1835 — **the cotton
  boom is the 1840s** (S5-Boom Time).
- **A bare, staked grid on three sides of the built street.** This is the most characteristic thing
  about the place and the easiest thing to get wrong.

**Genuinely missing:**

## 9. Verdict

**Can Washington be drawn honestly? Yes — but only as a very small, very specific thing, and only if
the layout is registered as fiction.**

**What is solid.** The site is solid. `HIST-TEX-010` puts Washington within 286 ft of the corner
where Independence Hall stood, which after Columbia is worth celebrating. The ground is solid and
measured: an eighty-eight-foot bluff, a flat town shelf, a two-hundred-foot landing bench, a cut
bank. The crossing is solid: Robinson's ferry from 1822, a ford in dry weather, five hundred feet
from the mouth of the Navasota, which is exactly where `HIST-TEX-008` guesses the La Bahía road
crossed. One street is solid — **Ferry Street, bearing N 26.7° E, 1,296 feet from the corner to the
landing, and it is the La Bahía road.** One corner is solid: **Main and Ferry**, with a
nineteenth-century monument and a twenty-first-century excavation agreeing on the spot. And the
materials are solid: **post oak and cedar, round logs, saddle notches, mudcat chimneys**, one
carpenter from Maine, and **no brick, no sawmill and no stone before 1837.**

**What would have to be invented is the plan.** Not some of it — **the plan.** There is no plat, no
module, no lot size, no block size, no street width, no count of blocks, no unit of measure, no
public square, and only three street names of which one may be modern. Two block numbers and three
lot numbers survive, from deeds of 1859 and from a shop that operated 1837–1855. **Every other town
in this series could be drawn from a measured or documented grid with invented buildings in it.
Washington has to be drawn the other way round: a handful of real buildings and a wholly invented
grid to stand them in.**

**And the dating is the discipline.** **Nine-tenths of what everybody knows about
Washington-on-the-Brazos is five months too late for this game.** The Convention, the Declaration,
the Hall as a building anyone would look at twice, the taverns, the stores, the drugstore, the
Exchange, the capital, the steamboats, the cotton, the brick, the school, the sawmills — **all of it
is after October 1835.** So are the lots: **the town was surveyed in 1833 and was still not selling
lots in October 1835.** Draw a staked grid with almost nothing in it.

**What the report found that the brief did not know is the best reason to care about this town.** In
the first fortnight of October 1835 — the exact window the game simulates — **Washington was the
place the Consultation of All Texas had been called to meet, on 15 October.** San Augustine elected
seven delegates to ride there. Travis wrote to Austin asking somebody to settle whether it was
Washington or San Felipe. As late as 19 October a resolution to hold it at Washington passed twelve
or fifteen to one. It never happened, because the war started at Gonzales on 2 October and nobody
could get a quorum anywhere. **A game whose whole subject is news travelling between settlements in
the first days of October 1835 has, in Washington, a town that was supposed to be the centre of
everything and was instead a half-built crossing with a waggon of gunpowder parked in it.** That is
a better story than the Declaration, and it is documented in a volume this repository already cites.

**The five things most worth doing before drawing it**, in order of value:

1. **Ask the Texas Historical Commission for the block-and-lot key behind the townsite
   reconstruction.** They rebuilt the town's cadastre from tax rolls, deed records and geophysics
   between 2019 and 2025, they cite it by block and lot, and they cast it in bronze at the Townsite
   Pavilion. **One email or one photograph of that bronze map closes half of §6.** This is the
   single highest-value action in this entire report.
2. **Read the 2023–24 excavation report when Integrated Environmental Solutions publish it, and
   find Davis and Corbin's 1966 report now.** The 1966 report has plan and profile maps of townsite
   structures and is a 58-page state publication; it is catalogued by booksellers and is not online.
   **It is the only source that might give an 1830s building footprint.**
3. **Get into the Portal to Texas History and find the advertisement for the lot auction of 8
   January 1836.** If it was printed — and a town company with four partners advertising a
   speculative auction almost certainly printed it — **it will name the number of lots, very
   probably their size, and possibly the streets.** Eight reports in a row have been stopped by that
   site, and this is the eighth time it is the largest unopened door.
4. **Read *McFarland v. John W. Hall's Heirs*, 17 Tex. 676, in a public-domain copy of *Texas
   Reports* volume 17.** It states the facts of the town company and it should date the written
   agreement that preceded the January 1836 conveyance — **which is the missing 1835 date in
   §2.2.** It may also describe the tract and the plat.
5. **Amend the repository in three small places.** (a) `docs/COLONIES.md`'s `ceiling:` on Washington
   should say that the gap is Almonte's 1834 census predating the **July 1835** creation of the
   municipality, not that Washington was a sub-district of San Felipe at the game's date. (b)
   `HIST-TEX-008`'s "is thought to have crossed the Brazos at the mouth of the Navasota" can be
   strengthened: **the crossing is measured here at 523 ft from the confluence.** (c) If Washington
   ever carries more than a road node, **`HIST-TEX-006`'s and `HIST-TEX-014`'s "the District of
   Washington is turning out" deserves a companion claim that the Consultation had been called to
   meet there on 15 October 1835** — from the same volume, the same editor and the same three weeks.
