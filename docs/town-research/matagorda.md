# Matagorda in 1835 — what is known about the town plan

Research only. Nothing here is built, and no claim below has a `HIST-` or `FIC-` ID yet;
IDs are assigned when something is drawn. Read alongside `docs/town-research/san-felipe.md`,
`docs/town-research/victoria.md` and `docs/town-research/mina.md`, which this follows in shape,
and `public/bexar-layout.js` and `public/gonzales-art.js` — the two towns already drawn building
by building, which set the vocabulary the sketch in §7 uses. (The brief for this report named
`public/gonzales-layout.js`; **no such file exists** — the Mina researcher found the same thing.
Gonzales' drawn scenery lives in `public/gonzales-art.js`, in miles rather than feet, and
`public/bexar-layout.js` is the file whose terms §7 actually uses.)

Researched 2026-09-15. Every URL in §1 was opened and read on that date. The measurements in
§2.4, §2.5, §3.3 and §3.5 were computed here on that date from the sources named.

**The one-line answer.** **Matagorda's plat survives, and this research read it.** A manuscript
*Survey of the Town of Matagorda, Texas and Environs*, drawn by the town's own surveyor Elias
Wightman and certified in April 1838 by the proprietors as "the true and original Map – Plat –
Survey or Chart of the Town and Town league" (S4, quoted), is photographed at readable resolution
and shows the whole grid: every block, every lot, the **Public Square**, the **School** block, the
**Burying Ground**, the bay-front water lots, the Colorado on one side and the Bay on the other,
and the town league divided into eight numbered strips with their dimensions written on them in
varas. No other town in this series has a surviving plat this complete — San Felipe's is a
photostat too coarse to read, Victoria's is missing, and Mina's is destroyed.

The plat and the modern ground agree. The surviving street grid, measured off OpenStreetMap,
bears **N 60.5° E / S 29.5° E** — perfectly square — on a module of about **415 ft**, which is
**150 varas** to within half a per cent; the plat's own internal scale, taken from a stated
718-vara strip width, gives **152 varas** for the same module. Each block carries **six lots in
two rows of three**, read directly off the plat. Four of the surviving street names — Wightman,
Fisher, Ingram, Lewis — are the men who signed the 1838 plat, and Austin, Market, Cypress, Caney,
Live Oak, Magnolia, Mulberry, Peach, Catalpa, Cedar, Laurel, Orleans and Center run with them.
**This town's plan is the best-recovered of the four.**

Two things are missing, and one of them is a trap.

**The buildings are the thinnest record of the four.** Not one building in Matagorda has a lot,
a block or a corner in anything read. There is a customhouse from 1831 with no location, "stores"
holding public powder with no location, a Committee Room that met on 30 September 1835 with no
location, a courthouse that is later, and a church that did not exist yet — **Christ Church, the
oldest Episcopal parish in Texas, was organised in January 1839 and its first building opened at
Easter 1841. There was no Episcopal church in Matagorda in 1835** (§4.3). What is unusually good
is **what the buildings were made of**: under Article 13 of the Mexican law of 6 April 1830,
**frame houses and lumber entered the ports of Galveston and Matagorda free of duty for four
years** (S7). On a coast with no timber, Matagorda imported its houses.

**And the water is the trap.** The Mina researcher found that modern Bastrop is the post-1836-flood
town. Matagorda's trap runs the other way: **the town stayed put and the water moved.** The
Colorado's log raft was blasted out in 1925–29; the 1929 flood swept the raft and its impounded
silt into Matagorda Bay; by 1936 the delta had grown clear across the bay to the peninsula (S13).
**The open bay that the 1838 plat draws against the town's south-east edge — the bay a schooner
crossed "in plain view of Town" in 1835 (S6, quoted) — is now land.** A Matagorda drawn from a
modern map would put the town a mile and a half inland from a water that used to be at the end of
its streets.

- **Wightman's original 1828–29 survey and his field notes were not located.** S2 says the field
  notes were held "at the Matagorda county clerk's office and museum" into the 1980s. **Nobody
  contacted either.** The 1838 plat certifies itself as "the true and original" survey, which is
  a claim about the original, not the original itself (§2.2).
- **A higher-resolution scan of the plat was not found.** The dealer photograph is about 55 pixels
  to the inch of original sheet. Large labels (*Public Square*, *School*, *Burying Ground*, *Bay*,
  *Colorado River*) are legible; block and lot numbers are legible; **street names on the plat
  are not** — and it is not certain the plat carries any (§5.3). Arader's copy of the same image
  is smaller. The original was sold and its present owner is unknown.
- **The Matagorda County Museum and the Matagorda County Historical Commission** were not
  contacted, and their own publications were read only through the USGenWeb transcription of
  *Historic Matagorda County* vol. I (S9). That book is the county's standing history and the
  obvious next source.
- **No published archaeology of the Matagorda townsite was found.** Searches turned up a great
  deal of *marine* archaeology in Matagorda Bay — around 150 vessels lost in the bay and at Pass
  Cavallo, twelve confirmed wrecks at the bay entrance, the Indianola wharves surveyed in
  1995 and 2005–07 — and **nothing on the town itself**. This may be a real absence: the 1835 town
  now lies under a living town and, toward the bay, under the modern Colorado delta.
- **Almonte's *Statistical Report on Texas* (1835) was not read in the original**, only through
  the summaries already used by `HIST-TEX-011`. The town-versus-jurisdiction split in §4.5 turns
  on it and two sources state it in opposite senses.
- **The General Land Office map database was not used.** The brief warned that it returns its
  whole 41,251-record collection for every query; that warning was taken and the time spent on
  the plat instead. GLO may well hold another Matagorda sheet.
- **Kennedy's *Texas* (1841) and *Texas in 1837* were not read.** Both are near-contemporary and
  both likely describe the town. Neither was retrievable as searchable text in the time available.

## 2. Documented — the plan

### 2.1 Who laid it out, and when — the sources disagree, as usual

- **1826, the petition and the site.** In August 1826 Elias R. Wightman, Hosea H. League, James C.
  Ludlow and Richard Matson petitioned Stephen F. Austin to establish a town at the mouth of the
  Colorado, and sent him their description of the ground: they had found, they wrote, "one of the
  most beautiful situations for the building a large commodious… town" (S9, quoted), a large
  amphitheatre with a semicircular bluff. Austin then obtained Mexican permission, arguing that a
  military post was needed to protect settlers landing inside the restricted ten-league coastal
  strip (S3, S9).
- **1827, the permission and the proprietors.** Austin secured permission in 1827 (S1, S9). The
  townsite was held in four quarter-shares. **The lists disagree.** S1 and S4 give James E. B.
  Austin, Hosea H. League, Thomas M. Duke and William Selkirk (jointly), and Elias R. Wightman.
  S9 gives James B. Austin, H. H. League, Duke and William Selkirk, and Wightman. **The 1936 town
  marker gives a different set: Stephen F. Austin, Elias R. Wightman, Hosea H. League and Ira
  Ingram** (S5-City of Matagorda). Do not resolve this. Wightman and League are in all three.
- **When the town was actually laid out is not settled.** The dealer's catalogue says Wightman
  "laid out the town in 1827" in one sentence and "originally laid out the Town of Matagorda in
  1828" in another, on the same page (S4). S1 says he laid it out in 1827 and then recruited the
  colonists. **But S2 — TSHA's own entry on Wightman — says he served as surveyor for Matagorda
  beginning in August 1829**, and the colonists he brought landed on 27 January 1829 (S9). The
  1936 marker says the town was "Founded in 1829" and calls 1826 the "projected site"
  (S5-City of Matagorda). S3 also says 1829.
  **The defensible statement is: projected 1826, permitted 1827, settled January 1829, surveyed by
  Wightman in or about 1829.** Every version puts the survey six or more years before 1835.
- **1829–30, the town government.** On 1 August 1829 the proprietors met, elected town officers
  and donated lots to certain people for services (S1, S4). The town was incorporated in 1830 (S1).
  The **Matagorda municipality** was established in 1834 (S3), and **Ira Ingram was its first
  alcalde in 1834** (S5-Ira Ingram).

### 2.2 The plat that survives — what it is, and what it is not

The sheet is a manuscript **"Survey of the Town of Matagorda, Texas and Environs"**, 38 × 24
inches, pen and ink with wash colour, laid on linen, dated **1838** (S4). The dealer attributes
the draughtsmanship to **Elias Wightman** and the certification, in a different hand, to
**Samuel Rhoads Fisher**. The certification, transcribed by the dealer and legible in outline on
the photograph, is the proprietors declaring the sheet to be "the true and original Map – Plat –
Survey or Chart of the Town and Town league of Matagorda" (S4, quoted), recording that they had
partitioned the property among themselves according to it, and depositing it with the Clerk of
the Court of Matagorda County by a resolution of the Board of Proprietors dated **26 April 1838**.
It is signed by S. Rhoads Fisher, **Seth Ingram** as President of the Board of Proprietors,
D. C. Cady as Secretary, Ira R. Lewis and Elias Wightman.

**Two things follow, and they cut in opposite directions.**

1. **The geometry is the original survey's.** The proprietors say so in as many words, and the
   surveyor who made the original signed it. The plat's grid, its block and lot divisions, its
   public ground and its relation to the river and the bay are the town as laid out — which is to
   say, the town of 1835.
2. **The ownership shown is 1838's, not 1835's.** The colour wash and the initials written across
   the blocks — **S.F.A., I.R.L., I.I., S.I., E.R.W., S.R.F.** — are the partition made in April
   1838 between Stephen F. Austin's estate, Ira R. Lewis, Ira Ingram, Seth Ingram, Elias R.
   Wightman and S. Rhoads Fisher. Two of those men (Lewis, Fisher) were not original proprietors,
   and Ira Ingram died in 1837. **Nothing on this sheet tells you who held a lot in 1835, and
   nothing on it tells you what was built.** It is a plat of a paper town with a schedule of sales
   written over it.

A third thing is worth saying plainly: **Seth Ingram, who presided over this board, is the same
Seth Ingram who platted San Felipe de Austin in 1823–24** (S4; and San Felipe's own research,
§4.2, has Smithwick naming him). The two towns in this series with the best-recovered grids were
laid out by men who worked for the same empresario, and one of them worked on both.

### 2.3 What the plat shows — read off the photograph

Readings below are from the 2,100 × 3,335-pixel photograph (S4), marked `[legible]` where the
label can be read as written and `[probable]` where the reading is inferred from partial strokes.

- **The town grid** occupies the lower-left of the sheet: a dense, close-lotted grid set at an
  angle to the sheet's own frame, bounded on the north-west by the river and on the south-east by
  the bay.
- **"Colorado River"** `[legible]`, written along the channel that comes down the left of the sheet
  from the north and bends away to the south-west below the town.
- **"Bay"** `[legible]`, written in open water immediately beyond the grid's south-east edge. There
  is no intervening land drawn. **The bay is at the end of the streets.**
- **"Bayou"** `[probable]`, on a watercourse entering from the south-west and running along the
  grid's south-western corner into the bay. A second small watercourse cuts diagonally into the
  grid from the bay side and is drawn crossing several blocks.
- **"Public Square"** `[legible — "Public" clear, "Squa…" partial]`, written across a large open,
  uncoloured, undivided space inside the close-lotted grid. Measured against the block module on
  the same sheet, the open ground spans **about two blocks by two blocks** (§2.6).
- **"SCHOOL"** `[legible]`, written across one of the larger blocks in the outlot band north-east
  of the dense grid, with the block number **9** beside it.
- **"Burying Ground"** `[probable]`, written across another block in the same north-east band,
  beside block number **4** or **3**.
- **"Well"** `[probable]`, with a dotted enclosure, marked in the outlot band well to the
  north-east of the town.
- **Block and lot numbering.** Every block in the dense grid carries a block number and is divided
  into **six lots in two rows of three**, numbered 1 2 3 along one side and 4 5 6 (written 6 5 4)
  along the other `[legible on dozens of blocks]`. This is the single most useful thing on the
  sheet after the grid itself.
- **The bay front.** The last tier of ground before the water is drawn **not** as lotted blocks but
  as **long narrow strips running down to the shore**, six of them clearly outlined in blue and
  yellow. These are water or landing lots (§5.6 — the plat does not label them).
- **The outlot band.** North-east and east of the dense grid, larger blocks (about two town-block
  modules across) carry proprietors' initials and their own number series running at least to 23.
  Beyond them, narrow strips labelled **"Shell"**, **"Salt…"** and **"Bank Marsh"** `[probable]`
  run out toward the flats.
- **The town league**, beyond all of that, is divided into **eight numbered strips (1 to 8)** drawn
  across the sheet, each with dimensions written on it in varas. Two lengths read cleanly at full
  resolution — **3974** and **3936** `[legible]` — and one width reads **718** `[legible]`, with
  **918**, **1930** and a figure near **555** also present `[probable]`.
- **No north arrow, no scale bar and no title cartouche were found on the photograph.** The
  certification occupies the lower-right quarter of the sheet.

### 2.4 The grid on the ground, measured

Every named street centreline in Matagorda was pulled from OpenStreetMap (S11) and projected into
a local plane. Results:

- **Bearing.** The streets fall into two families and nothing else. One runs at a median
  **N 60.5° E**; the other at **150.5°**, i.e. **S 29.5° E** — exactly perpendicular. Fourteen
  named streets sit within a degree of one or the other. **Matagorda's grid bearing is not
  unknown; it is 60.5° east of north.**
  Three streets are visibly skewed and should be treated as later: **Fisher Street (54.2°)**, which
  is State Highway 60 and has presumably been realigned, **Matagorda Street (54.4°)** and **Ingram
  Street (50.6°)**.
- **Module.** Consecutive parallel street centrelines in the un-skewed families are **about 415 ft
  apart**, in both directions, with individual spacings from 375 to 480 ft and several gaps of two
  modules (833 and 861 ft). A least-squares fit to the better-behaved family gives **127.3 m
  (417.6 ft) with an r.m.s. residual of 2.5 m**. At 0.8467 m to the vara that is **150.3 varas**.
- **The plat agrees.** On the plat photograph the block-plus-street module measures **44.2 pixels**
  (found by rotating the image and taking the strongest periodicity in the 20–90 px band; the same
  rotation gives exactly half that, 22.1 px, in the cross direction, which is the mid-block lot
  line). The plat's own scale, from the **718-vara** strip width measured at **209 px**, is
  **3.44 varas per pixel**. 44.2 × 3.44 = **152 varas**.
  **Two independent routes — a surveyor's stated dimension on an 1838 manuscript, and the modern
  street grid measured off OSM — give 152 and 150 varas for the same module.** That is a
  1 % agreement, and it is the strongest single result in this research: **the streets of
  Matagorda today stand where Wightman put them.**
- **The extent.** The close-lotted grid on the plat covers an envelope of roughly **19 modules by
  18 modules** — about **1.5 by 1.45 miles** — though it is not filled to a rectangle: the river cuts
  its north-west side and the bay its south-east. **The surviving named streets span 19 modules in
  the N 60.5° E direction** (Orleans Street to Maynard Street) **and only about 9½ in the other**.
  So the grid's long axis survives at its full platted width, and the bay-ward end of it does not.
- **Where the burying ground is, checked.** The plat puts the Burying Ground in the outlot band
  north-east of the dense grid. The modern Matagorda Cemetery, founded "about 1829"
  (S5-Matagorda Cemetery), stands **about 4,900 ft east-north-east of the Cypress-and-Lewis
  corner, measured along the grid's own axis** — outside the close-lotted grid, in exactly that
  band. Documented and measured agree.

### 2.5 Block, lot and street dimensions

**No source read states a block, lot or street dimension for Matagorda.** What follows is
measurement off the plat photograph, checked against the module, and it must be labelled as such.

**How the block/street split was measured.** At a rotation that squares the grid, the folded
intensity profile across the plat shows a bright band — blank paper, no wash — about six to seven
pixels wide once per module, against a 44.2-pixel module: **20 to 24 varas of street**. Measured
a second way, on a zoomed crop of four adjacent blocks, the drawn block width is about 37 px and
the gap about 8 px: **125 varas of block and 27 of street**. Both put the street between 20 and
30 varas and the block between 120 and 130.

**The tidy reading, marked as inference (§6, I-2):** a **120-vara block, a 30-vara street and lots
of 40 × 60 varas**, six to a block, summing to exactly 150. It is clean, it is inside the
measurement, and **nobody states it.** The alternative that fits equally well is a 130-vara block
with a 20-vara street and lots of 43⅓ × 65. Do not print either as a fact.

For comparison across this series: **Mina's streets were 20 varas by the surveyor's own
instruction; San Felipe's Calle del Colegio was probably 25; Victoria's were 21.43 on a c. 1850
plat.** A 25-to-30-vara street at Matagorda would make it the widest-streeted of the four, which
is what one would expect of a speculative port laid out flat on open prairie with no ford, no
bluff and no existing settlement to work around.

### 2.6 The public ground

Three pieces of public ground are drawn and named on the plat, and that is three more than San
Felipe's photostat yielded.

1. **The Public Square.** One large open space inside the close-lotted grid, uncoloured, with no
   block outline and no internal street drawn across it, labelled *Public Square*. Measured against
   the block module on the same sheet it spans **about two blocks by two blocks** — roughly **300
   varas, 833 ft, on a side** including the street that would otherwise have run through it.
   `[The label is legible; the extent is a measurement off a low-resolution photograph and the
   edges of the wash are soft. Treat the two-by-two reading as probable, not certain.]`
   **No source read names this square, gives its dimension, or says what stood on it.**
2. **The School block**, numbered 9, in the outlot band north-east of the grid — a block about two
   town-block modules across, i.e. roughly 300 varas square. A **log schoolhouse with a dirt floor**
   and a house for the teacher **Josiah P. Wilbarger** are among the town's first buildings (S9);
   nothing read connects that schoolhouse to this block.
3. **The Burying Ground**, a block in the same north-east band, confirmed on the ground by the
   modern cemetery's position (§2.4). Matagorda Cemetery is "one of earliest cemeteries in Texas",
   founded about 1829 (S5-Matagorda Cemetery, quoted). **Seth Ingram, Ira Ingram, R. R. Royall,
   G. M. Collinsworth and S. Rhoads Fisher — five of the men who ran the town in 1835 — are buried
   there**, and their 1936 markers are what fixes the ground.

There is **no church block, no market square, no plaza de armas and no government square** on the
plat or in any source read. Matagorda was not laid out under the Laws of the Indies the way
Victoria was; it was laid out by an American surveyor for an American land company on a Mexican
permission, and its one public square is called, in English, a public square.

## 3. Documented — the water

This is the section that has no counterpart in the other three reports. San Felipe has a ferry,
Victoria a ford, Mina a gravel crossing. **Matagorda has a sea.**

### 3.1 The bar, the pass and where a ship could get to

Two 1836 books describe the approach, and they agree closely.

- **Pass Cavallo**, at the south-west end of Matagorda Peninsula, is **the only entry to Matagorda
  Bay from the Gulf** (S15). Holley gives it "twelve feet water over the bar, and a safe anchorage
  within" (S8, quoted) with four fathoms inside. Edward gives the same bar **ten to twelve feet**,
  with "a commodious harbor within it" and safe anchorage in three to four fathoms, **opposite the
  oyster peninsula of Matagorda** (S7).
- **The bay itself is shallow.** Holley: the average depth across it to the mouth of the Colorado
  "is not more than eight feet" (S8).
- **A ship could not reach the town.** Holley: vessels that can cross the bay "cannot approach
  nearer than one mile of the mouth of the river, and are compelled to unlade their cargoes by
  means of lighters" (S8). Edward and S3 put it as **six miles from the town**. **Every cargo that
  reached Matagorda in 1835 was lightered the last few miles.**
- **A pilot worked the pass.** The Matagorda committee's letter of 30 September 1835 mentions a
  boat landing three men "at the Pilot's house" at Pass Cavallo (S6).
- **The sea is visible from the town.** The same letter reports a schooner that "passed down the
  coast, in plain view of Town" a few days before (S6, quoted), and citizens going down to the
  Pass to help her in. **From the streets of Matagorda you could see a sail.**

Pass Cavallo lies about **25 miles south-west of the town across the bay**. Nothing read gives the
sailing time.

### 3.2 The Colorado, and the raft that made the town a port

- The Colorado runs some 600 miles and "disembogues itself into the bay of Matagorda" (S8). The
  town stands on its **east (or, as Edward puts it, south-east) bank at the mouth**.
- **The raft.** Holley, 1836: the river "would be navigable almost to the mountains, were it not
  obstructed by a raft of drift wood ten miles above Matagorda"; it "fills the bed so as to cause
  a dispersion of the stream into several channels" (S8, one quotation). First noted by Alonso de
  León in 1690; head of the obstruction twelve miles above the town; over forty miles long at its
  peak; goods were carried round it by wagon from the head of the raft into town (S13).
  **Ben Milam had undertaken to clear the Colorado in exchange for a steam-navigation monopoly**,
  and was dead at Béxar by December 1835 (S8).
- **What this means for the town.** The raft is why Matagorda existed and why it never became what
  its proprietors intended. It made the town the compulsory transhipment point for everything
  going up or down the Colorado — Holley calls Matagorda "a place of great business, trading with
  the interior and New Orleans" (S8) — and at the same time it silted the mouth and made a real
  port impossible (S3).

### 3.3 The ground — and why there is no bluff to draw

USGS 3DEP ground elevations (S12), sampled across the town:

| Point | Elevation |
| --- | --- |
| Cypress × Lewis (the church-marker corner) | 9.1 ft |
| Fisher × Cypress (the Methodist corner) | 8.6 ft |
| Laurel × Fisher (the old post office corner) | 9.1 ft |
| Town centre | 8.6 ft |
| Matagorda Cemetery | 10.1 ft |
| River bank west of the town | 6.5 ft |
| Ground toward the bay, south of the town | 6.9 ft |

Wikipedia gives the town's elevation as **7 ft** (S13). **Matagorda stands between eight and ten
feet above the sea, and the whole town is within two or three feet of the same level.**

This matters, because the sources use the word *bluff* three times and it is not what the word
means at San Felipe. The 1826 petitioners described "a large amphitheatre, a semicircular bluff"
(S9). Edward says the town is "laid out on a prairie bluff, which makes up to the northeast side
of Matagorda Bay and the southeast side of Colorado river" (S7, one quotation). S9 has Jesse
Burnham's first log cabin "on the bluff". **Read against the measurement, the bluff is a
two-to-three-foot prairie rise above marsh and bay flat, in a semicircle around the meeting of the
river and the bay.** It is a real feature — it is why the town is there rather than a mile away —
and it is not a landform anyone would draw as a bluff.

Edward's other judgement on the site should be kept with it: the town, he says, is by far the most
healthy, airy and agreeable spot on the seaboard of the province (S7). Matagorda was a summer
resort for Colorado and Caney planters by the 1840s (S1). **The thing this town had that Brazoria
and Columbia did not was sea wind.**

### 3.4 The country around it, and where the timber was not

- Matagorda is **coastal prairie**. Edward: a large extent of surrounding country, perfectly level
  and moderately rich, its soil lighter and easier to cultivate than Columbia's, and "not quite of
  such a woody and luxuriant nature" (S7).
- `HIST-TEX-016` already carries Holley's regional picture and it applies directly here: near the
  coast the bottom timber was mostly **live oak "of enormous size"**, the live oak region reaching
  **from Matagorda Bay to Galveston Bay**; between the rivers lay open prairie with points and
  islands of timber; cottonwood on the river banks, the least valuable timber but easy to cut.
- **Caney Creek**, "Old Caney", east of the town, carried the plantations and most of the county's
  timber and traffic. The Matagorda committee sent men "to Caney, and up B. Prairie" to raise
  volunteers in September 1835 (S6).
- **There are no pines.** Mina had the Lost Pines at its door; Matagorda had live oak on the
  bottoms, cottonwood on the banks, and prairie.

### 3.5 The trap: the water that survives is not the 1835 water

**Read this before drawing anything.** The Mina researcher's warning was that modern Bastrop is
the town the 1836 flood made. Matagorda's version is worse, because the town did not move:

| Date | What happened | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| to 1925 | The raft blocks the Colorado ten to twelve miles above the town and impounds its silt; the river disperses into several channels at the mouth | S8, S13 |
| 1925–29 | The raft is dynamited and a pilot channel cut through it | S13 |
| 1929 | A flood sweeps the remains of the raft **and the sediment it had held back** into Matagorda Bay | S13 |
| 1930 | The new delta covers **1,780 acres** | S13 |
| 1936 | **The delta has grown clear across the bay to Matagorda Peninsula**; a channel is cut through the peninsula and the river discharges straight into the Gulf | S13 |
| 1992 | The river is diverted back into the bay | S13 |

Measured on the modern map (S11), the town centre is now about **2,850 ft from the Colorado's
channel** and about **1.4 miles from open water in the direction the plat calls "Bay"**. **In 1835
the bay was at the foot of the town's south-east streets, where the plat draws the narrow water
lots.** The intervening ground is younger than 1930.

The same caution applies in the other direction. The Colorado's channel past the town runs almost
due north–south today; it has been dredged, diverted and re-diverted; and the 1838 plat draws it
dispersing and bending south-west below the town. **The polyline in §7.4 is the modern channel and
it is the wrong century.**

## 4. Documented and strongly supported — the buildings

### 4.1 What the town was built of — imported frame houses, duty-free

This is the best fact in the report for anyone drawing the place, and it is documented from a
contemporary printed source quoting a law.

**Article 13 of the Mexican law of 6 April 1830** provided that, for four years, **the introduction
of frame houses, lumber and every kind of provisions should be admitted into the ports of
Galveston and Matagorda free of duty** (S7). Edward prints it in his summary of the land and
commerce laws, alongside Article 12's four-year free coasting trade.

Three things follow, and together they make Matagorda look unlike every other town in this series:

1. **The buildings came in by sea.** Not "were built of imported lumber" — **frame houses** are
   named in the statute as an import, and this is the port the statute names. A frame house shipped
   in frame from New Orleans or Mobile and raised on a Texas lot is a documented category of thing
   at exactly this place in exactly this decade.
2. **The exemption ran out in 1834**, four years from April 1830. The Revolution began eighteen
   months later. **The town standing in October 1835 was built largely during the exemption.**
3. **San Felipe's research already asked for a frame building** — Cooper & Chieves' saloon was "the
   only frame building in the place" in 1828, and lot 16's store was frame with a brick foundation
   and cedar shingles. **At Matagorda the frame building is not the exception. It is the town.**
   The art request San Felipe raised is the same one, and it is now load-bearing for two settlements
   (§7.7).

What the log record says, against that:

- The **first** buildings, 1828–29, were log: **James Cummins' double log cabin near the river** and
  **Jesse Burnham's log cabin on the bluff** were the only houses on the townsite when the colonists
  landed (S9). The **schoolhouse was of logs with a dirt floor** (S9).
- The **stockade** (§4.2) was of set timber.
- The **old Matagorda post office**, built before 1872 on a lot bought in 1837, is "constructed of
  hand-cut lumber and held together with wooden pegs" (S5-Old Post Office, quoted). That is
  thirty-odd years late but it is the one surviving building in the town whose fabric is described,
  and it is **sawn lumber, pegged — not logs.**
- When the Methodists rebuilt in 1893 they brought **long leaf yellow pine and cypress in from
  Galveston** (S5-Methodist). The habit of importing the timber outlasted the century.

**No source read describes a brick building, an adobe building or a stone building in Matagorda
before 1836**, and on this ground there is no stone to describe.

### 4.2 The first buildings, and the stockade

From *Historic Matagorda County* through S9, for the founding winter of 1828–29:

- Sixty passengers landed from the schooner **Little Zoe** on **27 January 1829**, after a voyage
  of thirty-one days instead of the expected seven.
- The only houses on the townsite were **Cummins' double log cabin, going up near the river**, and
  **Burnham's log cabin on the bluff**.
- **A small stockade, fifty feet square and ten feet tall**, sheltered the new arrivals while they
  built. S9 notes it had **mosquito netting**.
- A **log schoolhouse with a dirt floor** and a house for the teacher **Josiah P. Wilbarger**
  followed.

**The stockade is the one 1829 structure with a stated dimension anywhere in this research, for any
of the four towns.** Fifty feet square and ten feet tall, for sixty people plus the two families
already there.

**But it is 1829, not 1835, and nothing read says it still stood.** Mina's stockade is documented
standing through the 1830s and has a block; Matagorda's is a landing-party shelter of the first
winter with no location and no end date. Do not draw it standing in October 1835 without saying so.

Two **salt factories** were among "several large business enterprises" in the town by 1832 (S9) —
a coastal industry with no inland equivalent, and the plat's outlot strips labelled *Salt*,
*Shell* and *Bank Marsh* are presumably where they worked.

### 4.4 Who governed, and who the town's men were

- **Ira Ingram was first alcalde of the Matagorda municipality in 1834**, and later first Speaker of
  the House of the Republic; the marker calls him a "Pioneer Patron of Public Schools"
  (S5-Ira Ingram). He died 22 September 1837.
- **The Committee of Safety, Vigilance, Election and Correspondence** convened on the morning of
  **25 September 1835** with **W. J. Russell** in the chair and **Seth Ingram** as secretary,
  appointed a committee of five — **Ira Ingram, R. R. Royall, G. M. Collinsworth, Samuel Williams
  and Charles Willson** — to report on Austin's circular of the 19th, approved it, recommended the
  immediate formation of a militia company, and reconstituted itself as Russell, Royall, Ira
  Ingram, Seth Ingram and **Hamilton S. Cook** (S6).
- By **30 September** **R. R. Royall** was chairman and **Ira Ingram** secretary (S6). Royall went
  on to be temporary chairman of the Consultation of 1835 and a member of the General Council
  (S5-Royall).
- **G. M. Collinsworth commanded the Texans who captured Goliad on 9 October 1835**
  (S5-Collinsworth) — that is, a Matagorda man led the action at La Bahía two weeks after his own
  town's committee sat.
- **S. Rhoads Fisher**, who certified the plat, set up a mercantile house and shipping business in
  Matagorda in 1830, signed the Declaration at Washington-on-the-Brazos, and became Secretary of
  the Texas Navy in October 1836 (S5-Fisher).
- **Ira R. Lewis**, another plat signatory, was a Matagorda lawyer, a member of the Consultation
  and of the General Council; Edward Gritten wrote to him at Matagorda from Béxar on 1 October
  1835 (S6).
- **B. Hardiman** represented Matagorda at the Convention of 1836 (S8's appendix).

**No source read names a building any of these men met in, lived in or kept shop in.**

| 1832 | **250** in the town and **1,400** more in its jurisdiction | town and jurisdiction | **S1 (TSHA)** |
| 1832 / 1835 | **1,400** in the town and **250** more in its jurisdiction | town and jurisdiction — **reversed** | **S4 (dealer), S10 (chamber)** |

**The reversal is the thing to notice.** TSHA reads Almonte's 1,400 as the jurisdiction and puts
250 in the town; the map dealer and the chamber of commerce read the same figures the other way
and put 1,400 in the town. `HIST-TEX-011` already resolves this the TSHA way — Almonte counted
**municipalities, not towns** — and that resolution should stand, because it is the one that makes
the rest of the record consistent: a town of 1,400 could not credibly write that its numbers were
"too few, to enable us to spare many", and Holley, writing in 1836, gives the town **five hundred**.

**Five hundred is the best town figure for the period**, from a printed contemporary source, and it
happens to be the same figure San Felipe's research settles on. **No source read gives a number of
houses or buildings in Matagorda at any date.**

The 1936 marker's "third largest town in Texas in 1834" cannot be checked against Almonte's
municipality figures, where 1,400 is fifth or sixth. Record it; do not use it.

### 4.6 What happened in 1835–36 — and why the fabric did not survive

- **September–October 1835.** The committee sent two men "to Caney, and up B. Prairie" to raise a
  volunteer corps, and **recommended the volunteers of the town and of the eastern part of the
  jurisdiction to rendezvous at James Kerr's on the Lavaca as speedily as possible** (S6). This is
  `HIST-TEX-014` and `docs/COLONIES.md` §5.4a, and reading the original confirms both: the town
  also reported itself "badly armed", held 440 kegs of public cannon powder subject to Austin's
  order, asked that regard be had to "the weak, and uncovered condition of this point", and — the
  part the game already carries — resolved on precautions against a slave rising after word from
  the Brazos. The election of delegates to the Consultation was set for **5 October** in the town.
- **April 1836.** Urrea's column reached the coast; Matagorda was taken about **13 April 1836**
  during the Goliad campaign, and the Mexican army held the coast from San Patricio to Brazoria
  until the retreat after San Jacinto (S15). The town was deserted in the Runaway Scrape and
  reoccupied afterwards (S4).
- **11 September 1854.** The hurricane **"leveled almost every building in town"** (S5-Methodist,
  quoted); every vessel and wharf on Matagorda Bay was destroyed (S14); Christ Church's 1841
  building was destroyed and the town's houses unroofed.
- **1875, 1886, 1894.** Three more hurricanes; the county seat moved to Bay City in 1894 (S1).
- **1942 and 1961.** Another hurricane and flood; a levee built in the 1940s; Carla in 1961 (S9).

**Answering the brief's question directly: is the Matagorda that survives the town of 1835?**

**Its plan is. Its fabric is not, and its water is not.**
The grid stands where Wightman laid it — that is measured, twice, in §2.4. But the 1854 hurricane
levelled almost every building, three more hurricanes followed, and the oldest fabric in the town
is a store built before 1872 on a lot sold in 1837. **Nothing standing in Matagorda today was
standing in 1835.** And the bay that the town was built to face is now the Colorado's delta
(§3.5). This is the opposite of the Mina trap and it needs the same warning in the opposite
direction: **at Bastrop, trust the documents and distrust the modern street; at Matagorda, trust
the modern street and distrust everything else you can see.**

## 5. Unknown — what a layout would need and no source read supplies

Everything in this section would have to be invented and registered as fiction.

1. **The original 1828–29 survey.** The 1838 sheet certifies itself as the original's true
   representation; the original itself, and Wightman's field notes, were not located (§1b).
2. **Whether the plat carries street names, and what they were in 1835.** No street name is
   readable on the photograph, and the streets appear to be drawn blank. **The thirteen or so
   surviving names (§7.2) are modern names on ancient lines**, and four of them — Wightman,
   Fisher, Ingram, Lewis — honour men who signed the 1838 partition, which makes 1838 their
   earliest plausible date, not 1829.
3. **The block and lot dimensions.** §2.5 is measurement off a photograph, not a stated figure.
   Nobody says how wide a Matagorda street is.
4. **The number of blocks and lots.** Counted approximately off the plat as an envelope of about
   19 × 18 modules, not filled; the block numbering runs at least into the twenties in the outlot
   series and the town blocks are numbered separately. **No count of town lots exists in anything
   read** — the figure that Victoria has (59 titles) and San Felipe has (582 lots) is missing here.
5. **The size and shape of the Public Square, and what stood on it.** Read as about two blocks by
   two; nobody states it; and no source read says what the square was for or whether anything was
   ever built on it.
6. **What the bay-front strips were.** The plat draws six long narrow lots running down to the
   water where the blocks stop. They are unlabelled on the photograph. **Whether they are wharf
   lots, warehouse lots, a public landing or simply undivided ground is unknown**, and so is
   whether anything stood on them in 1835.
7. **Where the customhouse stood.** Documented 1831–1836 (§4.3). **No location, in anything read.**
   For a port town this is the single most valuable missing position.
8. **Where the landing was.** That vessels lightered their cargo in is documented (§3.1). **Where
   the lighters put it ashore is not.**
9. **Where the Committee Room was**, where any store stood, where the schoolhouse stood, and where
   the two salt factories worked.
10. **The 1835 shoreline.** The plat draws the bay against the grid's south-east edge; the modern
    shore is a mile and a half further out and ninety years old (§3.5). **The line the 1835 water
    actually took is not recoverable from anything read.**
11. **Whether the 1829 stockade still stood in 1835** (§4.2), and where it was.
12. **Any dimension of any building except the stockade.** No length, no width, no height, no roof
    pitch, no storey count. **Every footprint would be invented.**
13. **The number of buildings or houses in 1835.** Nothing. §4.5 is the whole of it.
14. **What a frame house imported into Matagorda actually looked like** — clapboard or flush
    siding, shingle or board roof, raised on piers or on the ground, one storey or two, galleried
    or not. The statute names the category; nothing read describes an example.
15. **Any archaeology.** Nothing read describes a single excavated feature in the townsite (§1b).
16. **Whether the town was fenced, walled or open** beyond the 1829 stockade.

## 6. Inferred — things that follow but that nobody states

Each is an inference of this document and must be labelled as one if it is used.

- **I-1. The modern street grid is Wightman's grid.** The plat's module (152 varas, from its own
  stated 718-vara dimension) and the surviving streets' module (150 varas, from OSM) agree to about
  1 %; the plat's grid stands in the same relation to the river, the bay and the burying ground as
  the modern streets do; and four surviving street names are the plat's own signatories.
  **Nobody says the grid was never re-laid.** This is the load-bearing inference of the document:
  if it is wrong, §7.2's positions are wrong, though the plat's geometry is not.
- **I-2. A block is 120 varas square, the streets are 30 varas wide, and a lot is 40 × 60 varas.**
  The measured module is 150 and the measured split is 120–130 against 20–30; 120/30/40×60 is the
  clean arithmetic inside that range. **Nobody states any of the three figures.**
- **I-3. The Public Square is four blocks, not one.** Read off the photograph against the block
  module on the same sheet. The label is documented; the extent is measurement.
- **I-4. Most of the platted town was empty in 1835.** An envelope of roughly 19 × 18 modules of
  six-lot blocks against a town Holley put at five hundred people. Nobody counts unbuilt lots; the
  arithmetic is this document's. **A Matagorda drawn as a filled grid would be wrong**, exactly as
  at San Felipe and Victoria.
- **I-5. The built town in 1835 clustered at the water end of the grid, toward the bay and the
  river junction, not around the Public Square.** The reasoning: the town existed to tranship
  cargo; the stores held the public powder and "a large amount of goods"; the plat's water lots
  are drawn at the bay end; the first two cabins were "near the river" and "on the bluff"; and the
  two churches, the post-office lot, the Masonic lodge and every marker in the town cluster in the
  same few blocks near the river-and-bay corner today. **Nobody says it.**
- **I-6. The town's buildings in 1835 were predominantly frame, of imported sawn lumber, on a
  prairie with no local building timber.** The statute (S7) documents the import; the prairie is
  documented; the logs are documented only for 1828–29. The step from "frame houses entered
  duty-free" to "most of the town was frame" is this document's.
- **I-7. The "bluff" is a two-to-three-foot prairie rise, not a landform.** Three sources use the
  word; the DEM says the whole town is 6.5 to 10 ft (§3.3). **No source read qualifies the word.**
- **I-8. The 1835 bay shore ran close to the south-east edge of the platted grid**, roughly along
  the line of the modern Matagorda Street / Matagorda Avenue. Follows from the plat drawing the
  water against the last tier of lots, and from the post-1929 delta being younger than that
  (§3.5). **The line is not recoverable and must be drawn as invented.**
- **I-9. The road to San Felipe left the town on the inland side of the grid.** Holley's mail route
  No. 11 runs San Felipe to Matagorda, 90 miles, weekly, by Phillips' and Cook's Island, three days
  each way (S8); the game's own `FIC-GONZ-027` already routes Matagorda–Victoria and the Colorado
  crossing. **No 1835 course is documented.**

   Gonzales and is the fact that makes this town's economy look different.

### 7.6 What must be drawn *around* the town

- **The town league**, divided into eight numbered strips, with the outlot blocks, the School block,
  the Burying Ground and a marked **Well** in the band between town and league (§2.3). The league
  runs inland to the north-east.
- **Coastal prairie**, level and open, with **live oak** on the bottoms and **cottonwood** on the
  river bank (§3.4, `HIST-TEX-016`). **No pines. No cedar brake. No cane-covered hills.**
- **Salt marsh, shell bank and salt flat** on the bay side, which the plat labels (§2.3).
- **Caney Creek and the plantations east of the town** — the country the committee sent to for
  volunteers (§3.4, S6). A Matagorda drawn as a town alone misses that it was the outlet for the
  Caney and lower Colorado plantations, and that means it was the outlet for cotton grown by
  enslaved people. `HISTORY.md`'s representation limits apply to anything drawn from that.
- **The raft**, ten to twelve miles up the Colorado, if the map reaches it — and the wagon road
  from its head down to the town (§3.2), which is how up-country goods arrived.
- **Pass Cavallo and the Pilot's house**, twenty-five miles across the bay to the south-west, if
  the map reaches that far (§3.1).

**Already covered — do not raise a request for these:**

- **`wharf`** exists. This is the single luckiest thing about drawing Matagorda and it is the only
  town in the four that needs it.
- **`skiff`** and **`ferry-raft`** exist, and between them will stand in for a lighter.
- **`storehouse`**, **`trading-house`**, **`frame-hall`**, **`timber-hall`** and **`timber-shop`**

- **`live-oak-large` / `-log` / `-pole`** and **`cottonwood`** exist — exactly the two trees the

  set logs they will serve better than they serve Mina's town-sized stockade.

**Genuinely missing:**

- **A sea-going schooner.** The atlas has `skiff` and `ferry-raft` and nothing with a mast. **A
  two-masted coasting schooner, at anchor in the offing and under sail passing the town**, is the
  thing that makes this place read as a port and nothing in the library can stand in for it. This
  is the highest-value art request in the report.
- **A frame (clapboard) house.** **San Felipe's research already raised this**, for a building that
  was the exception there. At Matagorda it is the rule (§4.1), and the request should be re-priced
  accordingly: **two or three frame silhouettes, sided and shingled, distinct from a log wall** —
  one dwelling, one store with a front, one larger public building. Stand-in: `trading-house` and
  `frame-hall`, marked `stand-in:` naming the request.
- **A cargo lighter.** A flat, open, shallow boat under oar or sail carrying barrels and bales from
  a ship's side to a landing. `skiff` is a rowing boat, not a lighter. Stand-in: `ferry-raft` loaded
  with `barrel` and `sacks`, marked `stand-in:`.
- **A warehouse standing on a wharf**, rather than a warehouse and a wharf side by side. Probably a
  composition note on the existing `wharf` and `storehouse` frames rather than a new sprite —

- **Open bay water and a horizon.** Every water surface in the atlas belongs to a river. **A bay

  the game has not needed it before. Raise it as a ground/terrain request, not a sprite.

- **Salt-works pans.** Two salt factories are documented by 1832 (§4.2). Stand-in: `shed-open` with

## 8. Verdict

**Can Matagorda be drawn honestly? Yes — and its plan is the best-founded of the four.**

This is the only one of the four towns whose **plat survives and can be read**. The grid's bearing
is measured, its module is confirmed twice from independent evidence a century and a half apart,
its blocks are documented as six lots in two rows of three, and its Public Square, School block and
Burying Ground are named on the sheet, with the burying ground independently confirmed on the
ground. Nothing comparable was recoverable for San Felipe; Victoria's plat is missing and Mina's
destroyed. **A student can be walked down a street of Matagorda at its real angle, past a real
square, on blocks of the real size.**

**And the buildings are the thinnest of the four, by a wide margin.** San Felipe has Smithwick's
walk down the road and twenty named businesses on numbered lots. Victoria has four buildings at
four crossings. Mina has one store, one lot sale and a stockade with a block. **Matagorda has a
customhouse with no location, stores with no location, a Committee Room with no location, a
stockade from 1829 with a dimension and no location, and no church at all.** Every building drawn
would be invention placed on a real street.

**What this town has that the others do not is a character that is documented even though its
buildings are not.** It is flat, low, open and windy; its houses came in by ship as frame and
lumber under a Mexican statute that named this port; its streets are wide and square and mostly
empty; the sea is at the end of them and no ship can reach it; its cargo comes ashore in lighters
and its up-country goods come in by wagon round a forty-mile logjam; and in October 1835 its
committee wrote that its numbers were too few to spare many, held four hundred and forty kegs of
the revolution's powder in its stores, and sent its volunteers to gather at Kerr's on the Lavaca —
which is the call the game already carries.

**The four things most worth doing before drawing it**, in order of value:

1. **Get into the Portal to Texas History and read the *Matagorda Bulletin* (1837–39) and the
   *Colorado Gazette and Advertiser* (1839–43).** A newspaper printed in the town two years after
   the period, with its advertisements, is the likeliest surviving description of Matagorda's
   buildings and businesses, and it would probably give street names and locations that nothing
   else in this research could. **Three reports in a row have been stopped by this one site.**
2. **Find a better scan of the 1838 plat, or the original.** The sheet was sold; the county clerk
   deposited a copy in 1838 and **Matagorda County's clerk and museum held Wightman's field notes
   into the 1980s** (S2). A readable scan would settle the street names, the block and lot
   dimensions, the size of the Public Square and the use of the water lots — six of the sixteen
   unknowns in §5, from one document.
3. **Read *Historic Matagorda County*, Volumes I and II**, in full rather than through one
   transcribed chapter, and ask the **Matagorda County Museum** whether anything places the
   customhouse, the landing or a store of the 1830s.
4. **Establish the 1835 shoreline.** The 1838 plat draws it; a georeferenced overlay of that plat on
   the modern grid — which §2.4 shows is now possible, because the two grids are the same grid —
   would put the 1835 bay edge on the ground to within a block. That is the one measurement that
   would let this town be drawn facing the water it was built to face.
